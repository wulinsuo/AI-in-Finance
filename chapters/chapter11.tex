\chapter{Insurance and Actuarial Modeling}

\notocsection{Introduction}

In August 1992, Hurricane Andrew crossed southern Florida and destroyed, alongside tens of thousands of homes, the insurance industry's confidence in its own loss estimates. Insurers had priced Florida windstorm coverage from a few decades of loss experience that happened to contain no direct hit on a major metropolitan area; Andrew produced insured losses several times larger than the industry's models had contemplated, and roughly a dozen insurers were rendered insolvent by a single storm. The failure was not one of underwriting discipline but of method: historical claims experience, the industry's traditional basis for pricing, is structurally silent about events larger than any it happens to contain. Out of that failure grew the modern catastrophe-modeling industry, simulation-based and data-hungry, and the lesson this chapter develops: insurance is above all a business of estimating loss distributions, and every advance in estimating them becomes an advance in how insurance is priced, reserved, and supervised.

Insurance deserves its own chapter for two reasons. First, it is enormous, by assets, premium volume, and employment of quantitatively trained people, and its analytical core, actuarial science\index{actuarial science}, is the oldest data science in finance: actuaries were fitting frequency and severity distributions to loss data a century before ``machine learning'' existed. Second, its structure makes the AI questions crisp. An insurer prices a contract before knowing what it will cost, observes rich behavioral data about its customers, and adjudicates millions of claims a year, all inside one of the most consumer-protective regulatory perimeters in finance. Every theme of this book (predictive modeling, text and image data, fraud detection, explainability, fairness) arrives here at once.

The chapter's central claim is the asymmetry Chapter~\chnum{oprisk} found in operational risk. Machine learning does not replace the actuarial core; it feeds and sharpens it wherever the data is \emph{granular} or \emph{unstructured}: driver-level sensor streams instead of rating-cell averages, claim-level features instead of aggregate triangles, documents and images instead of questionnaires, and interactions no multiplicative rating plan anticipates. The classical structure (frequency times severity, credibility, the development triangle, the filed rating plan) remains the layer regulators audit and balance sheets are built on. Each section therefore gives the classical calculation first, then a hand-checkable worked example of the machine-learning layer beneath it.

Figure~\ref{fig:ins-value-chain} maps where AI enters. The chapter first covers what makes insurance different and how an insurer makes money; then the classical core (frequency-severity pricing, the one-way-rating failure that motivated generalized linear models, credibility, chain-ladder reserving); then where machine learning changes pricing, telematics, claims, reserving, and underwriting. It widens to the balance sheet (life and health insurance, asset-liability management, catastrophe risk, solvency capital) and closes with fairness, price optimization, and proxy discrimination.

\section{The Economics of Insurance}\index{insurance!economics of}

An insurer sells a promise and invests the proceeds, which makes its economics unlike any other financial firm's.

\subsection{What Makes Insurance Different}
\label{sec:ins-fundamentals}

Insurance inverts the production cycle of almost every other business: the price is collected first and the cost of goods sold, the claims, is discovered afterward, sometimes decades afterward. Everything distinctive about the industry follows from this inversion. Profitability cannot be read off current revenue; it must be \emph{estimated}, by forecasting the ultimate cost of claims already incurred (the reserving problem of Section~\ref{sec:ins-reserving}) and of contracts still in force. The product is a promise, so the industry is regulated primarily for solvency (an insurer must demonstrably be able to pay claims years away) and secondarily for fairness in pricing, since coverage is often practically or legally mandatory.

The economic engine is risk pooling\index{risk pooling}, and a single calculation explains why the industry exists. Suppose each of $n$ independent policies produces an annual loss with mean $\mu = \$720$ and standard deviation $\sigma = \$3{,}000$, a violently uncertain individual prospect, with a coefficient of variation of $3{,}000/720 \approx 417\%$. The pooled loss across $n = 10{,}000$ such policies has mean $n\mu = \$7.2$ million but standard deviation only $\sigma\sqrt{n} = \$300{,}000$, a coefficient of variation of $4.17\%$: aggregation has shrunk relative uncertainty by a factor of $\sqrt{n} = 100$. A loss more than $10\%$ above expectation is a $z = 2.4$ event, roughly a $0.8\%$ probability under a normal approximation. Individually unpredictable losses have become collectively predictable, and \emph{that predictability, not risk-bearing appetite, is the product}: the insurer sells the law of large numbers, retail.\index{law of large numbers}\index{risk pooling!law of large numbers} The calculation also shows its own failure mode (Section~\ref{sec:ins-cat}): it assumed independence, and a hurricane, correlating a hundred thousand claims into one event, deletes the $\sqrt{n}$.

Two classic frictions limit the engine. Adverse selection\index{adverse selection} is the tendency of higher-risk customers to seek more coverage, combated with underwriting and risk-based pricing; moral hazard\index{moral hazard} is the tendency of insured parties to take more risk once covered, combated with deductibles and coverage limits. Both are information problems, which is why the industry has always been an early adopter of technology that reduces information asymmetry\index{information asymmetry}, from the mortality table to the telematics device.

An insurance portfolio's annual loss is a compound frequency-severity sum, the loss distribution approach of Chapter~\chnum{oprisk}, since both fields face a random number of losses of random size. Historically, operational risk borrowed this machinery \emph{from} actuarial science.

\subsection{How an Insurer Makes Money: Float and the Combined Ratio}
\label{sec:ins-economics}\index{insurance!float}

An insurer's operating result has two engines, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ins-cycle}. The underwriting engine is summarized by the combined ratio\index{combined ratio}: losses plus expenses, divided by premiums. A combined ratio below $100\%$ means the insurance operation is profitable in its own right. The investment engine runs on float\index{float (insurance)}: because premiums arrive before claims are paid, the insurer continuously holds a large pool of policyholder money (the loss reserves of Section~\ref{sec:ins-reserving} plus unearned premiums), which it invests for its own account.

A worked example shows how the engines combine. An insurer writes \$100 million of premium, incurs \$65 million of losses (a $65\%$ loss ratio)\index{loss ratio} and \$30 million of expenses (a $30\%$ expense ratio)\index{expense ratio}, for a combined ratio of $95\%$ and an underwriting profit of \$5 million. It also holds \$150 million of float invested at a $4\%$ yield, earning \$6 million, so total pre-tax operating income is \$11 million, a $22\%$ return on its \$50 million of supporting capital. Now rerun the year with a $103\%$ combined ratio: underwriting loses \$3 million, yet the insurer still earns \$3 million overall, because the \$3 million underwriting loss is the \emph{cost} of holding \$6 million worth of investable float, cheaper than any other funding the insurer could raise. This arithmetic, made famous by Berkshire Hathaway's shareholder letters, explains why insurers will write at small underwriting losses in competitive markets, why reserving accuracy (which determines how much float there is, and for how long) is a first-order economic question, and why asset-liability management (Section~\ref{sec:ins-alm}) belongs in the same chapter as pricing.

It also locates where machine learning can move the profit needle, because the combined ratio has only two components. Better risk selection and pricing (Sections~\ref{sec:ins-ml-pricing}--\ref{sec:ins-telematics}) and earlier fraud detection (Section~\ref{sec:ins-claims}) lower the \emph{loss} ratio; automated claims handling and NLP-driven underwriting (Sections~\ref{sec:ins-claims} and~\ref{sec:ins-underwriting}) lower the \emph{expense} ratio. On the worked book each point of combined ratio is worth \$1 million of premium, pre-tax, every year. That is the business case for the industry's AI investment, and it sets this chapter's evaluation standard: an insurance model is judged by the ratio points it moves, not by its accuracy in the abstract.

\subsection{The Classical Pricing Baseline: Frequency Times Severity}
\label{sec:ins-pricing}\index{frequency-severity model!insurance pricing}\index{premium (insurance)}

Suppose an auto insurer must set next year's premium for a policyholder it has never observed have an accident. The classical answer prices the \emph{expected} loss and then loads it for expenses and profit. The pure premium\index{pure premium} is
\begin{equation}
\text{pure premium} \;=\; \mathbb{E}[N] \times \mathbb{E}[X],
\label{eq:insurance-pure-premium}
\end{equation}
expected claim frequency ($N$) times expected claim severity ($X$), and the art of ratemaking is estimating how each component varies with observable characteristics of the risk.

A worked example fixes the mechanics. Suppose the book-wide base rates are a claim frequency of $0.08$ claims per policy-year and a mean severity of \$9{,}000, giving a base pure premium of $0.08 \times 9{,}000 = \$720$. Rating factors enter multiplicatively\index{rating relativities}: suppose young drivers exhibit $1.8$ times the base frequency, urban territories $1.25$ times the base frequency, and urban claims run $1.10$ times the base severity (denser traffic produces more collisions; urban repair and injury costs run higher). A young urban driver's expected frequency is then $0.08 \times 1.8 \times 1.25 = 0.18$ and expected severity $9{,}000 \times 1.10 = \$9{,}900$, for a pure premium of $0.18 \times 9{,}900 = \$1{,}782$, about $2.5$ times the base risk. Loading for a $25\%$ expense ratio and a $5\%$ profit provision\index{premium (insurance)!risk loading}, the gross premium solves $\text{premium} \times (1 - 0.25 - 0.05) = \text{pure premium}$, giving $1{,}782 / 0.70 \approx \$2{,}545.71$.

This arithmetic is the skeleton; the instrument that estimates the relativities is the generalized linear model of the next section. A note on vocabulary before the machine-learning comparison arrives: when the insurance industry says ``traditional pricing,'' it means \emph{GLM-based} pricing. The GLM is not a stepping stone to modern methods; it has been the established standard for decades, and it is the incumbent that machine learning must beat.

\section{Pricing and Claims}

This part covers how a premium is set, from the generalized linear model through credibility to telematics, and what happens when a claim arrives.

\subsection{GLM Ratemaking: The Traditional Approach}
\label{sec:ins-glm}\index{ratemaking!GLM}\index{generalized linear model (GLM)!ratemaking}

Where do relativities like the $1.8$ and $1.25$ above come from? The tempting answer, computing each factor's relativity from its own marginal experience one factor at a time, contains a trap worth working through numerically, because escaping it is what made the generalized linear model the industry's standard.

Consider a portfolio with two rating factors, age (young/experienced) and territory (urban/rural), with exposures and claim counts by cell:

\begin{booktable}[htbp]{Exposures (policy-years), claim counts, and claim frequencies by rating cell}{tab:ins-oneway}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Cell & Exposure & Claims & Frequency \\
\midrule
Young, urban & 200 & 54 & 0.270 \\
Young, rural & 50 & 9 & 0.180 \\
Experienced, urban & 300 & 45 & 0.150 \\
Experienced, rural & 450 & 45 & 0.100 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{booktable}

The table was built with an exactly multiplicative structure: a base frequency of $0.100$, a young relativity of $1.8$, and an urban relativity of $1.5$ (check: $0.100 \times 1.8 \times 1.5 = 0.270$). Now compute the relativities the one-way approach recovers.\index{ratemaking!one-way rating bias} Young drivers overall: $(54+9)/(200+50) = 0.252$; experienced overall: $(45+45)/(300+450) = 0.120$; one-way age relativity $0.252/0.120 = 2.10$, not $1.8$. Urban overall: $(54+45)/(200+300) = 0.198$; rural: $(9+45)/(50+450) = 0.108$; one-way territory relativity $1.833$, not $1.5$. Both are inflated, and for the same reason: young drivers in this portfolio cluster in urban territory ($200$ of $250$ exposures), so the age marginal absorbs part of the territory effect and vice versa, double-counting their overlap. A rating plan built from the one-way factors would charge the young urban cell $2.10 \times 1.833 = 3.85$ times base, against a true relativity of $2.70$, a $43\%$ overcharge, and would symmetrically undercharge the experienced rural cell. The mispricings then feed adverse selection\index{adverse selection!in insurance} as overcharged customers defect to better-priced competitors.

The remedy is simultaneous estimation, which is what the generalized linear model\index{generalized linear model (GLM)} (GLM) does. A Poisson GLM with a log link fit to claim counts\index{generalized linear model (GLM)!Poisson regression} (with exposure as an offset), and a Gamma GLM fit to severities, estimate all relativities \emph{jointly}, each factor's effect measured holding the others fixed, recovering the true $1.8$ and $1.5$ from Table~\ref{tab:ins-oneway} exactly. GLMs extended the ordinary regression of Chapter~\chnum{ml} to the skewed, non-negative distributions of insurance data. Spreading from British motor insurance in the 1980s and 1990s, GLM ratemaking displaced the earlier one-way and minimum-bias methods to become the industry's standard classification-ratemaking approach worldwide, the established baseline in the Casualty Actuarial Society's own rating monograph. ``Traditional pricing'' in today's debates thus refers to a method that was, in its time, the data-science modernization. The multiplicative-relativity output is also \emph{filed}: in most U.S. states an insurer must submit its rating plan to a regulator, justify each factor, and use what it filed, a constraint with deep consequences for what follows.

\subsection{Credibility: The Actuary's Bayesian Shrinkage}
\label{sec:ins-credibility}

Between the book-wide rate and the individual risk sits a question the GLM alone does not answer: how much should one policyholder's \emph{own} experience move their price? A commercial fleet with three years of history and one claim exhibits an observed frequency of $1/3 \approx 0.333$ against a class rate of $0.100$. Charging either number is wrong, the first because three years of one fleet is noise, the second because it ignores real information. Credibility theory\index{credibility theory}, actuarial science's oldest continuously used tool, prescribes the blend
\begin{equation}
\hat f \;=\; Z \times \bar f_{\text{own}} + (1 - Z) \times f_{\text{class}}, \qquad Z = \frac{n}{n + k},
\label{eq:insurance-credibility}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the volume of the risk's own experience and $k$ measures how noisy individual experience is relative to how much risks truly differ (formally, the ratio of expected process variance to the variance of true means across risks, the B\"uhlmann\index{Buhlmann credibility}\index{credibility theory!Buhlmann credibility} formulation). With $n = 3$ years and $k = 12$, the credibility weight is $Z = 3/15 = 0.20$ and the blended frequency $0.20 \times 0.333 + 0.80 \times 0.100 \approx 0.147$: the fleet's bad year moves its rate meaningfully but not catastrophically, and each additional year of clean experience will pull it back.

The formula appears three times over in this book. It is Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s Bayesian updating\index{Bayesian updating!credibility as}, with the class rate as prior, own experience as likelihood, and $Z$ the posterior weight on the data. It is the shrinkage of ridge regularization\index{regularization!ridge}, pulling noisy individual estimates toward a grand mean by an amount that declines as evidence accumulates. And it is structurally the partial pooling that modern hierarchical models perform. Actuaries derived it in the 1910s--1960s because rate regulation forced them to price small risks fairly; machine learning rederived it as regularization half a century later. Credibility is this chapter's cleanest evidence that actuarial science is not a field awaiting rescue by data science but a parallel, older branch of the same subject.

\subsection{From GLMs to Machine Learning in Pricing}
\label{sec:ins-ml-pricing}\index{ratemaking!machine learning}

The machine-learning question in pricing is therefore sharply posed. The incumbent is not a naive method but the GLM, a decades-refined statistical model with a regulatory apparatus built around it, so ``machine learning versus traditional pricing'' means boosted trees versus the GLM. Everything Chapter~\chnum{ml} established about flexible models applies, with one structural twist. Gradient-boosted trees routinely beat a GLM's predictive accuracy\index{gradient boosting} on large personal-lines datasets, for the familiar reason: real loss processes contain interactions (age with vehicle power, territory with annual mileage) that a multiplicative model captures only if an actuary thinks to specify them, while a tree ensemble finds them automatically. It is the lesson of Section~\ref{sec:ins-glm}'s one-way trap, one level up.

\weheading{\subsection}{ins-interaction}{The Interaction a GLM Cannot Price}\index{generalized linear model (GLM)!interaction a GLM cannot price}
\label{sec:ins-interaction}

Table~\ref{tab:ins-oneway}'s portfolio provides numbers with one realistic change. Suppose inexperience is more dangerous in dense traffic, so the young-urban cell's true frequency is $0.32$ ($64$ claims on $200$ exposures) rather than the multiplicative $0.27$, while the other three cells keep their multiplicative values. No multiplicative model can fit these four cells: multiplicativity forces the young-urban frequency to equal base times young times urban, and the data now violate that. Refitting the Poisson GLM to the modified portfolio shows how the failure manifests, which is subtler than mispricing the interaction cell. The fitted parameters come back as a base frequency of $0.0977$ with relativities of $2.05$ (young) and $1.57$ (urban): the interaction has \emph{leaked into the marginals}, inflating both, because the young-urban cell dominates the exposure behind each. The fitted model then prices the young-urban cell at $0.315$ against a true $0.320$, only $1.6\%$ light, but overprices young-rural at $0.201$ against a true $0.180$, an $11.4\%$ overcharge on the cell least responsible for the signal, with the experienced cells absorbing smaller distortions. The mispricing lands on the \emph{other} cells, where nobody is looking.

A depth-two decision tree\index{decision tree}, Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s simplest nonlinear model, prices all four cells exactly: its first split on age and second on territory partition the portfolio into the four cells themselves, each predicted at its own observed frequency, and a gradient-boosted ensemble reaches the same fit within a few trees. This is the GLM-versus-machine-learning pricing debate in a $2 \times 2$ table: the flexible model wins by declining to impose a structure the data contradict. On the standard academic benchmark for this comparison, a French motor liability dataset of nearly seven hundred thousand policies\index{freMTPL2freq dataset} (this chapter's closing challenge exercise), the accuracy improvement is consistent and material, and the same result reproduces across insurers' internal books.

\subsection{Where Machine Learning Improves on the Traditional Approach, and Where It Does Not}
\label{sec:ins-beyond-glm}

The interaction is one instance of a general pattern. The improvement comes through four channels, each matching a specific limitation of the traditional toolkit rather than generic ``more power.'' First, \emph{interaction discovery at scale}: an actuary can hand-specify the young-urban term once told about it, but not the thousands of candidate interactions among dozens of rating variables that a boosted ensemble searches automatically. Second, \emph{nonlinear continuous effects without hand-chosen bins}: a GLM represents driver age through bands or polynomial terms chosen in advance, so the true age curve (falling steeply through the twenties, flat in mid-life, rising again past seventy) is captured only as well as those choices allow, while a tree ensemble learns its shape from data. Third, \emph{high-cardinality categorical variables}: vehicle model and postcode take thousands of levels that a GLM must manually group (or shrink level by level with Section~\ref{sec:ins-credibility}'s credibility machinery), while tree ensembles and learned embeddings handle them natively. Fourth, and most consequentially, \emph{features the GLM could never build}: the deep-learning methods of Chapters~\chnum{nlp} and~\chnum{altdata} construct features themselves, such as the braking signature in a telematics stream, the roof condition in an aerial image, or the hazard buried in a broker narrative. The largest practical gains have come less from swapping the GLM out of the filed plan than from feeding it these richer inputs. The same channels recur in reserving (Section~\ref{sec:ins-reserving}), lapse modeling (Section~\ref{sec:ins-alm}), and mortality: in each case the traditional method is not wrong but \emph{coarse}, summarizing at a level of aggregation chosen for an era of scarce data and hand computation.

The other column matters as much. Where the book of business is small, the traditional method's low variance and the credibility machinery win, Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s bias-variance logic\index{bias-variance trade-off}; where the rating plan is narrow and well understood, there are few interactions left to find; and at the regulatory interface, the GLM's auditability is the point. Mature practice is therefore not replacement but a loop: fit the boosted challenger, let it reveal which interactions and curve shapes the filed GLM is missing, and \emph{distill} those findings back into added GLM terms. The filed model then captures most of the challenger's lift while remaining a document a regulator can read.

The structural twist is that the filed-rating-plan constraint turns Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s explainability discussion into a binding production requirement\index{explainability}. A rate filing must state what the model charges and why; an adverse underwriting decision must often be explained to the applicant under the same family of laws as adverse-action notices; and several U.S. states now require insurers to demonstrate that models built on nontraditional data do not proxy for prohibited characteristics\index{proxy discrimination!insurance} (Section~\ref{sec:ins-fairness}). The industry pattern is therefore a hybrid. Machine learning is used freely where it does not directly set the filed rate (underwriting triage, retention and demand modeling, claims analytics, challenger benchmarking), while the filed rating plan evolves more slowly, with the GLM (or a constrained, explainable boosted model) as the regulatory interface. It is this book's accuracy-versus-accountability trade-off, institutionalized more explicitly than anywhere else in finance.

\subsection{Telematics and Usage-Based Insurance}
\label{sec:ins-telematics}

Traditional rating variables (age, territory, vehicle) are all \emph{proxies} for how a person drives. Telematics\index{telematics} replaces the proxy with the behavior itself: a device or phone app records mileage, speed, time of day, and the harsh-braking and cornering events that predict collisions, and usage-based insurance prices on that record.\index{usage-based insurance}\index{telematics!usage-based insurance} It is the cleanest example in this book of AI shrinking an information asymmetry\index{information asymmetry} at the individual level: the insurer no longer infers risk from group membership but observes each driver's behavior.

In the worked example,\index{telematics!feature-to-relativity model} the telematics layer is itself a GLM on behavioral features: a log-link frequency model whose fitted coefficient on harsh-braking events is $+0.163$ per event per hundred miles relative to the rating cell's average. Six months of data show the young urban driver braking harshly two fewer times per hundred miles than their cell's average, so the telematics multiplier is $e^{0.163 \times (-2)} = e^{-0.326} \approx 0.722$, converting the age-based relativity of $1.8$ into a personal one of $1.8 \times 0.722 \approx 1.30$. The pipeline runs raw sensor stream $\rightarrow$ engineered behavioral features (Chapter~\chnum{altdata}'s feature extraction applied to accelerometer data) $\rightarrow$ a GLM coefficient $\rightarrow$ a rating relativity a regulator can read. The revised expected frequency is $0.08 \times 1.30 \times 1.25 = 0.13$, the pure premium falls to $0.13 \times 9{,}900 = \$1{,}287$, and the gross premium to $1{,}287/0.70 \approx \$1{,}838.57$, a saving of about \$707, or $27.8\%$, earned by demonstrated behavior rather than demographic reclassification. Run in reverse, the same calculation explains the controversy: a driver whose data reads badly faces a surcharge no traditional variable would have imposed, and continuous location and motion records raise privacy questions regulators are still working through\index{data privacy}. Enrollment is also voluntary, and drivers who expect their data to read well are the ones who opt in, so the telematics book is not a random sample of the portfolio\index{selection bias}.

\subsection{Claims: Triage, Automation, and Fraud}
\label{sec:ins-claims}

Claims is where an insurer's promises are kept, and it is the most AI-saturated function in the industry. Three applications dominate. \emph{Severity triage} predicts\index{claims triage}, from the first notice of loss, which claims will become expensive (the injury claim that will develop into litigation, the water leak that will become a mold claim), so that experienced adjusters are assigned early where they change outcomes. The target is long-tailed and partially censored\index{censoring}: open claims' ultimate costs are not yet known, the censoring structure of Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s survival analysis.

A minimal triage model shows the mechanics. Suppose a logistic model scores the probability that a new auto claim ultimately exceeds\index{logistic regression}\index{claims triage} \$50{,}000, from three first-notice features: an attorney is already involved ($+1.6$ on the log-odds), an injury is reported ($+1.3$), and the claim was reported more than a week after the accident ($+0.8$), against an intercept of $-2.2$. Here $\sigma$ is Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s sigmoid,\index{sigmoid function} $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$, not the standard deviation of the pooling calculation earlier; both meanings are standard, and a $\sigma$ applied to an argument is always the sigmoid. A claim arriving with attorney and injury flags but promptly reported scores $\sigma(-2.2 + 1.6 + 1.3) = \sigma(0.7) \approx 0.668$; a promptly-adjusted no-injury claim reported late scores $\sigma(-2.2 + 0.8) = \sigma(-1.4) \approx 0.198$. The first is routed to a senior adjuster on day one, the second to straight-through processing, and the model's value is the outcome difference early senior attention makes on the claims it flags. \emph{Straight-through processing} pays simple, well-documented claims automatically, using computer vision on damage photos to estimate repair costs and NLP on claim descriptions to verify coverage. Chapter~\chnum{oprisk}'s lesson applies: an automated adjudication pipeline is itself a system whose failures (overpayment at scale, systematic wrongful denial) are operational events. \emph{Claims fraud detection} is Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s machinery transplanted\index{insurance fraud}: rare positives, adversarial adaptation\index{adversarial adaptation}, network structure (staged-accident rings share repair shops, clinics, and phone numbers as laundering networks share addresses), and the same precision-recall economics, since every investigated honest claim delays a legitimate payment to a customer who has just suffered a loss.

\section{Reserving, Life, Health, and Assets}

Beyond pricing, an insurer must estimate what it already owes, and manage the assets held against it.

\subsection{Reserving: The Chain Ladder and Its Successors}
\label{sec:ins-reserving}\index{loss reserving!chain ladder}

An insurer's largest liability is typically not claims it knows about but claims it must \emph{estimate}: losses incurred but not yet reported or not yet fully paid\index{incurred but not reported (IBNR)}. Reserving estimates this liability, and the classical tool, the chain ladder\index{chain ladder}, is worth working once by hand: it is the industry's balance-sheet backbone and an elegant use of minimal data.

The chain ladder assumes each accident year develops proportionally the way its predecessors did. The 12-to-24-month development factor, estimated from all years observed at both ages, is $(600+675)/(400+450) = 1{,}275/850 = 1.50$; the 24-to-36-month factor is $660/600 = 1.10$. Year 2's ultimate loss is then projected as $675 \times 1.10 = 742.5$ and Year 3's as $500 \times 1.50 \times 1.10 = 825$, so the required reserve, ultimate losses minus amounts already paid, is $(742.5 - 675) + (825 - 500) = 67.5 + 325 = \$392.5$ thousand.

Machine learning enters reserving along two paths.\index{loss reserving!machine learning successors} Individual-claim reserving replaces the aggregated triangle with claim-level prediction, typically a gradient-boosted or neural regression of each open claim's remaining cost on the same first-notice features as the triage model above (injury type, attorney involvement, jurisdiction) plus its paid-to-date trajectory. It improves accuracy and, more importantly, explains \emph{why} reserves move, information a triangle cannot contain. Neural extensions of the chain ladder itself, which let the development factors vary with claim characteristics, are an active actuarial literature (see W\"uthrich and Merz in Further Reading). The chain ladder's own assumption, that development patterns are stable across accident years, breaks when claims inflation shifts or a new claims process changes settlement speed, so the drift monitoring of Chapter~\chnum{xai}\index{model monitoring!drift} applies to reserving models too. The stakes are high: reserves \emph{are} the float, so systematic under-reserving manufactures phantom profits today at the cost of real insolvency risk later. That is why reserve opinions are among the few statistical estimates in finance that a named, professionally liable individual must sign.

The \$392.5 thousand above is an undiscounted best estimate, the traditional basis for property-casualty reserves. Under IFRS 17\index{IFRS 17}, the international insurance-contracts standard effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2023, the same liability is measured as \emph{fulfilment cash flows}: expected future payments, spread over the years in which they will be paid and discounted at current rates, plus an explicit \emph{risk adjustment} for bearing uncertainty about the amount and timing of claims. On new business, a \emph{contractual service margin} holds back the expected profit and releases it as coverage is provided, while a loss-making group of contracts recognizes its loss at once \parencite{iasb2017ifrs17}. The chain ladder still supplies the cash-flow projection, but it now needs a payment pattern, a discount curve, and a view of the reserve's \emph{distribution}, since insurers must disclose the confidence level to which their risk adjustment corresponds. Stochastic reserving and the claim-level models above can answer that question; a point estimate cannot. In the United States, the FASB's long-duration targeted improvements (LDTI)\index{LDTI (long-duration targeted improvements)}, effective for most SEC-filing insurers for fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2022 and for other insurers two years later, move U.S. GAAP for life and annuity contracts in the same direction: assumptions must be reviewed at least annually, and the liability is discounted at a current upper-medium-grade (single-A) fixed-income yield updated every reporting date \parencite{fasb2018ldti}.

\subsection{Life Insurance, Mortality, and Longevity}
\label{sec:ins-life}\index{life insurance}\index{insurance!life}

Everything so far has been property-casualty insurance: short contracts, uncertain frequency and severity. In life insurance the event is certain, only its timing is not, and contracts run for decades, which moves the modeling weight to mortality curves and discounting, Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s machinery applied to human lifetimes. A one-year term policy pays \$500{,}000 if a policyholder with annual death probability $q = 0.002$ dies during the year. Its expected present value, the net premium, is\index{life insurance!net premium}\index{premium (insurance)!net premium}
\begin{equation*}
q \times B \times v \;=\; 0.002 \times 500{,}000 \times \frac{1}{1.04} \;\approx\; \$961.54,
\end{equation*}
mortality probability times benefit times a one-year discount factor at $4\%$. Every life product (term, whole life, annuities) is an expected-present-value sum of such terms across future years. Level-premium products overcharge early years to prefund the later ones, creating the enormous, long-duration liabilities that drive Section~\ref{sec:ins-alm}.

The modeling frontier is the mortality curve itself. Mortality improves over time, unevenly by age and cohort. The standard stochastic model, the Lee-Carter model\index{Lee-Carter model}\index{mortality modeling!Lee-Carter model}, decomposes the age-by-year mortality surface into age profiles and a single time-varying mortality index, a factor extraction in the spirit of Chapter~\chnum{timeseries}'s PCA term-structure example\index{principal component analysis (PCA)}, and forecasts that index as a random walk with drift\index{random walk}. The symmetric risk is longevity risk\index{longevity risk}: an annuity or pension book loses when people live \emph{longer} than projected, a slow-moving, undiversifiable trend risk transferred in practice through longevity swaps between pension funds and reinsurers. AI contributes on both fronts: mortality models enriched with cause-of-death, socioeconomic, and medical-advance information, and accelerated underwriting\index{underwriting!insurance}, which replaces the medical exam with predictive models on application data, prescription histories, and, controversially, wearable-device data. It moves a weeks-long process toward minutes for low-risk applicants while concentrating the fairness questions of Section~\ref{sec:ins-fairness} on whoever the model routes into full underwriting.

\subsection{Health Insurance and Risk Adjustment}
\label{sec:ins-health}\index{health insurance}\index{insurance!health}

Health insurance runs on different economics: contracts reprice annually, so the long-tail reserving problem largely disappears, and the dominant friction is selection\index{adverse selection!in insurance}, since individuals know far more about their expected medical costs than any application reveals. Modern health-insurance markets manage this with one of the largest predictive-modeling systems in the world with money attached: risk adjustment\index{risk adjustment}. Regulators pay (or transfer between) insurers according to a model-predicted cost score for each enrollee, so that an insurer attracting sicker members is compensated rather than bankrupted.

The risk score \emph{is} a predictive model: an additive regression of next-year cost on demographics and diagnosis indicators, whose fitted coefficients are published as score weights. In the U.S. Medicare Advantage program's HCC framework\index{hierarchical condition categories (HCC)}\index{risk adjustment!HCC score}, a 76-year-old female enrollee might carry a demographic base of $0.45$, plus $0.30$ for diabetes with complications, $0.33$ for heart failure, and $0.22$ for COPD: a risk score of $1.30$, which against a baseline payment of \$10{,}000 brings the insurer \$13{,}000. The incentive problem follows immediately, and it is Goodhart's law\index{Goodhart's law} at industrial scale: the insurer controls the diagnostic coding that feeds the model, so ``upcoding,''\index{upcoding} documenting members as sicker than clinically warranted, converts directly into revenue. Adding one unsupported vascular-disease code at $0.13$ lifts the score to $1.43$, worth an extra \$1{,}300 per member per year, or \$1 billion a year across a million-member book for every tenth of a point. Both academic estimates and government audits have found aggregate coding-intensity effects of roughly this magnitude, making risk-score integrity one of the largest financial-modeling enforcement areas in the United States.

The AI story is symmetrical. On the revenue side, insurers deploy NLP on clinical charts\index{natural language processing (NLP)} to find \emph{legitimately supportable} diagnoses the treating physician documented but never coded, a use that is lawful and, from the model's perspective, accuracy-improving. On the audit side, the simplest anomaly test is already decisive at scale\index{risk adjustment!upcoding audit}\index{anomaly detection}. If an insurer codes vascular disease in $9\%$ of $10{,}000$ enrollees against a clinically expected regional prevalence of $6\%$, the excess is $z = (0.09 - 0.06)/\sqrt{0.06 \times 0.94/10{,}000} \approx 12.6$ standard errors. No plausible casemix difference explains that pattern; it flags the book for chart-level review, where Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s anomaly-detection machinery takes over. A large $z$ proves the coding is anomalous, not that it is fraudulent, and distinguishing real from manufactured casemix is the human-review step the statistics only prioritize.

\subsection{The Investment Side: Asset-Liability Management}
\label{sec:ins-alm}\index{asset-liability management (ALM)}

An insurer's balance sheet is a leveraged bond portfolio wrapped around a promise, managed with Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s duration machinery, with the twist that both sides move with interest rates. Consider a life insurer with \$110 million of assets of duration $5$ against \$100 million of liabilities of duration $12$ (long-duration promises are the nature of the product), for a surplus of \$10 million. A one-percentage-point \emph{rise} in rates moves assets by $-110 \times 5 \times 1\% = -\$5.5$ million but liabilities by $-100 \times 12 \times 1\% = -\$12$ million, so surplus \emph{rises} to \$16.5 million; a one-point \emph{fall} symmetrically compresses surplus to \$3.5 million. The sign is the point: with liabilities longer than assets, the classic life-insurance posture, it is \emph{falling} rates that quietly impair the industry\index{interest rate risk}. That is what a decade of near-zero rates did to life insurers and pension funds worldwide, and why duration-matching, immunization, and long-dated hedging are a core insurance discipline.

The machine-learning layer enters through a fact the calculation assumed away: a liability's duration is not bond mathematics, because the ``bond'' can walk. Policyholders hold options (to lapse a policy, surrender it for cash, or pour new money into a guarantee) and exercise them in response to rates. When market rates rise well above the credited rate, surrender becomes attractive; when guarantees are above market, policyholders cling to them. Insurers model this with dynamic lapse\index{dynamic lapse}\index{asset-liability management (ALM)!dynamic lapse} functions fitted to policyholder-level experience data, with the rate gap, policy age, tax penalties, and distribution channel as features, and the fitted behavior sets the liability's \emph{effective} duration. Rerunning the worked example with behavior in it: if a one-point rate rise accelerates lapses enough to shorten effective liability duration from $12$ to $9$, surplus rises only to $110 - 5.5 - (100 - 9) = \$13.5$ million rather than \$16.5 million; if a one-point fall slows lapses and extends duration to $14$, surplus compresses to $110 + 5.5 - (100 + 14) = \$1.5$ million rather than \$3.5 million. Behavior has clipped the good outcome \emph{and} deepened the bad one, the signature of a sold option: Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s negative convexity\index{convexity} with a human exercising it. The behavioral model, not the bond arithmetic, determines whether the hedge book is sized correctly, and the 2022--2023 rate spike gave these models their first real out-of-sample test in a generation.

Two further machine-learning deployments round out the ALM picture. Valuing blocks with embedded guarantees (variable annuities above all) requires nested simulation, with economic scenarios outside and valuation runs inside each scenario, whose cost grows multiplicatively. Insurers increasingly train neural-network surrogates\index{neural network} (function approximators in the least-squares Monte Carlo tradition) to replace the inner valuation loop, making hedging programs computable overnight rather than over weeks; the surrogate's approximation error is itself a model risk to be validated. And the portfolio problem that remains, investing float and surplus against regulatory constraints on asset risk, is Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s mean-variance machinery under a solvency constraint\index{mean-variance optimization}. That is why insurers became early, large buyers of the private credit assets\index{private credit} Chapter~\chnum{markets} described: long, illiquid, yield-bearing assets are a natural match for long, illiquid, \emph{behaviorally modeled} liabilities.

\section{Capital, Catastrophe, and Fairness}

The tail is where insurance is decided; this part covers catastrophe capital, solvency regulation, and the fairness questions new data raises.

\subsection{Catastrophe Risk, Reinsurance, and Alternative Capital}
\label{sec:ins-cat}\index{catastrophe risk}

Pooling fails where insurance is needed most. Catastrophe losses are not independent across policyholders (a hurricane correlates a hundred thousand claims into one event), so Section~\ref{sec:ins-fundamentals}'s $\sqrt{n}$ arithmetic collapses and the tail must be either modeled and capitalized or transferred. Transfer happens through reinsurance\index{reinsurance}, insurance for insurers, typically written in layers stacked into a tower\index{reinsurance!layers}, as in Figure~\ref{fig:ins-tower}. For example, a reinsurer sells a \$100 million layer attaching at \$50 million (it pays losses between \$50M and \$150M from a single event). If catastrophe models put the annual probability of an event reaching the layer at $0.04$ and the expected loss within the layer, given such an event, at \$60 million, the layer's expected annual loss is $0.04 \times 60 = \$2.4$ million, a ``rate on line''\index{rate on line}\index{reinsurance!rate on line} of $2.4\%$ of the limit, before the substantial risk load such correlated, capital-intensive exposure commands. The same exposure increasingly reaches capital markets directly through catastrophe bonds\index{catastrophe bond}\index{catastrophe risk!alternative capital}, whose investors receive a spread, typically a multiple of the modeled expected loss, for bearing a tail risk largely uncorrelated with market risk: the diversification argument of Chapter~\chnum{portfolio} applied to hurricanes.

The models behind these numbers are Hurricane Andrew's institutional legacy: simulation engines that generate tens of thousands of synthetic hurricane seasons or earthquake catalogs\index{catastrophe risk!catastrophe models}, propagate each event's physics over a portfolio of insured locations, and apply engineering damage functions: Monte Carlo over a compound process, Chapter~\chnum{oprisk}'s architecture at continental scale. AI's contribution is concentrated in the inputs. Computer vision on aerial and satellite imagery\index{satellite imagery} (Chapter~\chnum{altdata}'s methods) now determines roof geometry, construction type, and post-event damage at the individual-building level, replacing what was once a coarse, self-reported exposure database. Climate-conditioned event generation, which draws synthetic seasons from warming scenarios rather than from the historical record alone, extends these models toward the forward-looking scenario analysis that climate stress testing requires, the physical-risk counterpart of the transition-risk stress\index{climate risk!physical risk} that Section~\chnum{creditrisk}.2.5 applies to a bank's loan book.

That extension confronts the deepest assumption in catastrophe modeling. A simulated event catalog treats the hazard as \emph{stationary}\index{physical climate risk}: the frequency and severity of hurricanes, floods, and wildfires are estimated from decades of history and assumed to describe next year, which a warming climate undermines. Warmer oceans shift the distribution of hurricane intensity and rainfall, and rising sea levels raise the base on which every storm surge builds, so a catalog calibrated on the past understates the present, and the bias compounds over the multi-decade horizons of mortgages and infrastructure. Near-term, modelers use ``warm-sea-surface-temperature'' or climate-conditioned event sets that reweight the historical catalog toward the conditions expected in the coming seasons. Longer-term, they condition the hazard module on climate-model output under named scenarios, the NGFS-style families supervisors use\index{climate risk!scenario analysis}, so that a 2050 loss distribution can be produced alongside today's. Machine learning does most of its work in the mapping: convolutional networks trained on satellite and radar imagery\index{convolutional neural network (CNN)} delineate past flood extents and burn scars where no survey exists, and statistical downscaling translates coarse global climate projections, run on grids tens of kilometers wide, into street-level hazard estimates. The model risk is substantial and largely unvalidatable\index{model risk}, since the out-of-sample period is the future, so such models are best used, as Chapter~\chnum{oprisk} prescribes for every structural model, for comparative scenario analysis rather than as a single forecast.

\subsection{Parametric Insurance and Basis Risk}
\label{sec:ins-parametric}

Parametric insurance\index{parametric insurance} pays a fixed amount when a measured \emph{index} crosses a trigger (wind speed at a reference station, rainfall over a growing season, ground acceleration at a site) rather than indemnifying assessed damage. The payout requires no adjuster, so it arrives in days rather than months, and the product can be sold where claims adjustment is impractical. That is why parametric structures dominate sovereign disaster pools, index-based crop insurance across the developing world, and consumer novelties like flight-delay cover, and why they are a natural match for the satellite and sensor data of Chapter~\chnum{altdata}.

The price of simplicity is basis risk\index{basis risk (parametric insurance)}\index{parametric insurance!basis risk}: the index and the loss are correlated, not identical. A worked example quantifies it. A parametric hurricane policy pays \$200{,}000 if measured wind at the reference station reaches the trigger. Suppose the modeled annual probability of the policyholder suffering material damage is $0.05$; given damage, the trigger fires with probability $0.80$; in the $0.95$ of years without damage, it false-fires with probability $0.01$. Then the policyholder faces a $0.05 \times 0.20 = 1.0\%$ annual probability of the worst outcome, real damage and no payout, while the insurer faces a $0.95 \times 0.01 = 0.95\%$ probability of paying without damage; the overall payout probability is $0.05 \times 0.80 + 0.0095 = 0.0495$, for a pure premium of $0.0495 \times 200{,}000 = \$9{,}900$. Both mismatch probabilities are design parameters. A denser sensor network or a model-based index (a ``cat-in-a-grid'' trigger computed from the physics models above) tightens the loss-index correlation and shrinks basis risk, which is where machine learning earns its keep in parametric design. The trade-off between trigger fidelity and trigger verifiability is Chapter~\chnum{oprisk}'s Goodhart discipline applied to contract design: an index must be objective and manipulation-proof because no adjuster will ever look at the actual loss.

\subsection{Solvency: Capital Regulation as a Value at Risk}
\label{sec:ins-solvency}\index{regulatory capital!insurance}

The regulatory capstone ties insurance back to Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}. Modern insurance solvency regimes, Solvency II\index{Solvency II} in Europe most explicitly, define required capital as a Value at Risk. The Solvency Capital Requirement is calibrated so that the insurer can absorb a $1$-in-$200$ one-year loss: a $99.5\%$ one-year VaR on the change in the insurer's own net asset value\index{Solvency II!99.5 percent VaR}, aggregated across underwriting, market, credit, and operational risk modules with prescribed correlations. The calculation is market-risk machinery pointed at a balance sheet instead of a trading book. If an insurer's one-year net-asset-value change has standard deviation \$40 million, a normal approximation puts the $99.5\%$ requirement at $2.576 \times 40 \approx \$103$ million. Everything Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk} said about the normal approximation understating fat tails\index{fat tails} applies with force, since underwriting results are compound-Poisson skewed, not Gaussian. U.S. risk-based capital reaches a similar structure by formula rather than internal model, and the internal-model-versus-standard-formula tension (risk-sensitivity against comparability and gameability) is the debate Chapter~\chnum{oprisk} described operational-risk capital resolving in favor of the formula.

Machine learning enters the capital calculation through its computational bottleneck. The $99.5\%$ one-year VaR requires the \emph{distribution} of the insurer's net asset value a year ahead, and net asset value at year one is itself a valuation of every embedded guarantee and option on the book, under each scenario: the nested-simulation explosion of Section~\ref{sec:ins-alm}, now at whole-balance-sheet scale. Internal-model insurers therefore lean on proxy models\index{proxy model (Solvency II)}\index{Solvency II!proxy models}: least-squares Monte Carlo regressions and, increasingly, neural-network approximators trained to reproduce the full valuation as a fast function of the scenario variables. A supervisor approving such an internal model is approving a machine-learning approximation of a balance sheet, so European supervisors have begun publishing guidance on ML inside internal models, and the surrogate's fitting error is examined in the far tail, the $0.5\%$ that sets the capital number, where any regression approximates worst. Every model in this chapter (pricing, reserving, catastrophe, mortality, behavior, and the proxy models on top) thus feeds a regulated capital number and lives inside the model-governance perimeter\index{model risk management} of Chapters~\chnum{oprisk} and~\chnum{xai}, with validation, documentation, and independent challenge as legal requirements.

\subsection{Underwriting with New Data and Text}
\label{sec:ins-underwriting}\index{underwriting!insurance}\index{underwriting!new data sources}

Commercial insurance underwriting is a document business: a submission arrives as a broker's narrative, loss runs from prior insurers, financial statements, and schedules of locations or vehicles, and an underwriter's first hours are spent extracting structure from paper. NLP-driven submission ingestion (extracting exposures, flagging omissions, triaging which submissions deserve an underwriter's scarce attention) has become the standard first deployment of language models in commercial carriers. Property underwriting increasingly begins from imagery rather than questionnaires, the building-level computer vision of Section~\ref{sec:ins-cat} applied one policy at a time. Cyber underwriting, one of the industry's fastest-growing and hardest lines, prices from external scans of an applicant's security posture, an alternative-data exercise in Chapter~\chnum{altdata}'s sense\index{alternative data}, applied to a peril whose correlated, catastrophe-like tail (one vulnerability, thousands of simultaneous claims) the industry is still learning to model.

\subsection{Fairness and Proxy Discrimination}
\label{sec:ins-fairness}\index{fairness!insurance}\index{proxy discrimination!insurance}

Insurance pricing discriminates by design, in the actuarial sense of charging different expected costs differently, and the law's task has always been separating legitimate risk-based distinctions from prohibited ones. AI sharpens this into Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s proxy problem: a model barred from using a protected characteristic\index{protected attribute} can reconstruct it from correlated inputs, the better the richer the feature set. Insurance regulators have moved on this most concretely. Colorado's SB 21-169 requires insurers to test models and external data sources for unfairly discriminatory outcomes and to govern them accordingly. The European Union's gender ruling prohibits gender-based pricing outright even where it is actuarially predictive, a legislature deliberately overriding statistical validity with a social judgment. And the U.S. state-by-state debate over credit-based insurance scores, predictive of claims but correlated with protected characteristics, rehearsed every argument in Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s fairness section decades before machine learning raised the stakes.

Colorado's statute has since been joined by a national template. On 4 December 2023 the National Association of Insurance Commissioners adopted its Model Bulletin on the Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems by Insurers\index{NAIC Model Bulletin on AI}. It expects each insurer to maintain a written program governing its AI systems (covering governance and senior-management accountability, risk management and internal controls, and due diligence on third-party models and data), encourages testing for bias and unfair discrimination, and reminds insurers that decisions made with AI must still comply with existing unfair-trade-practice and unfair-discrimination laws \parencite{naic2023bulletin}. A bulletin binds only where a state adopts it: as of 8 October 2026 the NAIC's own tracker lists it as adopted by 27 states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico, with California, Colorado, New York, and Texas regulating through their own insurance-specific rules instead \parencite{naic2026adoption}. In the European Union, the Artificial Intelligence Act\index{EU Artificial Intelligence Act}\index{EU Artificial Intelligence Act!high-risk systems} classifies AI systems used for risk assessment and pricing of natural persons in life and health insurance as high-risk, with the obligations Chapter~\chnum{xai} describes \parencite{eu2024aiact}, now deferred by the Digital Omnibus to 2 December 2027 \parencite{eu2026omnibus}.

A second, subtler fairness frontier is price optimization\index{price optimization (insurance)}: using demand models (each customer's estimated willingness to pay and propensity to shop) to set premiums \emph{above} risk-based cost for the customers least likely to leave. The practice is standard revenue management in airlines and retail, but insurance regulators in many U.S. states have banned it. Their ground is that an insurance price must reflect expected cost and expense, not exploitable inertia, and that the customers who shop least, often older and lower-income policyholders, are those the loyalty penalty falls on hardest. The United Kingdom reached the same conclusion in 2021, prohibiting renewal prices above the equivalent new-customer price. Here the objectionable model is \emph{perfectly accurate}: demand estimation is legitimate science, and the prohibition targets the model's use, not its errors. It is the clearest illustration in this book that model governance is about deployment decisions, not only statistical quality. Insurance supplies the financial industry's most developed case law on what ``fair pricing'' can mean operationally, and Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s apparatus (fairness metrics, proxy detection, counterfactual testing) supplies the tools its newest statutes effectively mandate.

\vignetteheading{\section}{snapshot}{Progressive's Snapshot and the Telematics Market}
\label{sec:ins-vignette}\index{Progressive Snapshot}\index{telematics}

Usage-based insurance moved from concept to mass market when Progressive, after more than a decade of pilots dating to the late 1990s, rolled out its Snapshot program nationally in the early 2010s: a plug-in device, later a phone app, recording mileage, time of day, and hard-braking events, with discounts tied to observed behavior. Two features of the episode reward attention. First, the earliest and largest benefit was not pricing precision but \emph{selection}. The offer of a monitoring discount attracted drivers who believed they drove well and repelled those who knew they did not, so the program improved the insurer's book before any telematics rating factor was filed: adverse selection running in reverse\index{adverse selection!in insurance}. Second, the program's evolution traces this chapter's regulatory arc in miniature. Early versions surcharged nothing and only discounted, because filing a telematics \emph{surcharge} required convincing regulators of the model's fairness and accuracy, and the industry's subsequent decade of negotiating what behavioral data may be used, retained, and shared previews the governance questions every AI pricing innovation now faces.

\section{Challenges in Insurance and Actuarial Modeling}

\textbf{The tail is uninsurable by experience.} Hurricane Andrew's lesson recurs: the losses that matter most are those history has not yet produced, so catastrophe, cyber, and pandemic risk must be modeled from structure rather than experience, with all of the model risk Chapter~\chnum{oprisk} attaches to that move.

\textbf{Feedback and selection are everywhere.}\index{selection bias} Prices change who buys, monitoring changes who enrolls, and claims automation changes what is claimed; almost no insurance dataset is a random sample of the risk it is used to price, and Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s selection warnings bind with unusual force.

\textbf{Long feedback loops.} A mispriced auto book reveals itself in a year; a mispriced liability or life book can take a decade, so model validation must lean on the indirect methods of Chapter~\chnum{oprisk}'s validation discussion rather than rapid outcome feedback. The float arithmetic of Section~\ref{sec:ins-economics} means the errors compound financially while they remain statistically invisible.

\textbf{Correlated tails defeat the product's own logic.} The industry sells the law of large numbers, but its gravest exposures (hurricanes, earthquakes, cyber events, pandemics) are the ones where independence fails; managing the gap between the diversifiable book and the undiversifiable tail is the industry's permanent structural problem.

\textbf{Accuracy versus accountability, institutionalized.} The filed-rating-plan regime makes the interpretability constraint structural: the industry's central modeling question is rarely ``what is the most accurate model'' but ``what is the most accurate model that can be filed, explained, and audited.''

\textbf{Privacy as a pricing input.}\index{data privacy} Telematics, wearables, and property imagery make the underwriting data itself the sensitive object, so data governance is not compliance overhead but part of product design.

\notocsection{Key Takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
\item Insurance inverts the production cycle (price first, costs later) and sells the law of large numbers retail: pooling $10{,}000$ independent risks cut relative loss uncertainty from $417\%$ to $4.17\%$ in the worked example, a $\sqrt{n}$ improvement that correlated catastrophe losses delete.
\item An insurer earns from two engines, the combined ratio and investment income on float; in the worked example a $95\%$ combined ratio and \$150M of float at $4\%$ produced a $22\%$ return on capital, and even a $103\%$ combined ratio remained profitable because underwriting losses are the cost of cheap float.
\item Classical pricing is frequency times severity with multiplicative relativities; one-way estimation double-counts correlated factors (a $43\%$ overcharge in the worked $2\times2$ example), which is why the jointly estimated GLM displaced it. ``Traditional pricing'' today means \emph{GLM-based} pricing, the decades-established, filed incumbent that machine learning must beat.
\item Machine learning's pricing advantage is the interaction: in the worked example, a young-urban interaction that no multiplicative model can represent leaked into the GLM's fitted marginals and overcharged the young-rural cell by $11.4\%$, while a depth-two tree priced all four cells exactly. Telematics runs the same logic at driver level, a fitted coefficient of $+0.163$ per harsh-braking event converting sensor data into a filed relativity (two fewer events than the cell average give $1.8 \times e^{0.163 \times (-2)} \approx 1.30$).
\item Credibility theory, $Z = n/(n+k)$, is Bayesian shrinkage derived by actuaries decades before machine learning rederived it as regularization: the worked fleet's $0.333$ observed frequency was blended to $0.147$ against a $0.100$ class rate.
\item The chain ladder projects ultimate losses from development factors ($1.50$ and $1.10$ in the worked triangle, a \$392.5 thousand reserve); reserves are the float, so reserving error is an economic event, not an accounting one.
\item Life insurance moves the weight to mortality and discounting (a \$961.54 net premium in the worked term-life example), with Lee-Carter forecasting mortality as factor extraction plus a random walk. Asset-liability management is Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s duration machinery on both sides of the balance sheet, where falling rates hurt the long-liability insurer. Its ML layer is behavioral: dynamic-lapse models make liability duration a fitted model output, and in the worked example behavior clipped the favorable surplus outcome ($16.5 \to 13.5$) while deepening the unfavorable one ($3.5 \to 1.5$), a sold option with a human exercising it.
\item Catastrophe risk is transferred through reinsurance towers and cat bonds priced from simulation models ($2.4\%$ rate on line in the worked layer), and solvency regulation caps the chapter as a $99.5\%$ one-year VaR on the insurer's own net assets.
\item Health insurance replaces long-tail reserving with a selection problem managed by risk adjustment, a regression with a budget attached, whose coding incentives (an extra $0.13$ of risk score was worth \$1{,}300 per enrollee per year in the worked example) make model integrity an adversarial, audited contest.
\item Parametric insurance trades adjusters for an index, paying instantly at the cost of basis risk: in the worked example, a $1.0\%$ annual chance of damage without payout against a \$9{,}900 pure premium, with index design, and its manipulation-proofness, the modeling problem.
\item Insurance fairness law, from credit-based insurance scores to Colorado's SB 21-169, the EU gender ruling, and the price-optimization bans, is the financial industry's most developed treatment of proxy discrimination and model \emph{use}, and it now effectively mandates Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s technical toolkit.
\end{itemize}

\notocsection{Suggested Exercises}

\begin{enumerate}[exercises]
\item Recompute Section~\ref{sec:ins-fundamentals}'s pooling example for $n = 100$ and $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ policies. At what $n$ does the probability of the portfolio's loss exceeding $110\%$ of its mean fall below $0.1\%$ (using the normal approximation), and why should you distrust that approximation in the far tail?
\item Recompute the worked pricing example for an experienced rural driver with frequency relativities $0.9$ (age) and $0.8$ (territory) and a severity relativity of $0.95$. What gross premium results, and what fraction of the young urban driver's premium is it?
\item Using Table~\ref{tab:ins-oneway}, verify by direct computation that the one-way relativities are $2.10$ and $1.833$, and then show that if the exposures had been balanced ($250$ in every cell, claims scaled to preserve each cell's frequency), the one-way method would have recovered the true relativities. What general condition on the exposure distribution makes one-way estimation unbiased?
\item In Section~\ref{sec:ins-interaction}'s interaction example, the GLM overcharged the young-rural cell by $11.4\%$. Explain the mechanism in your own words (why does an interaction concentrated in one cell distort the price of a \emph{different} cell?), and then describe how an actuary could keep the filed model multiplicative yet still fix the mispricing (hint: an interaction term is itself another rating variable). What does your fix imply about the practical role of a boosted challenger model in a GLM-filing regime?
\item A fleet has $5$ years of experience with credibility constant $k = 12$. Compute its credibility weight, and the blended frequency if it observed $0.30$ against a class rate of $0.10$. How many years of experience would the fleet need for its own data to receive $75\%$ weight?
\item Extend the chain-ladder triangle with a fourth accident year showing 12-month cumulative paid claims of $550$. Compute its projected ultimate loss and the new total reserve, and state precisely which assumption you invoked about Year 4's future development.
\item Recompute Section~\ref{sec:ins-alm}'s ALM example for an insurer that has hedged its liability duration down to $8$ using swaps. What is the surplus after a one-point fall in rates, and what has the hedge cost the insurer if rates instead rise by one point?
\item Suppose a dynamic-lapse model of the kind Section~\ref{sec:ins-alm} describes, fitted to policyholder experience, implies a lapse rate of $5\% + 4 \times \max(0, r - 3\%)$ per year against a $3\%$ credited rate, where $r$ is the market rate. Compute the lapse rate at market rates of $3\%$, $5\%$, and $6\%$; explain in option language (Chapter~\chnum{valuation}) why this behavior leaves the insurer short an option, the policyholder's right to surrender at book value when rates rise, which is a put on the policy's bond-like value or, equivalently, a call on rates; and describe what data a Chapter~\chnum{ml} classifier would need, and what drift monitoring it would require, before you would trust it to set the hedge for a one-in-a-generation rate spike.
\item A reinsurance layer of \$50 million attaching at \$150 million has a modeled $0.02$ annual probability of attachment and an expected in-layer loss of \$30 million given attachment. Compute its expected annual loss and rate on line, and explain why its risk load per dollar of expected loss should exceed that of Section~\ref{sec:ins-cat}'s lower layer.
\item In the parametric example of Section~\ref{sec:ins-parametric}, suppose a better index raises the trigger's hit rate given damage from $0.80$ to $0.95$ while leaving the false-trigger rate at $0.01$. Recompute both basis-risk probabilities and the pure premium, and explain why the policyholder should be willing to pay \emph{more} than the pure-premium increase for this redesign.
\item An auditor observes that an insurer's average Medicare Advantage risk score rose from $1.05$ to $1.20$ over five years while its members' hospitalization and mortality rates were flat. Using Section~\ref{sec:ins-health}, lay out the innocent and the upcoding explanations for this pattern; recompute the section's prevalence $z$-test for an insurer coding the same condition in $7\%$ of $2{,}500$ enrollees against the $6\%$ benchmark, and explain why this smaller book's identical-direction deviation is far weaker evidence; then propose one \emph{longitudinal} test (using the flat hospitalization data) that the cross-sectional prevalence test cannot provide.
\item A regulator bars a rating variable that is predictive but correlates with a protected characteristic. Using Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s distinction between statistical validity and disparate impact, argue both sides: the actuarial case for the variable and the fairness case against it, and identify what empirical test would inform (though not settle) the dispute.
\item \textbf{Challenge (real data).} The French motor third-party liability dataset (\texttt{freMTPL2freq}, available through OpenML and the \texttt{CASdatasets} project) contains claim counts and rating features for roughly $678{,}000$ real policies. Fit a Poisson GLM for claim frequency on driver age, vehicle age, and bonus-malus level, then fit a gradient-boosted model on the same features. Compare out-of-sample Poisson deviance, plot each model's implied age relativity, and identify one interaction the boosted model found that the GLM missed. Discuss which model you could defend in a rate filing, and what you would change about the boosted model to make it filable.
\end{enumerate}
