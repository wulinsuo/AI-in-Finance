\chapter{Natural Language Processing in Finance}

Every earlier chapter has worked with numbers that arrive already structured: prices, returns, ratios, transaction amounts. Yet much of the information that moves markets starts as an earnings-call transcript, a paragraph in a 10-K's risk factors, a news wire, or a social-media post. It must be converted into features before any of the tools of Chapters~\chnum{ml} through~\chnum{fraud} apply. This chapter develops that conversion and the techniques built on it, collectively natural language processing (NLP).\index{natural language processing (NLP)!in finance}

Several earlier threads converge here. Chapter~\chnum{intro} noted that deep learning learns directly from unstructured text, and that large language model\index{large language model (LLM)}s (LLMs) extend it to summarizing earnings calls and extracting information from filings. Chapter~\chnum{markets} observed that large asset managers use NLP to parse filings faster than human analysts. Chapter~\chnum{ml} introduced Naive Bayes\index{Naive Bayes classifier} as a generative classifier for word counts, of which LLMs are far more elaborate examples. Chapter~\chnum{algotrading} flagged that a sentiment score is only half of an event-driven strategy; executing the trade is the other half. This chapter makes those threads concrete.

Nineteen worked examples run through the chapter. The first build representations: bag-of-words and TF-IDF vectors for a few earnings-call sentences, and a sentiment score from a subset of the Loughran-McDonald dictionary, the lexicon used in finance research and practice. Those feed an event-driven trading signal, evaluated as Chapter~\chnum{algotrading} evaluates any strategy, and a Naive Bayes classifier that sorts 10-K risk-factor sentences into categories. The chapter then turns to modern architectures: word-embedding geometry, including a vector-arithmetic analogy, and a hand-computed scaled dot-product self-attention\index{self-attention} calculation, the core of the transformer, extended to show how a causal mask changes a token's representation across encoder-only, decoder-only, and encoder-decoder architectures. Then come a retrieval-augmented generation\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)} example grounding an answer in a retrieved passage, with precision@k and faithfulness metrics; a forward diffusion step for synthetic stress scenarios; a Gunning Fog readability comparison; a low-rank adaptation (LoRA) parameter count; a three-step LLM agent that computes and cross-checks a financial ratio; how per-step reliability compounds over a multi-step agent task; and an API-versus-self-hosting cost comparison showing when quantization's memory savings become deployment savings.

Because generative AI (LLMs, retrieval, prompting, and synthetic data from GANs and diffusion model\index{diffusion model}s) is developed here in more depth than any earlier topic, this chapter runs longer than the rest of the book.

\section{Text as Data}

Most financial information arrives as prose, and this part turns that prose into numbers a model can use.

\subsection{Text as Data in Finance}
\label{sec:text-data}\index{text as data}\index{natural language processing (NLP)!text as data}

Financial text differs from the numerical data used so far in three ways that matter for modeling. First, it is unstructured: a sentence has no predefined fields the way a balance sheet line does, so the first step is always to impose structure. Second, it is domain-specific: sentiment analysis trained on product reviews\index{sentiment analysis} or social media misreads financial language. ``Liability'' is neutral accounting terminology, and ``losses were smaller than expected'' is good news phrased with two words, losses and smaller, that a generic model would likely score as negative. Third, financial text is produced under legal and strategic incentives: management chooses what to disclose, how to phrase it, and how much to hedge, so the text is a strategic object, not a neutral report.

The tokenize step\index{tokenization (of text)} decides what the units of text are. Classical methods split text into words at spaces and punctuation, so a word unseen in training is unknown. Transformer models instead use subword tokenization\index{tokenization (of text)!byte-pair encoding}, most often byte-pair encoding\index{byte-pair encoding (BPE)} (BPE)~\parencite{sennrich2016neural}: start from single characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new entry until the vocabulary reaches a target size, typically tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand entries. Common words become one token, while a rare one, a new ticker or a drug name, splits into familiar pieces, so no input is out of vocabulary. Numbers are where this matters in finance. A tokenizer chops a figure such as 1234567 into chunks set by merge frequencies, not place value, so the digit groups need not line up with thousands and millions. \textcite{singh2024tokenization} show the cost: merely forcing digits into groups of three from the right, as thousands separators do, raised GPT-4's accuracy on an addition task from 84.4\% to 98.9\%. The practical rule, applied in Section~\ref{sec:llm-agents}, is to treat a language model as a reader of numbers, not a calculator: let it extract figures, and hand the arithmetic to code.

\subsection{From Text to Numbers: Bag-of-Words and TF-IDF}
\label{sec:bow-tfidf}\index{bag-of-words}

Suppose an analyst wants to feed thousands of earnings-call transcripts into one of Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s classifiers, each of which expects a fixed-length numeric vector, not a paragraph. The simplest conversion is the bag-of-words representation: fix a vocabulary, and represent each document by the count of each term it contains, discarding word order. This is drastic (``growth despite headwinds'' and ``headwinds despite growth'' produce identical vectors), but it is a surprisingly effective baseline, and it is the ``word counts'' representation Chapter~\chnum{ml} anticipated for Naive Bayes.

A raw count, however, overweights common words that appear in almost every document and discriminate little. Term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) corrects for this.\index{TF-IDF} For term $t$ in document $d$, drawn from a collection of $N$ documents,
\begin{equation}
\text{tf-idf}(t,d) = \text{tf}(t,d) \times \ln\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right),
\label{eq:nlp-tfidf}
\end{equation}
where $\text{tf}(t,d)$ is the raw count of $t$ in $d$ and $\text{df}(t)$ is the number of documents containing $t$ at least once. A term that appears in every document has $\text{df}(t)=N$ and therefore an idf of $\ln(1)=0$, its tf-idf weight vanishes regardless of how often it appears, while a term confined to a single document receives the largest possible idf weight.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-bow-tfidf}{Bag-of-Words and TF-IDF for Four Earnings-Call Sentences}\index{TF-IDF}\index{bag-of-words!four earnings-call sentences}

Consider four short earnings-call sentences, treated as four documents ($N=4$):

\begin{quote}
\small
$D_1$: ``revenue growth exceeded expectations this quarter''\\
$D_2$: ``management is cautious about revenue growth given the headwinds''\\
$D_3$: ``management remains confident despite the headwinds this quarter''\\
$D_4$: ``expectations were not met and revenue declined''
\end{quote}

Restricting attention to six vocabulary terms gives the term-frequency matrix in Table~\ref{tab:bow_tfidf}, alongside each term's document frequency, idf, and resulting tf-idf weight in $D_1$.

\begin{booktable}[htbp]{Term frequency, document frequency, idf, and TF-IDF weight in $D_1$}{tab:bow_tfidf}
\begin{tabular}{lccccccc}
\toprule
Term & $D_1$ & $D_2$ & $D_3$ & $D_4$ & df & idf $=\ln(4/\text{df})$ & tf-idf in $D_1$ \\
\midrule
revenue & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 3 & 0.2877 & 0.2877 \\
growth & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 & 0.6931 & 0.6931 \\
headwinds & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 2 & 0.6931 & 0.0000 \\
expectations & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 0.6931 & 0.6931 \\
quarter & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 & 0.6931 & 0.6931 \\
management & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 2 & 0.6931 & 0.0000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{booktable}

The first four columns of Table~\ref{tab:bow_tfidf} are the bag-of-words representation. Over the vocabulary (revenue, growth, headwinds, expectations, quarter, management) they give $D_1 = (1,1,0,1,1,0)$ and $D_2 = (1,1,1,0,0,1)$, the vectors a classifier would receive without weighting. They make the loss of word order concrete: any rearrangement of $D_1$'s words produces the same vector, and $D_1$ and $D_4$ share two terms although one reports growth and the other a decline.

``Revenue'' appears in three of the four documents, so it earns a modest idf of $\ln(4/3) \approx 0.2877$; every other term appears in two, giving a larger idf of $\ln(4/2) = \ln 2 \approx 0.6931$. ``Headwinds'' and ``management'' do not appear in $D_1$, so their tf-idf weight in $D_1$ is zero whatever their idf, while ``growth,'' ``expectations,'' and ``quarter'' each occur once in $D_1$ and contribute their full $0.6931$. A downstream classifier, such as the Naive Bayes model of Section~\ref{sec:naive-bayes-text}, would receive this in place of raw counts.

\subsection{Financial Sentiment Analysis and the Loughran-McDonald Dictionary}\index{Loughran-McDonald dictionary}
\label{sec:sentiment}

Suppose a trader wants to know, within seconds of an earnings call ending, whether management sounded upbeat or defensive, without waiting for an analyst's summary. The most direct way to score sentiment is a lexicon-based approach\index{sentiment analysis!dictionary-based}: maintain a list of positive and negative financial words, count how many of each appear in a document, and combine the counts into a tone score,
\begin{equation}
\text{tone} = \frac{\#\text{positive words} - \#\text{negative words}}{\#\text{total words}}.
\label{eq:nlp-tone}
\end{equation}
Generic lexicons, built from product reviews or general text, perform poorly in finance for the reason Section~\ref{sec:text-data} raised: ``tax,'' ``cost,'' or ``liability'' read as negative in everyday English but are neutral in a filing. The Loughran-McDonald dictionary, built from 10-K filings, classifies words into finance-specific categories (positive, negative, uncertainty, litigious, and others) by how they function in financial disclosure.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-transcript-tone}{Scoring Two Earnings-Call Transcripts}\index{sentiment analysis!earnings calls}\index{earnings call!transcript scoring}

Using a small subset of real Loughran-McDonald positive words\index{Loughran-McDonald dictionary!net tone score} (\{strong, growth, exceeded, confident, improved\}) and negative words (\{cautious, headwinds, decline, declined, weak, concern, concerns\}), consider two short transcripts:

\begin{quote}
\small
Transcript A: ``revenue growth was strong this quarter and results exceeded expectations across every segment we remain confident heading into next year''\\
Transcript B: ``management is cautious about headwinds and results declined modestly we have some concerns about demand next quarter''
\end{quote}

Transcript A contains 20 words, of which 4 (growth, strong, exceeded, confident) are positive and none are negative, giving a tone score of $(4-0)/20 = 0.20$. Transcript B contains 17 words, of which none are positive and 4 (cautious, headwinds, declined, concerns) are negative, giving a tone score of $(0-4)/17 \approx -0.2353$. Scores like these feed the event-driven trading signal developed next.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-central-bank}{Scoring Central-Bank Communication}\index{sentiment analysis!central-bank communication}\index{central-bank communication}

Company disclosures are not the only text markets parse by machine. The Federal Open Market Committee's post-meeting statement\index{FOMC statement analysis} is arguably the most market-moving recurring document in finance, and it is scored with the machinery above, with one twist: the axis is not positive versus negative but \emph{hawkish} versus \emph{dovish}, language signaling tighter versus easier future monetary policy\index{monetary policy}. The sign convention depends on the asset: a ``strong'' labor market is good news about the economy but hawkish news for bond prices. A general-purpose dictionary, and even Loughran-McDonald, therefore misreads central-bank language as generic lexicons misread 10-Ks, and practitioners maintain purpose-built hawkish and dovish word lists instead.

Using the hawkish list \{strong, elevated, increases, tightening, restrictive\} and the dovish list \{moderated, eased, easing, patient, softening\}, consider two consecutive stylized statements:

\begin{quote}
\small
January: ``inflation remains elevated and the committee anticipates that ongoing increases in the target range will be appropriate the labor market remains strong''\\
March: ``inflation has moderated in recent months and the committee will be patient as it assesses incoming data the labor market has shown signs of softening''
\end{quote}

The January statement contains 22 words, of which 3 (elevated, increases, strong) are hawkish and none are dovish, giving a net-hawkishness score of $(3-0)/22 \approx +0.1364$. The March statement contains 25 words, of which none are hawkish and 3 (moderated, patient, softening) are dovish, scoring $(0-3)/25 = -0.1200$. What a rates trader cares about, though, is neither level but the \emph{change}: a drift of $-0.1200 - 0.1364 \approx -0.2564$, a decisive swing from hawkish to dovish. Every participant has already read the previous statement, so the information in a new one lies almost entirely in what changed. That is why coverage of an FOMC release is dominated by redline comparisons against the prior statement, and why the tone \emph{difference} is the natural trading signal: the event-driven logic of the next section applied to the policy calendar. Research versions, scoring decades of central-bank communication and measuring the market reaction to its unanticipated component, are now a standard tool in empirical monetary economics (see Further Reading).

\subsection{Disclosure Analysis: Readability and the Gunning Fog Index}
\label{sec:disclosure-analysis}\index{readability}

Regulators require public disclosures to be understandable to ordinary investors (the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's ``Plain English'' rule is one such requirement), and readability metrics measure whether a passage meets that bar. The Gunning Fog index estimates the years\index{readability!Gunning Fog index} of formal education a reader needs to understand a passage on a first reading,
\begin{equation}
\text{Fog} = 0.4 \left[ \frac{\text{words}}{\text{sentences}} + 100 \times \frac{\text{complex words}}{\text{words}} \right],
\label{eq:nlp-fog}
\end{equation}
where a complex word is (approximately) one with three or more syllables.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-fog-paragraphs}{Two Disclosure Paragraphs Compared}\index{Gunning Fog index}

Consider two short passages. Paragraph A: ``The firm's sales grew this year. Profits also rose. We expect this trend to grow next year.'' This has 17 words across 3 sentences and zero complex words (every word has one or two syllables), giving $\text{Fog} = 0.4\,[(17/3) + 100(0/17)] = 2.27$.

Paragraph B: ``The Company's operations are subject to numerous regulatory, competitive, and macroeconomic uncertainties. These uncertainties could materially and adversely affect anticipated profitability and operational continuity.'' This has 24 words across 2 sentences, of which 14 are complex (numerous, regulatory, competitive, macroeconomic, uncertainties, materially, adversely, anticipated, profitability, operational, continuity, and others), giving $\text{Fog} = 0.4\,[(24/2) + 100(14/24)] = 28.13$.

A Fog index above roughly 17 corresponds to a post-graduate reading level; Paragraph B's 28.13 is far beyond that. The toy paragraphs are built to contrast sharply, but the direction matches a well-documented finding: 10-K risk-factor sections and other mandatory disclosures routinely score\index{10-K filing} in this elevated range, dense, technical, and long-sentence.

Readability metrics give compliance teams an automatable way to flag disclosures that may miss the plain-English standard, and give researchers a candidate predictive signal (some published research finds that harder-to-read disclosures are associated with worse subsequent firm performance, consistent with strategic obfuscation).

The Fog index is also useful during drafting, not only after the fact. Rewriting Paragraph B in plainer language\index{readability!plain-English rewrite}, ``Our business faces many risks from rules, competition, and the economy. These risks could hurt our profits and how well we operate,'' gives 22 words across 2 sentences with only 3 complex words (competition, economy, operate), so
\begin{equation*}
\text{Fog} = 0.4\,[(22/2) + 100(3/22)] \approx 9.85,
\end{equation*}
close to Paragraph A's score and nowhere near Paragraph B's original 28.13, while covering the same categories of risk and the same warning about profitability. This is the use a Plain English rule intends to encourage: the legal and business meaning did not change, only sentence structure and word choice, yet the Fog score fell by nearly two-thirds. A readability check built into the drafting workflow can therefore flag an overly complex passage before it is filed, not after a regulator or researcher measures it.

\section{Classical Text Models}

Before transformers, text was classified and traded on with methods that remain fast, auditable, and often sufficient.

\weheading{\subsection}{nlp-event-signal}{An Event-Driven Sentiment Trading Signal}\index{event-driven sentiment trading signal}\index{sentiment analysis!trading signal}
\label{sec:event-driven-signal}

A sentiment score is only half of an event-driven strategy\index{news and event-driven trading}: the signal must still be evaluated like any trading strategy, and eventually converted into an executed position, which is Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s concern. This section stops at generating and evaluating the signal.

Suppose eight earnings-call events produce the tone scores and next-day stock returns in Table~\ref{tab:event_driven_signal}. A simple trading rule takes a long position when tone is positive and a short position when tone is negative, so the strategy's return on each event is $\text{sign}(\text{tone}) \times \text{return}$.

\begin{booktable}[htbp]{Eight earnings-call events: tone score, next-day return, signal, and strategy return}{tab:event_driven_signal}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Event & Tone score & Next-day return (\%) & Signal & Strategy return (\%) & Correct? \\
\midrule
1 & $+0.15$ & $+1.2$ & Long & $+1.2$ & Yes \\
2 & $-0.10$ & $-0.8$ & Short & $+0.8$ & Yes \\
3 & $+0.08$ & $+0.3$ & Long & $+0.3$ & Yes \\
4 & $-0.05$ & $+0.4$ & Short & $-0.4$ & No \\
5 & $+0.20$ & $+2.1$ & Long & $+2.1$ & Yes \\
6 & $-0.18$ & $-1.5$ & Short & $+1.5$ & Yes \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{booktable}

The strategy is correct (its position's sign matches the return's) in 6 of 8 events, a 75\% hit rate. Events 4 and 7 are misses: in event 4, mildly negative tone preceded a small gain, and in event 7, mildly positive tone a small loss. In both the tone score was close to zero and only weakly informative: a sentiment signal is noisy, not a deterministic predictor. Comparing the two strategies (Sharpe-like ratio: mean divided by standard deviation, with no annualization applied here),
\begin{align*}
\text{Signal-driven strategy:} &\quad \text{mean} = 0.775\%, \ \text{std} = 0.8481\%, \ \text{Sharpe-like} = 0.9138, \\
\text{Buy-and-hold:} &\quad \text{mean} = 0.075\%, \ \text{std} = 1.1829\%, \ \text{Sharpe-like} = 0.0634.
\end{align*} The signal adds value on this small sample, with a higher and more consistent return, even though it is wrong a quarter of the time. Turning it into an executed position, at the right size and without excessive market impact\index{market impact}, is the execution problem of Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}.

The tone score is a text-based sentiment measure, worth placing beside a price-based one: the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX)\index{CBOE Volatility Index (VIX)} from Chapter~\chnum{valuation}, the market's ``fear gauge,'' which aggregates option prices into a forward-looking measure of expected market-wide volatility without reading any text. The nickname needs care: VIX is mostly a forecast of how much the index will move, and only partly a reading of how investors feel, because an option's price compensates both for expected volatility and for the discomfort of bearing it. Only that second, risk-compensation component behaves like a sentiment score\index{CBOE Volatility Index (VIX)!as a sentiment proxy}; the headline index blends the two. The measures are complementary. A tone score captures sentiment specific to one company's call, which VIX cannot see, while VIX moves on macroeconomic or geopolitical news that names no company and that a firm-specific model was never trained to detect. A more sophisticated strategy conditions on both, using VIX as a market-wide risk-regime signal, as Chapter~\chnum{algotrading} describes, beside the firm-specific tone score: a positive tone is a different proposition in a calm market than in one gripped by elevated uncertainty.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-event-study}{An Event Study: Abnormal Returns Around One Call}\index{event study}

Table~\ref{tab:event_driven_signal} judged the signal against raw next-day returns, which mix the stock's reaction to its own news with its ordinary co-movement with the market. On a day the market rises $1\%$, a stock with a beta above one rises too, whatever management said. The event study \parencite{campbell1997econometrics} separates the two, and the evidence that text moves prices, including the research behind the Loughran-McDonald dictionary, is almost all stated in its terms. It has three steps. First, fit the market model\index{event study!market model}
\begin{equation*}
R_t = \alpha + \beta R_{m,t} + \varepsilon_t
\end{equation*}
over an estimation window that ends well before the event, so the news cannot contaminate the fit; here 120 trading days, days $-130$ to $-11$. Second, over a short event window, compute each day's abnormal return\index{event study!abnormal return} $AR_t = R_t - (\hat\alpha + \hat\beta R_{m,t})$, the part of the return the market does not explain, and add them into the cumulative abnormal return\index{event study!cumulative abnormal return} $CAR$. Third, test it: if the event carried no news, each $AR_t$ is noise with the residual standard deviation $\hat\sigma$ of the fit, so over an $L$-day window
\begin{equation}
t = \frac{CAR}{\hat\sigma\sqrt{L}},
\label{eq:nlp-car-t}
\end{equation}
which ignores the small additional error from estimating $\hat\alpha$ and $\hat\beta$ on 120 days.

Apply this to event 5 of Table~\ref{tab:event_driven_signal}, a tone score of $+0.20$ followed by a $+2.1\%$ return. The companion notebook simulates the estimation window from a market model with $\alpha = 0.02\%$, $\beta = 1.30$, and $\sigma = 1.40\%$ a day, so the fit can be checked against the truth: it returns $\hat\alpha = 0.0260\%$, $\hat\beta = 1.2949$, $\hat\sigma = 1.4215\%$, and $R^2 = 0.5022$. The call is held after the close on day 0, so the reaction falls on day $+1$, and the event window is days $-1$ to $+1$, with stock returns of $-0.1\%$, $0.3\%$, and $2.1\%$ and market returns of $-0.2\%$, $0.4\%$, and $0.9\%$ (expected returns computed from the unrounded estimates):
\begin{align*}
\text{Day } -1&: \quad AR = -0.10 - (0.0260 + 1.2949 \times (-0.20)) = -0.10 - (-0.2330) = 0.1330\%, \\
\text{Day } 0&: \quad AR = 0.30 - (0.0260 + 1.2949 \times 0.40) = 0.30 - 0.5439 = -0.2439\%, \\
\text{Day } +1&: \quad AR = 2.10 - (0.0260 + 1.2949 \times 0.90) = 2.10 - 1.1914 = 0.9086\%,
\end{align*}
so $CAR(-1,+1) = 0.7977\%$, with standard error $1.4215\sqrt{3} = 2.4621\%$, $t = 0.324$, and a two-sided $p$-value of $0.7465$.

Two lessons carry back to the trading signal. First, the market explains $56.7\%$ of the $2.1\%$ that Table~\ref{tab:event_driven_signal} credited to tone: the stock's expected return on day $+1$, given the market's $0.9\%$ rise and its beta of about $1.3$, was already $1.19\%$, and the abnormal part is $0.91\%$. A signal scored on raw returns gets credit for every market rally that follows a positive call, so the hit rate and Sharpe-like ratio above should be recomputed on abnormal returns before anyone trades on them. Second, one event proves nothing. With a daily idiosyncratic volatility of $1.42\%$, even day $+1$'s abnormal return alone has $t = 0.639$, and a three-day CAR of $0.80\%$ is indistinguishable from noise. Averaging over $N$ comparable events shrinks the standard error by $\sqrt{N}$: the same average CAR would give $t = 1.02$ over 10 events and $t = 2.05$ over 40. That is why published evidence on text and returns pools thousands of filings or calls, and why eight events cannot establish that a sentiment signal works.

\subsection{Naive Bayes and Document Classification}
\label{sec:naive-bayes-text}\index{document classification}\index{Naive Bayes classifier!document classification}

Suppose a compliance team must sort thousands of 10-K risk-factor sentences into ``Credit Risk'' or ``Market Risk,'' a task too large to do by hand each filing season and too nuanced for a keyword rule. Chapter~\chnum{ml} introduced Naive Bayes as a generative classifier that estimates
\begin{equation}
\Pr(y=k \mid x_1,\ldots,x_p) \propto \Pr(y=k)\prod_{j=1}^p \Pr(x_j \mid y=k)
\label{eq:nlp-naive-bayes}
\end{equation}
and noted its suitability for text, where features are word counts and the conditional-independence assumption, while not literally true, distorts less than it might for correlated financial ratios. This section applies it using only the counts of six vocabulary words (default, borrower, counterparty, volatility, rate, currency).

Table~\ref{tab:nb_training_sentences} shows the six training sentences, three per class, written in the style of a 10-K risk-factors disclosure.

\begin{booktable}[htbp]{Training sentences for the 10-K risk-factor Naive Bayes classifier}{tab:nb_training_sentences}
\begin{tabular}{L{10cm}l}
\toprule

\subsection{Word Embeddings: From Sparse Counts to Dense Vectors}
\label{sec:embeddings}

Bag-of-words and TF-IDF vectors are sparse and high-dimensional: with a vocabulary of tens of thousands of words, most entries in any document's
vector are zero, and nothing records that ``stock'' and ``equity'' are related while ``stock'' and ``bond'' are not: every
word is a separate dimension. An embedding\index{word embeddings} is a fixed-length, continuous-valued vector standing in for a discrete object, here a word, chosen so that geometric closeness reflects closeness in meaning. Word embedding methods (Word2Vec and GloVe\index{Word2Vec}\index{word embeddings!Word2Vec} are the classical examples) learn a dense,
low-dimensional vector for each word from co-occurrence patterns in a large corpus, so that words used in similar contexts get similar
vectors. Cosine similarity,
\begin{equation}
\cos(u,v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}
\label{eq:nlp-cosine}
\end{equation}
is the standard way to measure how similar two embedding vectors are.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-cosine-embeddings}{Cosine Similarity of Toy Embeddings}\index{word embeddings!cosine similarity}\index{cosine similarity}

To illustrate the geometry without training a model, assign six financial terms small hand-chosen two-dimensional vectors:
\begin{align*}
\text{stock} &= (0.90, 0.20), & \text{equity} &= (0.85, 0.25), \\
\text{bond} &= (0.10, 0.90), & \text{debt} &= (0.15, 0.85), \\
\text{bullish} &= (0.80, -0.30), & \text{bearish} &= (-0.75, -0.35).
\end{align*}
Computing cosine similarity between related pairs gives $\cos(\text{stock}, \text{equity}) \approx 0.9977$ and $\cos(\text{bond}, \text{debt}) \approx 0.9980$, both close to 1: the vectors point in nearly the same direction. Unrelated or opposed pairs differ: $\cos(\text{stock}, \text{bond}) \approx 0.3234$ is only weakly similar, and $\cos(\text{bullish}, \text{bearish}) \approx -0.7000$ is strongly negative, capturing near-opposites. In an embedding trained on financial text, the same structure, related concepts clustering and opposites pointing apart, emerges from co-occurrence alone, without the model being told what any word means.

A more striking property is that vector \emph{arithmetic}\index{word embeddings!vector arithmetic} can capture relationships between words, not just similarity, and this toy example was built so that the classic ``analogy'' relation holds exactly. Computing $\text{stock} - \text{equity} + \text{bond}$,
\begin{equation*}
(0.90, 0.20) - (0.85, 0.25) + (0.10, 0.90) = (0.15, 0.85) = \text{debt},
\end{equation*}
reproduces the debt vector exactly, because the near-synonym pairs stock/equity and bond/debt were placed at the identical offset, $(0.05, -0.05)$. In a trained embedding space, the same arithmetic famously (if imperfectly) captures ``king $-$ man $+$ woman $\approx$ queen.'' In a financial space, an analogous relation might hold between a country and its currency, letting an analyst query by relationship rather than single-word similarity. Real embeddings only approximate this, and the approximation is weaker for rare or abstract financial terms, but the toy example isolates the geometric idea before the noise of a high-dimensional trained model obscures it.

\section{Transformers and Large Language Models}

Attention changed what a language model can represent, and this part develops it from the mechanism up to retrieval and prompting.

\subsection{Attention and the Transformer Architecture}
\label{sec:attention-transformers}\index{attention mechanism}\index{transformer architecture}

Embeddings give each word a fixed vector regardless of context, but the same word can mean different things in different sentences (``the Fed will likely raise rates'' versus ``the flood raised water levels''). Transformer models address this with self-attention.\index{attention mechanism!self-attention} Each token's embedding $x$ is linearly projected, using three weight matrices $W^Q$, $W^K$, $W^V$ learned in training and shared across every token, into a query vector $Q=xW^Q$, a key vector $K=xW^K$, and a value vector $V=xW^V$: the same embedding viewed through three learned lenses. Intuitively, a token's query asks ``what am I looking for,'' its key advertises ``what I offer to a token looking for this,'' and its value is the information it contributes once attention focuses on it: a search query, an index entry, and a payload.

A token's contextual representation is then a weighted combination of every token's value vector, with weights determined by how well each token's key matches the query,
\begin{equation}
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V, \qquad \text{softmax}(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},
\label{eq:nlp-attention}
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ is the key dimension, included to keep dot products from growing too large as $d_k$ increases, and softmax\index{softmax} rescales raw scores into positive weights summing to one, so the combination of value vectors is an interpretable average rather than an arbitrary weighted sum.

This mechanism, applied across many layers and attention ``heads,'' is the core building block of BERT, GPT,\index{BERT} and every other modern transformer language model. Figure~\ref{fig:qkv_architecture} diagrams the three-projection, one-combination structure every attention layer repeats.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-self-attention}{Self-Attention for a Three-Token Sentence}\index{self-attention!three-token example}

Consider the toy sentence ``stocks rallied today,'' with $d_k = 2$. Rather than training projection matrices $W^Q$, $W^K$, $W^V$, this example hand-chooses the resulting keys and values, standing in for what a trained model would produce: $K_{\text{stocks}} = (1,0)$, $K_{\text{rallied}} = (0,1)$, $K_{\text{today}} = (1,1)$, and $V_{\text{stocks}} = (2,0)$, $V_{\text{rallied}} = (0,2)$, $V_{\text{today}} = (1,1)$. ``stocks'' and ``today'' share the same first key component, so a query matching on that component attends to both equally, the effect this example illustrates.

The representation of ``stocks'' uses its query $Q_{\text{stocks}} = (1,0)$. The raw scores $Q \cdot K_i / \sqrt{2}$ are $0.7071$ against ``stocks,'' $0$ against ``rallied,'' and $0.7071$ against ``today,'' since ``rallied''s key is orthogonal to the query. Softmax turns these into attention weights of $0.4011$, $0.1978$, and $0.4011$, which sum to $1$, and the weighted sum of value vectors is
\begin{equation*}
0.4011\,(2,0) + 0.1978\,(0,2) + 0.4011\,(1,1) = (1.2033,\ 0.7967).
\end{equation*}
Figure~\ref{fig:attention_weights} draws these weights, with edge thickness proportional to each token's contribution. ``Today,'' a different word, contributes exactly as much to the new representation of ``stocks'' as ``stocks'' itself, because both keys align with the query, while ``rallied'' contributes far less, its value $(0,2)$ pulled in only weakly.

\subsubsection*{Multi-Head Attention and Positional Encoding}\index{multi-head attention}\index{attention mechanism!multi-head}

A single attention calculation forces every token to distribute its focus by one notion of relevance, but relevance is not one-dimensional: local grammatical structure (which word modifies which noun) and long-range dependency (which earlier passage a later reference points back to) are different relationships, and one attention pattern cannot represent both well. Two refinements give the mechanism used in practice. First, a transformer layer computes several
attention calculations in parallel, called attention heads, each with its own learned $Q$, $K$, $V$ projections, and concatenates their outputs.
One head might attend mainly to nearby tokens (local syntax), another to the subject of a distant
verb (long-range dependencies, such as linking a hedge described early in a paragraph to a number reported much later),
and the model combines these perspectives. Second, the calculation above
treats the input as an unordered set: nothing in $Q \cdot K$ depends on position, so ``the bank raised rates'' and ``rates
raised the bank'' would receive identical weights given identical token embeddings. Positional encoding ($PE$)\index{positional encoding}\index{multi-head attention!positional encoding} fixes this by
adding a position-dependent vector to each embedding before attention, most commonly
$$PE_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)$$
and
$$PE_{(pos,2i+1)} = \cos\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d}}\right)$$
for position $pos$ and embedding dimension index $i$, so that word order survives into a mechanism that would otherwise discard it.

\subsection{Encoder-Only, Decoder-Only, and Encoder-Decoder Architectures}
\label{sec:transformer-architectures}

Self-attention is the building block of every transformer, but different financial NLP tasks arrange it differently, and the three resulting architecture families are not interchangeable. An encoder-only model\index{transformer architecture!encoder-only}
(BERT and its financial variant FinBERT are the standard examples) lets every token attend bidirectionally to every other token, the unmasked computation above, and is trained with a masked-language-modeling objective
(predicting a hidden word from its context in both directions). This makes encoder-only models well
 suited to classification and embedding tasks, such as the sentiment scoring of Section~\ref{sec:sentiment} and the document classification of
 Section~\ref{sec:naive-bayes-text}, where the full passage is available at once and the output is a label or vector,
 not new text. A decoder-only model (the GPT family\index{transformer architecture!decoder-only} and the LLMs of the rest of this chapter) restricts
 each token to attending to itself and earlier tokens, a causal mask, since it is trained\index{attention mechanism!causal mask} to predict each next token from what precedes
 it, the one-token-at-a-time process of Section~\ref{sec:llms-finance}. An encoder-decoder model (T5-style architectures)\index{transformer architecture!encoder-decoder}
 uses both: a bidirectional encoder reads the input, and a causal decoder generates the output while cross-attending
 to the encoder's output, a natural fit for input-to-output tasks such as translating a filing
 or condensing an earnings call into a summary.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-causal-mask}{How a Causal Mask Changes a Token's Representation}\index{causal mask}
Return to ``stocks rallied today'' and compute the representation of ``rallied'' with its query $Q_{\text{rallied}} = (0,1)$, first with full bidirectional attention (as an encoder-only model would) and then with a causal mask hiding the later token ``today'' (as a decoder-only model would). Bidirectionally, the raw scores $Q_{\text{rallied}} \cdot K_i/\sqrt{2}$ are $0$ against ``stocks,'' $0.7071$ against ``rallied,'' and $0.7071$ against ``today,'' giving softmax weights of $0.1978$, $0.4011$, and $0.4011$ and an output of
\begin{equation*}
0.1978\,(2,0) + 0.4011\,(0,2) + 0.4011\,(1,1) = (0.7967,\ 1.2033).
\end{equation*}
Under a causal mask, ``today'' is removed, leaving the scores against ``stocks'' ($0$) and ``rallied'' ($0.7071$); renormalizing these two via softmax gives weights of $0.3302$ and $0.6698$, and an output of
\begin{equation*}
0.3302\,(2,0) + 0.6698\,(0,2) = (0.6605,\ 1.3395).
\end{equation*}
The same word in the same sentence gets two representations, $(0.7967, 1.2033)$ versus $(0.6605, 1.3395)$, purely from architecture: an encoder-only model's ``rallied'' may reflect that ``today'' follows it, while a decoder-only model's, used to predict what comes \emph{after} ``rallied,'' cannot peek at ``today'' without trivializing its training objective.

So swapping a decoder-only LLM into a task an encoder-only model was designed for, or vice versa, is not a matter of using ``a big enough model'': the two produce different token representations by construction, not by training data.

\subsection{How These Models Are Trained, and Why They Eventually Need Retraining}
\label{sec:transformer-training}\index{large language model (LLM)!pretraining}

No one hand-writes the rule that tells a model which distant word a pronoun refers to, or which number a sentence's context makes plausible; the model must learn it, and the only training signal available at that scale is the text itself, with no human labels. None of the weight matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:qkv_architecture}, $W^Q$, $W^K$, $W^V$, or the many more in a transformer's feedforward and output layers, is hand-specified; each is learned by ordinary gradient descent (Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s Section~\chnum{ml}.2.3), applied to a self-supervised objective computed from raw\index{self-supervised learning}, unlabeled text at enormous scale.

An encoder-only model is pretrained by masking a random fraction of tokens and predicting each from its bidirectional context; a decoder-only model is pretrained on the single objective of predicting each next token\index{pretraining!next-token prediction} from everything before it, the causal-masked computation just carried out by hand, repeated across a very large corpus. No human labels the output token by token; the text supplies the signal, which is why these models can be pretrained on far more data than any supervised model in this book.

Pretraining is not, however, a one-time event. Financial language drifts\index{large language model (LLM)!retraining}: new instruments, regulations, tickers, and company names appear, established terms acquire new meanings (a ``pandemic bond'' meant nothing before 2020), and the pretraining vocabulary can gradually stop matching what a live system encounters, a text analogue of the concept drift of Chapter~\chnum{hft}'s Section~\chnum{hft}.6.2 and Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s population-stability index. Three responses are common, roughly in increasing order of cost: prompting or retrieval (Section~\ref{sec:rag}) supplies current information to a frozen model; parameter-efficient fine-tuning updates a small number\index{fine-tuning!parameter-efficient} of added parameters on a modest domain dataset; and continued pretraining resumes the self-supervised objective on fresh text, updating the weights more thoroughly at higher cost. The choice depends on whether the problem is a knowledge gap (a missing recent fact, best fixed by retrieval) or a shift in how language is used (better fixed by continued pretraining or fine-tuning), a diagnosis that requires the ongoing monitoring Chapter~\chnum{xai} established for every model.

\subsection{Large Language Models in Finance: Capabilities, Generative Modeling, and Limits}
\label{sec:llms-finance}\index{large language model (LLM)!capabilities}

Suppose an analyst is handed a hundred-page merger filing an hour before a decision, or a portfolio manager wants a plain-English answer to a question buried in it. None of this chapter's classification or scoring tools, built to output a single label or score, can do this. Chapter~\chnum{ml} noted that LLMs are far more elaborate examples of the generative principle behind Naive Bayes: both learn a probability distribution, Naive Bayes over word counts given a class, an LLM over the next token given all preceding tokens, and both can be used generatively. An LLM is, mechanically, a very large stack of the self-attention layers of Section~\ref{sec:attention-transformers}, trained on enormous volumes of text to predict the next token, then adapted (through instruction tuning and related techniques)\index{instruction tuning} to follow natural-language prompts.

In finance, this supports tasks well beyond classification and scoring: summarizing a long earnings call, extracting a table of figures from an unstructured filing, drafting a research note, or answering a question about a specific document. These tasks come with a different failure mode: hallucination\index{large language model (LLM)!hallucination}\index{hallucination}, fluent, confident text that is factually wrong, such as a number invented rather than retrieved, a misattributed filing detail, or a summary that omits a covenant violation buried in a footnote.

A classifier fails with a wrong label and a computable error rate; an LLM fails with a wrong but plausible answer\index{large language model (LLM)!limits}, harder to detect automatically and dangerous where one invented figure in a research note can be consequential. The next four sections develop the leading mitigation for hallucination (retrieval-augmented generation), how to instruct a generative model (prompting), a use of generative modeling that has nothing to do with text (synthetic data), and how an institution governs all of this.

\subsubsection*{Beyond Text: Code Generation and Multimodal Applications}

Two further capabilities go beyond text-in, text-out tasks. Code generation applies next-token prediction\index{code generation} to programming languages. An analyst can describe a calculation in plain English, for example ``load this CSV of daily returns and compute the annualized Sharpe ratio\index{Sharpe ratio} and maximum drawdown,'' and receive working Python code, extending the pandas and NumPy analysis this book has built since Chapter~\chnum{python}. The code still needs scrutiny before it runs against real portfolio data: a subtly wrong annualization factor or an off-by-one window is a bug, not a hallucination in the text sense, but no less consequential.

Multimodal models extend the architecture\index{multimodal model} to inputs beyond text, reading a scanned PDF page, a chart in an investor presentation, or a table image directly, without a separate, brittle optical-character-recognition step. This matters because much financial disclosure (annual report exhibits, scanned older filings, chart-heavy investor decks) is not available as clean machine-readable text.

\subsubsection*{Reasoning Models and Long Context Windows}

Two developments since 2024 have changed how these models are used. Reasoning models\index{reasoning model} are trained, largely by reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, such as mathematics and code, to write out a long chain of thought before answering~\parencite{deepseek2025r1}: the chain-of-thought prompting of Section~\ref{sec:prompting}, learned rather than requested. Accuracy then rises with test-time compute, the number of reasoning tokens spent per question, which turns cost and latency into a dial. Reconciling two differently defined metrics across a pair of filings justifies a long think; labeling a headline's sentiment does not. Reasoning tokens are usually billed as output tokens, the expensive side of Section~\ref{sec:api-vs-selfhost}'s cost formula. Reasoning lowers error rates without removing them, and a displayed trace is not guaranteed to be the computation that produced the answer, so a reviewer should treat it as evidence, not proof.

Context windows\index{context window}, the number of tokens a model can read at once, have grown from a few thousand to hundreds of thousands and, as of October 2026, a million or more for several models, enough to hold an entire 10-K. This changes what retrieval is for, not whether it is needed. A question about one filing can now often be answered by reading the whole filing, which removes the risk of retrieving the wrong passage. But a firm's corpus runs to millions of documents; every token in the prompt is paid for on every query; and models use long contexts unevenly: \textcite{liu2024lost} found accuracy highest when the relevant passage sits near the start or end of the context and markedly lower when it sits in the middle. Retrieval also keeps citations precise and lets access control be enforced document by document (Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance}).

\subsubsection*{Reading Loan Documents in Private Credit}\index{private credit}

One extraction task shows why reading, not writing, is the hard part. Private credit, loans made directly to mostly mid-sized companies by funds and other nonbank lenders rather than syndicated by banks, had grown to about \$2.1 trillion of assets and committed capital worldwide by 2023, roughly three-quarters of it in the United States, and the IMF noted signs of weaker underwriting and looser covenants as these lenders competed with banks for large deals \parencite{imf2024gfsr}. The loans rarely trade and are documented in bespoke agreements of a hundred pages or more, so a lender monitoring several hundred borrowers must answer the same questions from each agreement and quarterly compliance certificate: what is the maximum leverage, how is it measured, and how close is the borrower? These are the summarization and extraction tasks above, and the danger lies in the definitions\index{loan covenants}. Suppose a borrower has \$250 million of net debt, reported EBITDA of \$40 million, and a maintenance covenant capping total net leverage at $5.50$ times ``Consolidated EBITDA,'' which the agreement defines to include add-backs, here \$5 million of projected cost synergies and \$3 million of restructuring charges. With every add-back, leverage is $250/48 = 5.208$ times, a pass with $5.3\%$ of headroom, since the covenant needs only $250/5.50 = \$45.45$ million. If the definition caps add-backs at $15\%$ of EBITDA before them, the measure is $40 + 6 = 46$ and leverage $5.435$ times, still a pass but with $1.2\%$ of headroom. On reported EBITDA alone it is $250/40 = 6.250$ times, a breach. A model that summarizes the position as ``leverage of about 5.2 times against a 5.5 times covenant'' has answered from the wrong clause, and has done so fluently. The safe design is the one this chapter keeps arriving at: the model extracts the definition, the cap, and each add-back as structured fields, each tied to the clause it came from (the retrieval and citation of the next subsection); a calculator computes the ratio (the tool use of Section~\ref{sec:llm-agents}); and an analyst reviews every borrower near its limit.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}
\label{sec:rag}\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the most widely deployed mitigation for hallucination in finance. Rather than relying on facts an LLM absorbed in training, possibly outdated, generic, or never seen, a RAG system first retrieves the most relevant passages from a trusted, current collection (a bank's own filings, policy manuals, or licensed research), then conditions the answer on them.

This turns open-ended generation into something closer to open-book comprehension: the model answers from the retrieved text, and its output can be traced to a source passage, which is what an auditor or compliance reviewer needs. Retrieval applies Section~\ref{sec:embeddings}'s embedding geometry: embed the query and every candidate document in the same space, rank documents by cosine similarity to the query, and pass the top passages to the model.

Figure~\ref{fig:rag_pipeline} lays out the pipeline in two phases. Offline, the collection is split into chunks, tagged with metadata, embedded, and indexed. Online, each query is matched against the index, candidates are re-ranked, and the best few passages go into the prompt with the question; the answer cites those passages and is checked claim by claim before anyone sees it. Consider a knowledge base of four short passages about two companies, embedded in a hand-illustrative three-dimensional scheme whose dimensions loosely represent revenue, leverage, and other-company topics (Table~\ref{tab:rag_kb}).

\toprule
Document & Passage & Embedding \\
\midrule
Doc1 & ``Company X reported Q3 revenue of \$420 million, up 8\% year over year.'' & $(0.90, 0.10, 0.00)$ \\
Doc2 & ``Company X's debt-to-equity ratio increased to 1.8 in Q3.'' & $(0.10, 0.90, 0.00)$ \\
Doc3 & ``Company Y announced a new product launch in Q4.'' & $(0.00, 0.00, 0.90)$ \\

A user asks, ``What was Company X's revenue in Q3?'', embedded as $(0.95, 0.05, 0.00)$.

Computing cosine similarity between the query and each document gives
\begin{align*}
\cos(\text{query}, \text{Doc1}) &\approx 0.9983, \\
\cos(\text{query}, \text{Doc4}) &\approx 0.8008, \\
\cos(\text{query}, \text{Doc2}) &\approx 0.1625, \\
\cos(\text{query}, \text{Doc3}) &= 0.0000.
\end{align*}
Doc1 is the top match by a wide margin, and only Doc1 (or, at a higher retrieval count, Doc1 and Doc4) would be passed to the model, which answers from Doc1's text: ``Company X's Q3 revenue was \$420 million, up 8\% year over year,'' grounded in a verifiable source rather than invented from training data. Doc2 and Doc3, though they mention Company X or a business topic, rank far below Doc1 and Doc4, since neither answers a revenue question, the discrimination retrieval needs to make.

\subsubsection*{Chunking and Hybrid Retrieval}\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)!chunking}

A production system differs from this toy in two ways. First, documents must be split into chunks before embedding\index{chunking}, and chunk size is a trade-off: too small, and a figure is separated from the sentence saying which quarter it belongs to; too large, and one embedding averages over several topics and matches none well. Common practice is chunks of a few hundred tokens with some overlap, cut along the document's structure (sections together, tables whole) and tagged with metadata such as company, filing date, and section, so a query can be filtered to the right filing before similarity is computed. Second, dense embeddings encode what a passage is about and are weak at exact matches on the identifiers finance depends on: a ticker, a CUSIP, a clause number, ``Item 1A.'' Hybrid retrieval\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)!hybrid retrieval}\index{hybrid retrieval} therefore runs a lexical ranker, usually BM25\index{BM25}~\parencite{robertson2009probabilistic}, a refined descendant of Section~\ref{sec:bow-tfidf}'s TF-IDF, beside the dense retriever and merges the two ranked lists (for example by reciprocal rank fusion). The top few dozen candidates then go to a re-ranker, a model that reads the query and each passage together and scores relevance more accurately than either retriever, before the best handful reach the generator.

The companion notebook's optional ``at scale'' section repeats this retrieval with a real sentence-embedding model over a thirty-passage knowledge base, side by side with a TF-IDF ranking, and scores Section~\ref{sec:sentiment}'s two transcripts with a transformer classifier fine-tuned on financial news. It needs the extra packages listed in the repository's \texttt{requirements-dl.txt} and downloads the pretrained model weights on its first run.

\subsection{Evaluating a RAG System: Precision@k and a Faithfulness Score}
\label{sec:rag-evaluation}\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)!evaluation}

Retrieving \emph{a} document is not retrieving the \emph{right} one, and a production RAG system needs to check retrieval and generation separately, not only spot-check final answers. Precision@k measures retrieval alone\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)!precision at k}: what fraction of the top $k$ retrieved documents is relevant? For the revenue question, only Doc1 answers it; Doc4 (net margin) and Doc2 (leverage) concern Company X but do not answer \emph{this} question. Ranking by the cosine similarities already computed (Doc1, then Doc4, then Doc2, then Doc3),
\begin{equation*}
\text{Precision@1} = \frac{1}{1} = 100\%, \qquad \text{Precision@2} = \frac{1}{2} = 50\%, \qquad \text{Precision@3} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 33.3\%,
\end{equation*}
since only one document in each of the top 1, 2, and 3 is relevant. This is a quantitative reason to keep $k$ small and the similarity threshold strict: retrieving more documents mechanically dilutes precision, and every non-relevant passage passed to the model is another chance for it to draw on irrelevant context.

A second check addresses generation: given the right passage, did the answer stick to it? A faithfulness score compares\index{faithfulness!of RAG answers}\index{retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)!faithfulness} the answer's embedding to the source passage's, using the same cosine geometry as retrieval. The grounded answer, ``Company X's Q3 revenue was \$420 million, up 8\% year over year,'' embeds almost identically to Doc1, at $(0.89, 0.12, 0.00)$, giving
\begin{equation*}
\text{Faithfulness} = \cos\big((0.89, 0.12, 0.00),\ \text{Doc1}\big) \approx 0.9997,
\end{equation*}
close to a perfect match, since the answer restates Doc1. A hallucinated answer that invents a leverage claim absent from Doc1 embeds instead at $(0.30, 0.85, 0.00)$, closer to Doc2's topic than Doc1's:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Faithfulness} = \cos\big((0.30, 0.85, 0.00),\ \text{Doc1}\big) \approx 0.4349,
\end{equation*}
far below the grounded answer. A production system can flag any answer scoring below, say, $0.85$ against its source for rejection or human review. That makes the score a useful \emph{topical-drift} check: it catches an answer that has wandered to a different subject.

It does not catch the error that matters most in finance, a wrong number on the right topic. Suppose the model answers ``Company X's Q3 revenue was \$240 million, down 8\% year over year,'' transposing two digits and reversing the direction. The answer is about the same subject as Doc1, so in this topic-based embedding it sits where the grounded answer sits and scores the same $0.9997$, passing the $0.85$ threshold. Real embedding models behave the same way, because they encode what a sentence is about far more strongly than its digits. Even bag-of-words vectors built from the sentences (Section~\ref{sec:bow-tfidf}) give the wrong answer a cosine of $0.7333$ against Doc1, against $0.8667$ for the grounded one: lower, but nowhere near the leverage answer's $0.4349$, and a threshold strict enough to reject it would also reject correct answers phrased differently.

Faithfulness therefore needs a second, \emph{claim-level} check. Extract every verifiable claim from the answer (numbers, entities, dates, directions of change) and require each to appear in, or be computable from, the source. Here the check is mechanical: the grounded answer's three claims (420, 8, ``up'') are all in Doc1, while the wrong answer's (240, 8, ``down'') pass on only one of three, and it is rejected. Production systems go beyond exact matching by asking a natural-language-inference (entailment) model whether the source entails each claim, or a second LLM to judge each claim (``LLM-as-judge'')\index{LLM-as-judge}, and frameworks such as RAGAS package claim-level faithfulness scores with retrieval metrics like precision@k. Together, the two checks give the validation practice Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance} calls for, ``sample many outputs and check them against source documents for factual grounding,'' a computable form.

Finance-specific benchmarks test the whole pipeline.\index{large language model (LLM)!evaluation} FinanceBench\index{FinanceBench}~\parencite{islam2023financebench} poses 10,231 questions about public companies' filings, each with an answer and its supporting evidence passage (150 released openly); in its original 2023 evaluation, GPT-4-Turbo with retrieval answered 81\% of the sampled questions incorrectly or refused, a sobering baseline. Published scores age quickly and can leak into later models' training data\index{benchmark contamination}, so an institution should also keep a private question set from its own documents. When an LLM grades answers at that scale, the judge's biases need controlling. \textcite{zheng2023judging} document three\index{LLM-as-judge}: position bias (favoring the answer shown first), verbosity bias (favoring longer answers), and self-enhancement bias (favoring its own answers). The remedies are to swap the order and count only verdicts that survive the swap, to grade against the claim list or a reference answer rather than holistically, to use a judge from a different model family, and to calibrate the judge against a human-labeled sample, the validation against ground truth Chapter~\chnum{xai} requires of any model.

\subsection{Prompting Strategies for Financial Tasks}
\label{sec:prompting}\index{prompting}

How a task is phrased to a generative model, its prompt, materially affects output quality, and three strategies recur in finance. Zero-shot prompting\index{prompting!zero-shot}\index{zero-shot prompting} states the task: ``Classify the sentiment of the following earnings-call excerpt as positive, negative, or neutral: \emph{[excerpt]}.'' This works for common, well-defined tasks but can be inconsistent where domain conventions the model has not reliably learned apply, the finance-vocabulary issue of Section~\ref{sec:text-data}.

Few-shot prompting\index{prompting!few-shot}\index{few-shot prompting} puts a few worked examples in the prompt to show the labeling convention wanted: two or three (excerpt, label) pairs, perhaps from Section~\ref{sec:sentiment}'s transcripts, followed by a new excerpt to classify. This resembles Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s supervised learning, except the ``training'' happens within a single prompt, with no weight updates, and the ``training set'' can be two or three examples.

Chain-of-thought prompting\index{prompting!chain-of-thought}\index{chain-of-thought prompting} asks the model to reason step by step before answering, for example: ``First identify the reported revenue figure and the prior-year figure, then compute the percentage change, then state whether this represents an acceleration or deceleration relative to the previous quarter's growth rate, showing each step.'' For multi-step numerical tasks, such as computing a ratio from figures buried in a paragraph or reconciling two differently defined metrics, this measurably reduces errors compared with asking for the number directly, because it puts the intermediate steps in the output where they can be checked, much as this book shows every intermediate step of a calculation, a practice established in Chapter~\chnum{timeseries}.

\section{Generative AI in Practice}

Generation raises problems consumption does not, from synthetic data to governance to domain adaptation.

\subsection{Generative Models for Synthetic Financial Data}
\label{sec:generative-synthetic}\index{synthetic data}\index{generative AI!synthetic data}

A different application of generative modeling has nothing to do with text: generating synthetic numerical data (stress-test scenarios, order-book sequences, extra examples of a rare event class) that resembles real data closely enough to train or test other models. Two architectures dominate.

A generative adversarial network\index{generative adversarial network (GAN)} (GAN) pits two neural networks against each other: a generator $G$ maps random noise $z$, drawn from a simple fixed distribution $p_z$ such as a standard normal, to synthetic samples $G(z)$, and a discriminator $D$ tries to distinguish real samples $x$, drawn from the data distribution $p_{\text{data}}$ being imitated, from generated ones. They are trained jointly via
\begin{equation}
\min_G \max_D \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\log D(x)\right] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\!\left[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\right].
\label{eq:nlp-gan}
\end{equation}
Each term rewards $D$ for half its job: the first for assigning high probability to real samples, the second for assigning low probability to fakes. Only the second involves $G$, so the generator's objective is to drive $D(G(z))$ up while the discriminator pushes it down.
As training proceeds, each improves against the other, and at convergence the discriminator can no longer reliably tell generated samples from real ones. In finance, GANs have been used to augment a fraud model's minority class\index{synthetic data!GANs}, extending Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s resampling techniques such as SMOTE\index{SMOTE} with synthetic rather than interpolated examples, and to generate limit-order-book sequences for backtesting execution algorithms without exposing client order flow.

The companion notebook evaluates this minimax game on Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s fraud-scoring data: a fixed, illustrative discriminator $D(x) = \sigma(2.0x - 5.0)$ assigns transaction 1's fraud amount, standardized to $3.1$, a probability of being real of $D(x_{\text{real}}) = 0.7685$. (Chapter~\chnum{fraud} names this standardized feature with the conventional $z$ of a standard score; here $z$ is the generator's noise input, so the feature is called by its role.) An early-training generator, still producing samples near a typical legitimate transaction's scale, might output $G(z) = 1.0$, which the discriminator flags as almost certainly fake, $D(G(z)) = 0.0474$, a gap of $0.7211$ from $D(x_{\text{real}})$.

A later generator producing a fraud-scale sample, $G(z) = 2.9$, gets $D(G(z)) = 0.6900$, closing the gap to $0.0786$: the generator's loss, $-\log D(G(z))$, falls from $3.0486$ to $0.3711$, a numerical measure of how much harder it has become to catch.

GANs are also notoriously hard to train. Because generator and discriminator are optimized against each other rather than toward one shared objective, training can oscillate instead of converging, or fall into mode collapse\index{generative adversarial network (GAN)!mode collapse}\index{mode collapse}: the generator finds a few samples that reliably fool the discriminator and stops exploring the rest of the distribution, producing fraud examples or stress scenarios that are realistic but too uniform to augment a thin dataset. This instability, which not every generative architecture shares, is one reason diffusion models, described next, have become the more common choice for new synthetic-data applications despite being costlier to sample from.

Diffusion models take a different approach\index{diffusion model}\index{synthetic data!diffusion models}, motivated by physical diffusion: a forward process gradually adds noise to real data over many steps until it is indistinguishable from pure noise, $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$, where $x_0$ is the original data point, $\varepsilon$ is standard-normal noise, and $\alpha_t$ decreases from near 1 (step 0, almost no noise) toward 0 (fully noised) as $t$ increases. A neural network is trained to reverse this, removing noise one step at a time, so that running the learned reverse process from pure noise produces a new sample resembling the original distribution.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-diffusion-step}{One Forward Diffusion Step}\index{diffusion model!forward diffusion step}

Consider a toy stress-test scenario variable, a multiplier applied to a baseline loss, with baseline (undiffused) value $x_0 = 1.00$ (no stress) and a noise draw $\varepsilon = -0.50$. At an early diffusion step with
$\alpha_t = 0.80$,
$$x_t = \sqrt{0.80}\,(1.00) + \sqrt{0.20}\,(-0.50) = 0.6708.$$
At a later, noisier step with $\alpha_t = 0.55$,
$$x_t = \sqrt{0.55}\,(1.00) + \sqrt{0.45}\,(-0.50) = 0.4062.$$
As $\alpha_t$ falls from 0.80 to 0.55, the scenario value moves from 0.6708 down to 0.4062,
drifting from the baseline of 1.00 toward the noise draw of $-0.50$; continuing to $\alpha_t \to 0$
would carry $x_t$ to $\varepsilon$ itself, pure noise with no trace of the original scenario. The two coefficients are square roots chosen so that $\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\big)^2 + \big(\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\big)^2 = 1$: they hold the \emph{variance} of $x_t$ constant as noise replaces signal, and they are not a weighted average of $x_0$ and $\varepsilon$, since they sum to more than one. A same-signed noise draw can therefore push $x_t$ temporarily outside the range spanned by $x_0$ and $\varepsilon$ before it settles toward $\varepsilon$. A trained diffusion model learns
to run this in reverse, starting from noise and reconstructing a plausible, never-observed stress scenario that
Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s stress testing could use to probe losses beyond anything in the historical sample.

\subsection{Governance and Risk Management for Generative AI}
\label{sec:genai-governance}\index{generative AI!governance}\index{model risk management!for generative AI}

Chapter~\chnum{fraud} developed a three-lines-of-defense governance framework for fraud and compliance models; generative AI needs the same discipline, applied to risks deterministic models did not raise. Three deserve attention.

First, non-determinism: the same prompt submitted twice can produce two outputs, which breaks validation approaches that assume a fixed input gives a reproducible output, and pushes validation toward sampling many outputs. Second, prompt injection:\index{prompt injection} adversarial text in a retrieved document, an uploaded file, or ordinary user input can try to hijack the system, for example an instruction hidden in a retrieved filing telling the model to ignore its task, an attack surface with no counterpart in earlier chapters' classifiers. Third, data leakage: a RAG system with access to sensitive internal documents can, if not carefully scoped, surface information to a user who should not see it, an inadvertent access-control failure.

These risks change how the third line's independent validation, introduced in Chapter~\chnum{fraud}, operates. Rather than checking predictions against a fixed labeled test set, it must sample many outputs, check them against source documents for factual grounding (the check Section~\ref{sec:rag}'s retrieval supports), and test for prompt injection and data leakage, before deployment and on an ongoing basis as the model, document collection, or user population changes. Regulators have generally applied existing model risk management\index{model risk management} expectations rather than writing new rules, although the U.S. interagency guidance that replaced SR 11-7 in April 2026, SR 26-2, explicitly leaves\index{SR 26-2} generative and agentic AI outside its scope pending separate treatment \parencite{frb2026revised}. In practice a generative AI deployment is still expected to meet the same expectations, documented validation, ongoing monitoring, and a clear escalation path, though the tools for meeting them differ.

\section{Advanced Topics: Fine-Tuning, Agents, and Domain Adaptation}
\label{sec:advanced-nlp}

The base LLM of Section~\ref{sec:llms-finance}, trained only to predict the next token, is not yet the instruction-following assistant of Section~\ref{sec:rag} and this chapter's case vignette. Four further ideas close that gap.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback}
\label{sec:rlhf}

A model pretrained only to predict the next token is an unreliable assistant: asked a question, it may as easily continue writing in the same style as answer it, since nothing in next-token prediction teaches it to follow an instruction or to prefer a helpful, accurate response over a fluent but unhelpful one. After pretraining, a model is typically trained in two further stages: supervised fine-tuning on curated (prompt, ideal response) pairs,
teaching it to follow instructions, and reinforcement learning from human feedback\index{reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)} (RLHF), which uses human preference judgments between pairs of candidate outputs to train a reward model\index{reward model}\index{reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)!reward model}, then adjusts the LLM
to produce outputs that reward model scores highly. The reward model itself is typically trained as a preference classifier: given two candidate
outputs with reward scores $r_A$ and $r_B$, the Bradley-Terry model\index{Bradley-Terry model} estimates the probability that a human would prefer output $A$ as
\begin{equation}
\Pr(A \succ B) = \text{sigmoid}(r_A - r_B) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(r_A - r_B)}}.
\label{eq:nlp-reward-model}
\end{equation}

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-reward-preference}{Reward-Model Preference Probability}\index{reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)!preference probability}
Suppose a reward model scores a source-accurate earnings-call summary at $r_A = 2.1$ and a fluent but subtly inaccurate one at $r_B = 0.8$. The preference probability is $\text{sigmoid}(2.1 - 0.8) = \text{sigmoid}(1.3) \approx 0.7858$: the reward model favors the accurate summary about 78.6\% of the time, and RLHF nudges the LLM's outputs toward what the reward model scores highly, in aggregate across many such comparisons.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)}\index{fine-tuning!parameter-efficient}\index{Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)}
\label{sec:lora}

RLHF and supervised fine-tuning describe \emph{what} a model is trained to do after pretraining; a practical question is \emph{how}. Updating every weight, full fine-tuning, requires storing\index{fine-tuning!full} a second full copy of the parameters (the update) beside the original, and repeating this for every task or client is prohibitively expensive at the scale of a modern LLM.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is the most widely used solution\index{fine-tuning!LoRA}: instead of learning a dense update $\Delta W$ for a $d\times d$ weight matrix, it constrains the update to a low-rank decomposition, $\Delta W = BA$, where $B$ is $d\times r$, $A$ is $r\times d$, and the rank $r$ is far smaller than $d$. Only $B$ and $A$ are trained; $W$ is frozen, and the adapted forward pass uses $W + BA$ in place of $W$.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-lora}{Parameter Reduction from Low-Rank Adaptation}\index{Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)!parameter reduction}
Consider a single $d=4{,}096$ weight matrix, a realistic dimension for one projection inside a modern LLM. Full fine-tuning of it requires learning all $d^2 = 4{,}096^2 = 16{,}777{,}216$ entries of $\Delta W$. Using LoRA with rank $r=8$, the trainable parameters are instead only those of $B$ ($d \times r$) and $A$ ($r \times d$),
\begin{equation*}
2 \times d \times r = 2 \times 4{,}096 \times 8 = 65{,}536,
\end{equation*}
a reduction factor of $16{,}777{,}216 / 65{,}536 = 256\times$ for this matrix, with a comparable reduction for every matrix LoRA is attached to.

This is why LoRA has become the default way to adapt a large model to a bank's internal document style or an asset class's terminology. It avoids both the storage cost of a duplicate model per use case and the compute cost of updating billions of parameters. The frozen base model, possibly quantized as described below, can be shared across many clients or tasks, with only a small task-specific $B$ and $A$ pair, tens of thousands of parameters rather than billions, swapped in per use case.

\subsection{LLM Agents and Tool Use}
\label{sec:llm-agents}\index{LLM agents}

Computing and cross-checking a company's P/E ratio requires looking up its price, pulling its latest earnings from a filing, dividing, and comparing against a sector benchmark, a sequence no single LLM call, which produces text in one pass, can carry out. An agent extends a single LLM call into a multi-step loop\index{LLM agents!tool use}: the model can call an external tool (a retrieval system, a calculator, a database query, or a trading API), observe the result, and choose a next action, repeating until it produces a final answer. This composes Section~\ref{sec:rag}'s retrieval with ordinary computation.

In practice the loop runs on function calling\index{LLM agents!function calling}\index{function calling}, also called tool calling. The developer describes each tool by a name, a plain-language description, and a JSON schema for its arguments; when the model wants a tool, it emits a structured call, in effect \texttt{calculator(57.87/3.15)}, and the application checks the call against the schema, runs it, and returns the result as a new message. The same machinery provides structured outputs: constraining a model to produce JSON conforming to a schema, which turns ``extract the figures from this filing'' into typed fields a downstream system can validate rather than prose it must parse. The Model Context Protocol\index{Model Context Protocol (MCP)} (MCP), an open standard introduced by Anthropic in November 2024 and transferred in December 2025 to the Agentic AI Foundation, a Linux Foundation project~\parencite{anthropic2024mcp,linuxfoundation2025aaif}, standardizes how tools and data sources are offered to models, so that one connector to a market-data service or a document store can serve any compliant application.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-agent-pe}{A Three-Step Agent Computing and Cross-Checking a P/E Ratio}\index{LLM agents!P/E example}\index{price-to-earnings ratio (P/E)}
Suppose an agent is asked, ``What is Company X's price-to-earnings ratio using its Q3 diluted EPS and quarter-end share price?'' The agent first calls a retrieval tool (Section~\ref{sec:rag}'s mechanism), retrieving two facts: diluted EPS of \$3.15 and a quarter-end closing price of \$57.87. It then calls a calculator tool rather than doing the arithmetic itself, computing $\text{P/E} = 57.87 / 3.15 \approx 18.37$. This removes a whole category of arithmetic-hallucination risk: a calculator's division is exact by construction, while an LLM can make an arithmetic error despite retrieving correct inputs.

A more robust agent takes a third step, calling an independent tool to cross-check the result before presenting it. Suppose it queries a market-data vendor's reported P/E for Company X and retrieves $19.10$. Compared with its own value,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Discrepancy} = \frac{19.10 - 18.37}{18.37} \approx 3.97\%,
\end{equation*}
exceeds a pre-set tolerance of, say, $2\%$, so rather than presenting either number as final, the agent flags the disagreement, reporting both figures with a note that the gap may reflect a definitional or timing difference (the vendor may use trailing rather than diluted EPS, or a different price snapshot) worth investigating before either is used in a client-facing report.

The cross-check costs one tool call but turns a possibly wrong number into either a validated one or a flagged discrepancy, the independent-verification discipline Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s model-validation framework requires of a classical risk model.

An agent that can act also inherits Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance}'s risks in sharper form, because a successful prompt injection\index{prompt injection} now triggers tool calls rather than bad text. Indirect prompt injection, instructions planted in a web page, an email, or a retrieved filing that the agent reads as data, was demonstrated against deployed applications by \textcite{greshake2023not}, and prompt injection heads the OWASP Foundation's 2025 list of risks for LLM applications, with ``excessive agency'' also on it~\parencite{owasp2025llm}. No model-level defense is yet reliable, so the controls are architectural: least-privilege permissions (a research agent gets read-only data tools, and no trading or payment tool); every tool result and retrieved document treated as untrusted data, never as instructions; human approval before any irreversible or external action, such as sending an email, placing an order, or moving money; hard limits on spending and on the number of calls; and a complete log of every tool call for audit, the agent-level counterpart of Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s three lines of defense.

\subsection{Agentic AI in Finance: Planning, Memory, Evaluation, and Failure}
\label{sec:agentic-ai}
\index{agentic AI}

The P/E agent took three steps, each checkable by eye. The systems banks began piloting in 2025 and 2026 take tens of steps across several internal systems (refreshing a client's know-your-customer file, reconciling a failed trade, assembling a credit memo), and at that length the questions change. How does the agent decide what to do next? What may it touch? What does it remember between steps and tasks? How is it evaluated, when the output is a sequence of actions? And how does it fail? This subsection takes each in turn; its worked example shows why the last dominates: reliability that looks excellent per step becomes mediocre per task.

\textbf{The reason-act-observe loop.} Most agents run some version of the ReAct\index{LLM agents!ReAct}\index{ReAct} pattern of \textcite{yao2023react}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:agent_loop}. At each step the model writes a short reasoning trace (what it knows, what it still needs), emits one action, a structured tool call, and receives the tool's output as an observation appended to its context. The loop ends when the model gives a final answer, escalates to a human, or exhausts a step or spending budget. Two planning styles sit on top of this loop.\index{agentic AI!planning} In an interleaved style the agent decides one step at a time, which adapts to surprises but can wander; in a plan-then-execute style it first writes the whole plan (``retrieve the client's ownership records, screen each beneficial owner against the sanctions list, compare against the file on record, draft the exceptions''), executes it, and replans only when a step fails, which a reviewer can read in advance and check afterward. Regulated workflows tend to prefer the second for that reason.

\textbf{Permissions and sandboxing.}\index{agentic AI!governance} The permission check in Figure~\ref{fig:agent_loop} is where least privilege becomes engineering. Tools are sorted by consequence: read-only tools (a filing search, a market-data lookup) run freely; write tools whose effects can be undone (drafting a document, opening a ticket) run with logging; and tools whose effects cannot be undone or leave the firm (messaging a client, releasing a payment, submitting an order) run only after a named human approves that specific call, with its arguments, not the task in general. Code the agent writes runs in a sandbox, an isolated container with no network access or credentials, so a wrong or injected script damages nothing outside it. And the agent acts under its own service identity, never a borrowed human login, so every action is attributable and its entitlements reviewable like anyone else's; BNY, for example, gives each of its more than one hundred agent ``digital employees'' its own login credentials \parencite{axios2025bny}.

\textbf{Memory.}\index{agentic AI!memory} An agent has two kinds. Short-term memory is the context window, the running transcript of thoughts, actions, and observations, which is finite and degrades before it is full: models use information at the start and end of a long context more reliably than in the middle \parencite{liu2024lost}, so long tasks summarize old steps. Long-term memory is an external store, typically a vector database searched\index{vector database} with Section~\ref{sec:rag}'s retrieval, holding past interactions, client preferences, or lessons from earlier tasks. That is where finance-specific risks collect. A planted instruction written into memory persists into every later task that retrieves it, turning a one-time prompt injection into a standing one; a stored fact goes stale (a client's address, a counterparty's rating) and is retrieved with the same confidence as a fresh one; and memory shared across clients or desks can carry material nonpublic information across the information barriers a bank must maintain. Memory therefore needs the same retention schedule, access control, and periodic review as any other client record.

\textbf{Evaluating agents.}\index{agentic AI!evaluation}\index{agent evaluation} An agent is evaluated on a suite of realistic tasks, with two kinds of measurement. The first is outcome: did the task end in the correct state? The $\tau$-bench benchmark \parencite{yao2024taubench}, for instance, compares the database at the end of each simulated customer-service conversation with the annotated goal state, rather than trusting the agent's report, and asks whether the agent succeeds on all of $k$ repeated attempts, its pass$^k$ reliability; when it was published in June 2024, even the strongest function-calling agents solved fewer than half the tasks, and their pass$^8$ in the retail domain was below 25\%. The second is trajectory review, reading the steps themselves, because a correct end state reached by a wrong path (a forbidden data source consulted, an approval skipped, a calculation done by the model instead of the calculator) is a failure outcome scoring cannot see. In practice a firm reports task success rate, the rate and appropriateness of escalations, cost and steps per task, and the findings of humans reviewing a sample of trajectories, the agent-level version of Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s held-out evaluation.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-agent-reliability}{How Per-Step Reliability Compounds}\index{LLM agents!per-step reliability}
Suppose a task requires $k$ steps, each completed correctly with probability $p$, and, as a first approximation, that errors at different steps are independent. The task succeeds only if every step does:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{task succeeds}) = p^{k}.
\label{eq:nlp-agent-success}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:agent_reliability} evaluates this for two per-step reliabilities. An agent that is right 95\% of the time at each step completes a ten-step task correctly only $0.95^{10} = 59.87\%$ of the time, and a twenty-step task $35.85\%$ of the time. Even at 99\% per step, one task in five fails at twenty steps. Inverting the equation shows how demanding a task-level target is: a 95\% task success rate requires $p = 0.95^{1/k}$ per step, which is $0.98979$ at five steps, $0.99488$ at ten, and $0.99744$ at twenty.

Now add a verification step at the end of each task, an independent check of the kind the P/E agent ran, which catches a fraction $c$ of the tasks that went wrong and also, occasionally, flags a task that went right, at a false-alarm rate $f$. Every flag goes to a human reviewer. Over $N$ tasks the expected review load $R$ and the expected number of silent failures $S$, wrong outputs that pass the verifier unflagged, are
\begin{equation}
R = N\left[c\,(1-p^{k}) + f\,p^{k}\right], \qquad S = N\,(1-c)\,(1-p^{k}).
\label{eq:nlp-agent-review}
\end{equation}
Take $N = 1{,}000$ ten-step tasks, a verifier that catches $c = 80\%$ of failures, and a false-alarm rate of $f = 2\%$. At $p = 0.95$, $401.3$ of the tasks contain an error; the verifier flags $0.80 \times 401.3 = 321.0$ of them and $0.02 \times 598.7 = 12.0$ good tasks besides, so humans review $333.0$ tasks, a third of the volume, and $0.20 \times 401.3 = 80.3$ wrong outputs still go through unflagged. At $p = 0.99$, only $95.6$ tasks fail; humans review $76.5 + 18.1 = 94.6$ tasks, under a tenth of the volume, and $19.1$ errors go through silently. Without a verifier, all the failures, $401.3$ or $95.6$, would be silent.

Three lessons follow. First, per-step reliability is the economic lever: moving from 95\% to 99\% per step cuts the review load by a factor of about $3.5$ and silent failures by about $4.2$, which decides whether an agent saves analyst time or merely moves it into review. Second, a verifier converts silent failures into review work but never removes them; the residual $S$ is what the firm accepts, and $c$ must be estimated on tasks with known errors, not assumed. Third, as the agent improves, more of what the verifier flags is false alarm: the flags' precision, $321.0/333.0 = 96.4\%$ at $p=0.95$, falls to $76.5/94.6 = 80.9\%$ at $p=0.99$, because the same 2\% false-alarm rate applies to many more good tasks, the base-rate effect of Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s Bayes' theorem example. Independence is the assumption to doubt. Errors from one shared cause, a misread instruction or a stale fact in memory, cluster in the same tasks, which leaves more tasks clean than $p^k$ predicts but makes those errors systematic; and a verifier built on the agent's own model likely shares its blind spots, so its true $c$ on those errors is below its average.

\textbf{Failure modes.}\index{agentic AI!failure modes} The worked example is the arithmetic of the most basic failure, small errors compounding over many steps. The others are behavioral. An agent can loop, retrying a failing call or re-planning without progress until a budget stops it, which is why step and spending limits are design requirements. It can call a tool with a hallucinated argument, a plausible but wrong account number or ticker, which a schema check catches only if the value is malformed. It can pursue a proxy for the goal, closing reconciliation breaks by booking offsetting entries rather than finding their causes, the reward hacking Chapter~\chnum{rl} warns about. It can report success on a task it only partly completed, which is why evaluation checks the end state, not the agent's account. It can escalate too often to be useful or too rarely to be safe. And it remains exposed to prompt injection, now with memory to make it persistent. Industry forecasters expected many projects to fail on these grounds: in June 2025 Gartner predicted that over 40\% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls \parencite{gartner2025agentic}.

\textbf{What banks are doing.} As of October 2026, the public record shows banks moving from pilots to bounded deployments with three common features: the work is internal, middle- and back-office rather than client-facing; the agent drafts or prepares rather than decides; and a named human remains accountable. Citi began piloting agentic capabilities in its in-house Stylus Workspaces platform for 5,000 employees in September 2025, letting one prompt run a multi-step task such as researching a client and compiling a profile from public and internal sources \parencite{torres2025citi}. BNY described more than one hundred agent ``digital employees'' working alongside staff by October 2025, with tasks such as validating payment instructions \parencite{axios2025bny}. In February 2026 Goldman Sachs said it had spent six months working with embedded Anthropic engineers to build agents for trade and transaction accounting and for client vetting and onboarding, the know-your-customer work Chapter~\chnum{fraud} described, and that the effort was still at an early stage \parencite{cnbc2026goldman}. In August 2026 DBS extended an agentic corporate-credit tool from a 150-person pilot to about 1,500 bankers: specialized agents handle more than 70 tasks to produce a review-ready first draft of a credit memo from annual reports, industry research, and internal records, while relationship managers and credit risk managers remain responsible for the final memo; the bank's stated aim is to cut by at least 30\% the time spent on credit memos and related work, which it says can take up to 40\% of a relationship manager's time \parencite{dbs2026credit}. These are the firms' own descriptions and targets, not independently measured results. Each applies this subsection's arithmetic: a credit memo assembled from dozens of retrieval and drafting steps is a long-$k$ task, and the human sign-off is the verifier. How supervisors expect such systems to be governed is the subject of Chapter~\chnum{xai}.

\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts: Scaling Without Proportional Compute}
\label{sec:mixture-of-experts}

Making a language model more capable usually means making it larger, but a larger dense feedforward network makes every token more expensive, whether or not its context needed the full capacity. A mixture-of-experts\index{mixture of experts} (MoE) architecture replaces the large feedforward network in each transformer layer with several smaller ``expert'' networks and a router that sends each token to a handful of them, so capacity grows without a proportional increase in compute per token.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-moe-sparsity}{Sparse Activation Fraction}\index{mixture of experts!sparse activation}
Consider a MoE layer with 8 experts of 6 billion parameters each (48 billion total expert parameters) that routes each token to its top 2 experts. Each token activates $2 \times 6 = 12$ billion of the 48 billion expert parameters, an active fraction of $12/48 = 0.25$, or 25\%: the capacity of a 48-billion-parameter layer at the per-token cost of a 12-billion-parameter one.

\subsection{Domain-Adapted Financial Language Models: BloombergGPT}\index{BloombergGPT}
\label{sec:bloomberggpt}

Section~\ref{sec:sentiment}'s Loughran-McDonald dictionary made a lexicon-based version of a broader point: tools built on general-purpose text underperform on financial language. BloombergGPT, announced by Bloomberg in 2023, applies the principle to a full-scale LLM\index{domain adaptation}: a 50-billion-parameter model trained from scratch on a mixed corpus of over 700 billion tokens, roughly 363 billion of Bloomberg's own financial data (news, filings, and other proprietary text spanning decades) and roughly 345 billion of general-purpose text, with 569 billion tokens sampled for training. It outperformed similarly sized general models on financial benchmarks (sentiment analysis, financial named-entity recognition\index{named entity recognition}, and financial question answering) while remaining competitive on general benchmarks, confirming at LLM scale the domain-specificity argument of Section~\ref{sec:text-data}.

The later evidence is more mixed. Within months, \textcite{li2023chatgpt} found that general-purpose GPT-4, with no finance-specific pretraining, could outperform BloombergGPT on financial text tasks, although fine-tuned smaller models still beat it at structured extraction such as named-entity and relation recognition. Practice has broadly followed: as of October 2026, a general frontier model with retrieval over the institution's own documents is a common starting point, and domain-specific training tends to earn its cost for narrow, high-volume tasks, for data that cannot leave the firm, or for small models that must run in-house. The domain-specificity argument survives; what has changed is where domain knowledge is most cheaply supplied, at retrieval and fine-tuning time rather than by pretraining from scratch.

\subsection{Quantization: Deploying a Large Model at Lower Cost}
\label{sec:quantization}\index{quantization}

An institution that wants to run a model like BloombergGPT on its own infrastructure rather than send every query to an external API, a real consideration given Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance}'s data-leakage concern, faces a direct memory and compute cost. Quantization reduces it by storing each parameter with fewer bits than were used in training, trading a little accuracy for a large reduction in memory.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{nlp-quantization}{Memory Footprint Under Quantization}\index{quantization!memory footprint}
A 50-billion-parameter model held at 16-bit precision (2 bytes per parameter) requires $50\times10^9 \times 2 = 100\times10^9$ bytes, 100 gigabytes, just to store its weights, before activations or a long conversation's key-value cache. Quantizing to 8-bit precision (1 byte per parameter) halves this to 50 gigabytes, and 4-bit precision (0.5 bytes per parameter) brings it to 25 gigabytes, a fourfold reduction from 16-bit. This trade-off, some accuracy lost for a model that fits on cheaper hardware and answers faster, often decides whether an institution can run a domain-adapted model in-house at all.

\weheading{\subsection}{nlp-api-vs-selfhost}{API Cost versus Self-Hosting at Scale}\index{self-hosting versus API!cost at scale}
\label{sec:api-vs-selfhost}
Quantization's payoff is easiest to see by pricing the alternative, framed as a break-even volume because API prices move far faster than this book's print cycle. The two cost structures differ in kind. An API bills per token\index{large language model (LLM)!cost}, so its cost is purely variable, and output tokens cost more than input tokens, typically four to six times as much, since generating a token takes more compute than reading one. Self-hosting is mostly a fixed cost: suppose the 4-bit-quantized 50-billion-parameter model runs on one dedicated cloud GPU node reserved around the clock at \$20 an hour, or \$480 a day (\$14,400 a month), whether it is busy or idle, with capacity for $4{,}000$ queries an hour, $96{,}000$ a day.

Let a query carry $n_{\text{in}}$ input tokens (the prompt plus any retrieved context) and $n_{\text{out}}$ output tokens, with API prices $p_{\text{in}}$ and $p_{\text{out}}$ per million tokens. Self-hosting wins once daily query volume $Q$ exceeds
\begin{equation*}
Q^{*} = \frac{\$480}{(n_{\text{in}}\,p_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}\,p_{\text{out}})/10^{6}}.
\end{equation*}
Take an institution's financial-research assistant handling $40{,}000$ queries a day at $800$ input and $200$ output tokens each. The prices are the parameter that dates. At the 2023 list price of the original GPT-4, \$30 per million input tokens and \$60 per million output tokens, a query cost \$0.036, the API bill came to \$1,440 a day, three times the self-hosted node, and the break-even was only $13{,}333$ queries a day. As of October 2026, published list prices for mainstream models span roughly \$0.05 to \$10 per million input tokens and \$0.50 to \$50 per million output tokens, from the smallest models to the frontier ones, with premium reasoning tiers priced higher still. At an illustrative frontier-tier price of \$5 in and \$25 out, a query costs \$0.009, the same workload costs \$360 a day through the API, cheaper than the \$480 node, and self-hosting wins only above $53{,}333$ queries a day. At an illustrative mid-tier price of \$2 in and \$10 out, the break-even rises to $133{,}333$ queries a day, more than the node can serve, and at a small model's \$0.10 in and \$0.50 out, about \$7 a day buys the whole workload. Input and output asymmetry matters as much as the headline rate: tilt the same $1{,}000$ tokens toward long generated answers, $200$ in and $800$ out, and the frontier-tier cost per query rises to \$0.021, cutting the break-even to $22{,}857$ queries a day.

What survives price changes is the structure, not any single number. Falling per-token prices have pushed the break-even volume up roughly fourfold or more since 2023, so a pure cost case for self-hosting now needs very high volume, an output-heavy workload, or a premium model with a high API price. The memory-footprint calculation still makes self-hosting feasible at all: a model that fits on one GPU only at 4-bit precision keeps the fixed cost at one node rather than a multi-GPU cluster. And cost is rarely the whole decision: Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance}'s data-leakage concern can favor self-hosting even below the break-even, because every query stays inside the institution's infrastructure.

\vignetteheading{\section}{morgan-stanley}{AI @ Morgan Stanley}\index{Morgan Stanley}

In September 2023, Morgan Stanley announced that AI @ Morgan Stanley Assistant, built together with OpenAI on GPT-4\index{OpenAI}, was fully live for its wealth management financial advisors and support staff.\index{Morgan Stanley!AI assistant (AI at Morgan Stanley)} In this chapter's terms the assistant is a retrieval-augmented generation system: it gives advisors natural-language access to the firm's ``intellectual capital,'' roughly 100,000 research reports and internal documents, retrieving the passages relevant to a question and generating an answer grounded in them, the mechanism Section~\ref{sec:rag} worked through on four toy documents. Morgan Stanley later reported over 98\% adoption among advisor teams, and that the share of research content advisors could effectively find and use rose from roughly 20\% to roughly 80\%, a direct measure of the retrieval problem at large scale.

The firm then applied the same models to a structurally different task: AI @ Morgan Stanley Debrief, which uses OpenAI's Whisper speech-to-text model with GPT-4 to turn a client meeting recording, made only with the client's consent, into a structured summary, client notes filed automatically into the customer relationship management system, and a draft follow-up email an advisor reviews and sends. This is the summarization capability of Section~\ref{sec:llms-finance}, deployed on sensitive input (a recorded client conversation) rather than a public filing, which is why consent, access control, and human review before anything reaches a client are first-class design requirements, the discipline Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance} argued a deployment needs from the outset.

A third tool, AskResearchGPT, extended the same retrieval-augmented pattern into the firm's institutional research business: a RAG system validated for one internal use tends to generalize to others faster than building anew. The case is instructive because its two headline results, near-total adoption and a fourfold increase in effective document accessibility, came not from a more powerful model than other firms could license, but from the unglamorous work this chapter has emphasized: a well-curated retrieval corpus, retrieval grounded in the cosine-similarity geometry of Section~\ref{sec:embeddings}, and a governance structure (consent for Debrief's recordings, human review of drafted outputs) that made deployment at scale defensible.

\section{Challenges in Natural Language Processing in Finance}

Several challenges recur across the techniques this chapter developed, and many connect to themes from earlier chapters.

\textbf{Domain-specific vocabulary defeats generic tools.}\index{natural language processing (NLP)!challenges} Lexicons and language models trained on general text misread financial language (Section~\ref{sec:text-data}), which is why finance-specific resources like the Loughran-McDonald dictionary exist and why even a large general model benefits from finance-specific fine-tuning or careful prompting.

\textbf{A weak signal is still noisy, not deterministic.} Section~\ref{sec:event-driven-signal}'s signal beat buy-and-hold on mean return and Sharpe-like ratio while still being wrong on two of eight events: an NLP-derived signal is a probabilistic input to a strategy, not a certainty, as Chapter~\chnum{ml} emphasized about model evaluation.

\textbf{Hallucination is a different failure mode from misclassification.} A classifier's error rate is computable from a confusion matrix (Section~\ref{sec:naive-bayes-text}); an LLM's fluent, wrong answers (Section~\ref{sec:llms-finance}) have no equally clean error rate, since detecting a hallucination often requires the expertise the tool was meant to save. Retrieval-augmented generation (Section~\ref{sec:rag}) mitigates this without eliminating it: a model can still misread or misquote a correctly retrieved passage.

\textbf{Generative AI introduces risks classical models never raised.} Non-deterministic outputs, prompt injection, and data leakage (Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance}) have no counterpart in the deterministic models of Chapters~\chnum{ml} through~\chnum{fraud}, and require validation by statistical sampling of outputs rather than checking a single fixed prediction.

\textbf{Explainability and audit requirements constrain deployment.} As Chapter~\chnum{xai} develops, a trading decision or disclosure classification influenced by an NLP model may need to be explained after the fact, which is harder for the attention-based architectures of Section~\ref{sec:attention-transformers} than for the inspectable probabilities of Section~\ref{sec:naive-bayes-text}'s Naive Bayes classifier.

\textbf{Data licensing and evaluation are harder than they look.} High-quality financial text, especially real-time news and licensed feeds, is expensive and contractually restricted, and evaluating an NLP system is harder than evaluating a numerical model, since two humans often disagree about the ``correct'' sentiment or summary of a passage, so even the ground truth is somewhat subjective.

\notocsection{Key Takeaways}

Financial text must be converted into numerical features before this book's earlier tools apply: bag-of-words and TF-IDF (Section~\ref{sec:bow-tfidf}) give sparse count vectors, the Loughran-McDonald lexicon (Section~\ref{sec:sentiment}) gives a finance-specific sentiment score, and word embeddings (Section~\ref{sec:embeddings}) give dense vectors that capture semantic similarity geometrically.

A lexicon-based sentiment score is a useful but noisy trading signal: Section~\ref{sec:event-driven-signal}'s 75\% hit rate still beat buy-and-hold on average return and consistency, and generating the signal is only half the task Chapter~\chnum{algotrading} develops. It should be judged by an event study, on abnormal rather than raw returns and pooled over many events, since the market explained more than half of one event's reaction and a single event's cumulative abnormal return was indistinguishable from noise. Naive Bayes applies directly to document classification (Section~\ref{sec:naive-bayes-text}), including ambiguous cases where its independence assumption shows most.

Self-attention (Section~\ref{sec:attention-transformers}) lets transformer models, including the LLMs of Section~\ref{sec:llms-finance}, build context-dependent representations of text, and their generative capability supports summarization and extraction well beyond classification, at the cost of a new failure mode, hallucination, that a computable error rate cannot fully capture. Retrieval-augmented generation (Section~\ref{sec:rag}) is the leading mitigation, grounding answers in verifiable source passages, while prompting strategy (Section~\ref{sec:prompting}), zero-shot, few-shot, or chain-of-thought, shapes how
reliably a model performs a task without changing the model.

Generative modeling extends beyond text: GANs and
diffusion models (Section~\ref{sec:generative-synthetic}) generate synthetic numerical data, minority-class fraud examples or
stress scenarios beyond the historical sample, by fooling an adversarial discriminator or reversing a noising process.
All of this requires governance (Section~\ref{sec:genai-governance}) that extends Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s three lines of defense to risks,
non-determinism, prompt injection, data leakage, that deterministic models never presented.

In Section~\ref{sec:advanced-nlp}, RLHF's reward modeling turns human preferences into a trainable signal, and agents compose retrieval with computation through function calling
(delegating arithmetic to a calculator tool), with least-privilege permissions and human approval as safety controls. Over a long task, reliability compounds as $p^k$ (Section~\ref{sec:agentic-ai}), so an agent right 95\% of the time per step completes a ten-step task only about 60\% of the time, and per-step reliability, not headline capability, sets how much human review an agent saves or creates. Mixture-of-experts architectures decouple model
capacity from per-token compute, and BloombergGPT carries the domain-specificity argument to LLM scale, although general frontier models with retrieval have since often matched it. Finally, readability metrics like the Gunning Fog index (Section~\ref{sec:disclosure-analysis}) turn a regulatory concern, whether disclosure
  is understandable, into an automatable measurement.
