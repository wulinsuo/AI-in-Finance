\chapter{Reinforcement Learning in Finance}

\notocsection{Introduction}

Every model built so far in this book, however sophisticated, has answered a single question once. Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s classifiers predict
a label from a feature vector; Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s credit models predict a default probability; Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s
Almgren-Chriss\index{Almgren-Chriss model} trajectory prescribes an entire trading schedule, but it does so by solving one
 optimization problem in advance and then executing the resulting plan. None of these approaches change their answer in
 response to how the world responds to the decisions already made.

A portfolio manager, a market maker, and a trading desk do not operate this way: they observe an outcome, update their beliefs,
and choose their next action accordingly, over and over, for as long as the position stays open. Reinforcement
learning\index{reinforcement learning} is the branch of machine learning built for this setting,
sequential decision-making under uncertainty, where an agent's own actions influence the future states it will face,
and where the right action today depends on the consequences it sets up for tomorrow, not only on today's immediate reward.

This chapter formalizes that setting, the Markov decision process\index{Markov decision process (MDP)}, and develops the two families of algorithms built to solve it: value-based methods, culminating in Q-learning\index{Q-learning} and its neural-network extension, and policy-based methods, culminating in the policy-gradient\index{policy gradient} algorithms that power most modern applications. It builds on machinery this book has already developed: Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s binomial-tree early-exercise decision, Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s portfolio rebalancing problem, and Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s optimal-execution and market-making models are all special cases of the general sequential-decision problem, simple enough that a closed-form or backward-induction solution exists. Reinforcement learning is what a practitioner reaches for when the problem is still sequential but no longer simple enough for a closed form, a common situation once market impact, transaction costs, and a client's actual risk preferences no longer fit the clean functional forms those chapters assumed.

The chapter's central worked example makes this connection concrete. It reframes Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s American put early-exercise decision as a three-state Markov decision process, solves it with model-free Q-learning instead of backward induction, and shows that the learned answer converges to the value the tree already established: a small, verifiable proof that reinforcement learning recovers a known answer, before the chapter asks the reader to trust it on problems with no closed form.

The chapter proceeds in three stages. Sections~\ref{sec:rl-framework} through~\ref{sec:rl-taxonomy} build the Markov decision process framework, the Bellman equation, and the vocabulary (model-based versus model-free, on-policy versus off-policy) needed to place every algorithm that follows. Sections~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} through~\ref{sec:policy-gradient} develop the algorithms, from the verifiable tabular Q-learning example to deep Q-networks and policy-gradient methods for problems too large for a table. The remaining sections turn to application and assessment: optimal execution, market making, and portfolio management (Sections~\ref{sec:rl-execution} through~\ref{sec:rl-multiagent}), an industry case study (Section~\ref{sec:loxm}), and the evaluation methodology and risks (Sections~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} and~\ref{sec:rl-challenges}) that separate a system that merely impresses in a backtest from one that is safe to deploy with real capital.

\section{The Reinforcement Learning Framework}

This part sets up the sequential decision problem and the value functions that make it tractable.

\subsection{Markov Decision Processes: States, Actions, Rewards, and Policies}\index{reinforcement learning!framework}
\label{sec:rl-framework}

Consider a market maker deciding, moment by moment, how to skew its bid and ask quotes as its inventory evolves, or a trader deciding how many shares of a large order to release this minute versus holding back for a better price later. In both cases today's decision changes tomorrow's starting position, so the decisions cannot be solved one at a time, as most models in Chapters~\chnum{portfolio} and~\chnum{algotrading} solve them. A reinforcement learning problem is formalized as a Markov decision process, a tuple\index{Markov decision process (MDP)} $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$, to capture this kind of interlocking, path-dependent problem.

At each discrete time step $t$, an \textbf{agent} observes a \textbf{state}\index{Markov decision process (MDP)!states} $s_t \in \mathcal{S}$, a summary of everything about the environment relevant to the decision (a market maker's current inventory and the prevailing bid-ask spread; a trading agent's remaining order size and elapsed time; a portfolio manager's current holdings and estimated risk factors). The agent selects an \textbf{action}\index{Markov decision process (MDP)!actions} $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$ (post a quote at a given skew; sell a given number of shares this period; rebalance toward a given target weight) according to a \textbf{policy}\index{Markov decision process (MDP)!policies}\index{policy (reinforcement learning)} $\pi(a \mid s)$, the probability of taking action $a$ in state $s$, which maps each state to a distribution over the available actions with $\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = 1$. A policy that puts all its probability on one action per state is deterministic: the agent always does the same thing from the same state.

The environment then transitions to a new state $s_{t+1}$ according to a transition function $P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$ and returns a scalar \textbf{reward}\index{Markov decision process (MDP)!rewards}\index{reward function} $r_{t+1}$ (realized profit and loss net of costs; a small quadratic risk penalty; an exercise payoff), governed by a reward function $R(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$. Figure~\ref{fig:rl_loop} depicts this loop; it repeats until the episode ends, either at a fixed horizon (the market closes, the order is fully executed) or when the agent reaches an absorbing state (a position is liquidated, an option is exercised).

The word ``Markov'' is a precise mathematical assumption: $P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$ and $R(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$ depend only on the current state and action, not on the history that led there. Every stochastic process this book has used to model prices makes the same assumption, from the binomial tree's independent up-and-down moves in Chapter~\chnum{valuation} to the random-walk and GARCH innovations of Chapter~\chnum{timeseries}; it is a familiar assumption under a new name, now applied to an agent's decisions as well as to prices. Whether a proposed state representation is Markovian is itself a design choice: a market maker's state that includes only the current mid-price, and not recent order flow, discards information a real market maker would use, which is why Chapter~\chnum{hft}'s order-flow-imbalance regression and Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Kyle's-lambda framework are natural sources of state features for a trading agent, not only standalone predictive signals.

The agent's goal is to choose a policy that maximizes the expected \textbf{return}, the discounted sum of future rewards,
\begin{equation}
G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma r_{t+2} + \gamma^2 r_{t+3} + \cdots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k r_{t+k+1},
\label{eq:rl-return}
\end{equation}
where the \textbf{discount factor}\index{discount factor!in reinforcement learning} $\gamma \in [0,1]$ plays the role of the discount factor $1/(1+r)$ in Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s valuation formulas: a reward received later is worth less today, both because of the time value of money and because a distant reward is a noisier, harder-to-credit consequence of the current action. A myopic agent with $\gamma = 0$ maximizes only the immediate reward, the one-shot optimization earlier chapters' models perform; the closer $\gamma$ is to $1$, the more the current choice is shaped by consequences many steps away, the structure a sequential problem like optimal execution or ongoing portfolio management requires and a one-shot model cannot represent.

\subsection{From One-Shot Optimization to Sequential Decisions}
\label{sec:rl-bridge}

Why the models of Chapters~\chnum{portfolio} and~\chnum{algotrading} did not need this machinery clarifies what reinforcement learning adds. The Almgren-Chriss optimal-execution model (Chapter\index{Almgren-Chriss model}~\chnum{algotrading}'s Section~\chnum{algotrading}.3.5) assumes a known functional form for market impact, quadratic in the trade rate, and a known risk-aversion parameter $\phi$; given those, the optimal trajectory follows in closed form in one step, with nothing to learn from experience, because the model already specifies how the environment responds to every action. Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Avellaneda-Stoikov\index{Avellaneda-Stoikov model} inventory model (Section~\chnum{algotrading}.4) is more dynamic, recomputing the market maker's quotes at every instant as inventory and time-to-close evolve, but its update rule is a closed-form formula derived from an assumed order-arrival process, not a policy learned by trial and error. Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s Section~\chnum{portfolio}.4.1 allocation discussion goes furthest toward the sequential nature of real portfolio management, noting that rebalancing recurs and that transaction costs make each decision depend on the current position, but it treats each rebalancing as a separate, myopic optimization. Each model is right within its assumptions and remains the correct tool whenever they hold: a well-estimated impact function and a well-specified risk-aversion parameter make a closed-form solution more accurate and far cheaper to compute than a learned one.

Reinforcement learning becomes the better tool when that closed form is unavailable: when the true market-impact function fits no simple parametric form, when transaction costs interact with the portfolio's history in ways too complex to solve analytically, or when the reward the agent cares about, a client's realized utility net of taxes, fees, and tolerance for drawdowns, does not reduce to a quadratic cost. In each case the problem is still a sequential decision problem as formalized in Section~\ref{sec:rl-framework}; what changes is that no one can write down $P$ and $R$ in closed form, which rules out the direct optimization of Chapters~\chnum{portfolio} and~\chnum{algotrading} and motivates learning a policy from simulated or historical experience.

\subsection{The Simplest Case: Multi-Armed Bandits}
\label{sec:rl-bandit}

A trading desk sending orders to market faces a choice most descriptions of execution skip: which venue to send each order to\index{venue routing}. A large-cap U.S. equity trades on a dozen or more venues, and the same order routed to two of them comes back at different prices, because each venue has its own resting liquidity, fee schedule, and population of counterparties. A desk that knew each venue's average price improvement would route everything to the best one. No desk knows this, and the only way to find out what a venue delivers is to send it orders, so the desk pays, in worse fills, for every order spent learning rather than earning.

This tension separates reinforcement learning from Chapters~\chnum{ml} and~\chnum{timeseries}. A supervised model is handed a fixed dataset and never influences what it sees next; here the agent's own choices determine which data it observes, so an action that looks bad early may never be tried again and its true value never discovered. The problem in its purest form is the \textbf{multi-armed bandit}\index{multi-armed bandit}, named for a row of slot machines with unknown payout rates: at each step the agent picks one of $K$ actions, called \textbf{arms}, and receives a noisy reward drawn from that arm's fixed but unknown distribution.

The bandit is the right place to start because of what it leaves out. There is one state, which never changes; the reward depends only on the action just taken; and no action moves the agent anywhere. Section~\ref{sec:bellman}'s Bellman equation, with its discounting, bootstrapping, and credit assignment across a trajectory, is unnecessary, because $Q(a)$ is the mean reward of arm $a$, estimable by averaging. The one difficulty left is the one this chapter meets everywhere: the \textbf{exploration-exploitation trade-off}\index{exploration-exploitation trade-off}, choosing between the arm that currently looks best and one whose value is still uncertain.

\weheading{\subsection}{venue-routing}{Routing Orders Across Three Venues}\index{multi-armed bandit!venue routing}
\label{sec:rl-bandit-worked}

A desk routes each arriving order to one of three venues. Measured in basis points of price improvement against the arrival mid-price, venue $A$ delivers a mean of $1.2$, venue $B$ a mean of $2.0$, and venue $C$ a mean of $0.5$, each with a standard deviation of $2.0$ basis points. Those means are stated here so the example can be checked; the desk does not know them, and must infer them from the fills it receives. Since the standard deviation of a single fill is $2.5$ times the $0.8$ basis-point gap between the best venue and the second best, one order tells the desk almost nothing.

The desk keeps an estimate $Q(a)$ of each venue's mean and a count $N(a)$ of orders sent there, updating after each fill by the running-average rule
\begin{equation}
Q(a) \leftarrow Q(a) + \frac{1}{N(a)}\big[r - Q(a)\big],
\label{eq:rl-bandit-update}
\end{equation}
which is the sample mean of the rewards seen from $a$, written so it can be updated one fill at a time without storing them. After sending one order to each venue to get started, it follows an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy with\index{epsilon-greedy exploration} $\varepsilon=0.2$: with probability $0.2$ route to a uniformly random venue, otherwise route to whichever venue currently has the highest $Q$. The opening rounds show the failure mode that makes exploration necessary. The single warm-up order to venue $C$ returned $1.78$ basis points, a good fill drawn by chance from the \emph{worst} of the three venues, and that one lucky draw made $C$ the greedy choice. Orders 5 and 6 went there. What rescued the desk was not a clever exploration rule but repetition: order 6 came back at $-2.03$, dragging $Q(C)$ from $2.085$ down to $0.713$ and demoting $C$ below both rivals. Exploiting a wrong estimate long enough tends to correct it, because every exploit is also a sample. By order 12 the desk has $Q(B)$ highest and is routing to the best venue, though $Q(A)=1.313$ and $Q(C)=1.342$ remain badly estimated from three and four orders respectively.

Run the same policy for $5{,}000$ orders and the estimates sharpen to $Q(A)=1.25$, $Q(B)=1.99$, and $Q(C)=0.40$, against true means of $1.2$, $2.0$, and $0.5$. The desk routes $86.2\%$ of its orders to $B$ and realizes an average of $1.826$ basis points per order.

That average is visibly short of the $2.0$ basis points a desk that knew the answer would earn. The shortfall, $868$ basis points in total across the $5{,}000$ orders, is the \textbf{regret}\index{regret (bandit)}: what learning cost, measured against an oracle that never had to learn\index{multi-armed bandit!regret}. Most of it comes not from the early mistakes, which are cheap and brief, but from the $\varepsilon$-greedy rule itself, which keeps routing one order in five at random long after the answer is settled. That permanent tax is
\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon \times \left(2.0 - \tfrac{1}{3}(1.2 + 2.0 + 0.5)\right) = 0.2 \times 0.7667 = 0.1533 \text{ basis points per order},
\end{equation*}
which is why the asymptotic average is $0.8 \times 2.0 + 0.2 \times 1.2333 = 1.8467$ rather than $2.0$, and why the realized $1.826$ sits just below it. The remedy is to explore hard early and taper off\index{epsilon-greedy exploration!decaying epsilon}: decaying $\varepsilon$ over the same $5{,}000$ orders lifts the share routed to $B$ from $86.2\%$ to $95.2\%$ and the average fill from $1.826$ to $1.943$ basis points, while keeping the early exploration that found $B$. Every later worked example in this chapter uses a decayed exploration schedule for this reason.

\subsubsection*{Exploring Where It Pays: Upper Confidence Bounds and Thompson Sampling}

Decaying $\varepsilon$ fixes \emph{how often} the desk explores but not \emph{where}: an exploratory order is still as likely to go to venue $C$, which the data condemned long ago, as to venue $A$, which might yet be competitive. Two better rules direct exploration toward the venues whose value is still uncertain. The \textbf{upper confidence bound}\index{upper confidence bound (UCB)} (UCB) rule of \textcite{auer2002finite} routes each order to the venue with the largest optimistic estimate,
\begin{equation}
a_t = \arg\max_a \left[ Q(a) + \sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{N(a)}} \right],
\label{eq:rl-ucb}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma=2.0$ basis points is the fill noise and $t$ the order number. The bonus is large for a venue sampled rarely and shrinks as $N(a)$ grows, so a venue is revisited only for as long as its plausible upside could still beat the leader; the slowly growing $\ln t$ guarantees that no venue is abandoned forever on the strength of a few unlucky fills. \textbf{Thompson sampling}\index{Thompson sampling} takes a Bayesian route to the same end \parencite{russo2018tutorial}. Under a flat prior and the known noise $\sigma$, the posterior for venue $a$'s mean is normal with mean $Q(a)$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{N(a)}$; before each order the desk draws one plausible mean from each venue's posterior and routes to the largest draw. A venue therefore receives each order with exactly the probability that it is the best venue given the fills so far, so exploration fades on its own as the posteriors tighten.

Run on the same three venues, with the same warm-up and the same seed as the $\varepsilon$-greedy desk, the two rules route $96.7\%$ (UCB) and $98.5\%$ (Thompson sampling) of $5{,}000$ orders to venue $B$, against $86.2\%$ for fixed and $95.2\%$ for decayed $\varepsilon$, and earn average fills of $1.961$ and $1.971$ basis points against $1.826$ and $1.943$. Realized regret\index{regret (bandit)} falls from $868$ basis points to $286$ (decayed $\varepsilon$), $195$ (UCB) and $144$ (Thompson sampling). A single run mixes the policy's choices with the luck of the fills, so Figure~\ref{fig:bandit_regret} averages the \emph{expected} regret, the sum over orders of the gap between venue $B$'s mean and the chosen venue's mean, across $200$ seeds. The fixed-$\varepsilon$ curve is a straight line: over the last $1{,}000$ orders it adds $0.152$ basis points per order, the permanent $0.1533$ tax computed above. Decayed $\varepsilon$ bends but is still adding $0.028$ per order at the end, while UCB and Thompson sampling add only $0.005$ and $0.003$, finishing at $103$ and $74$ basis points of cumulative regret against $794$ for fixed $\varepsilon$. Their curves flatten in the way theory predicts: \textcite{lai1985asymptotically} showed that no rule can keep regret growing more slowly than the logarithm of the number of rounds, and UCB and Thompson sampling both achieve that logarithmic rate \parencite{lattimore2020bandit}.

\subsubsection*{When the Best Venue Depends on the Order: Contextual Bandits}

The three-venue desk assumed that one venue is best for every order. In practice the answer depends on what the desk observes when the order arrives: a small order may do best on a venue with tight quotes but little depth, a large one on a venue whose hidden liquidity absorbs size, and the ranking can shift with the spread, the time of day, or recent volatility. A \textbf{contextual bandit}\index{contextual bandit} lets the reward depend on a feature vector $x_t$ observed before acting and learns a value $Q(a, x)$ for each action as a function of context, typically a regression per arm such as $Q(a,x)=\theta_a^{\top}x$. The exploration ideas carry over: LinUCB, introduced by \textcite{li2010contextual} for news-article recommendation, adds to each arm's regression prediction a bonus proportional to that prediction's standard error, which is equation~\eqref{eq:rl-ucb} with the regression's uncertainty in place of $\sigma/\sqrt{N(a)}$. A contextual bandit is still not a full reinforcement learning problem, since the context arrives from outside and today's routing decision does not change tomorrow's context. That makes it attractive for routing, pricing, and recommendation decisions that recur thousands of times a day, and it is also its limit: once an action changes the next state, as when a large order's impact moves the price the rest of the order will face, the problem needs the machinery of the next subsection.

Two threads run forward from here. Add a next state, so that today's action changes tomorrow's situation, and averaging is no longer enough: the agent must reason about consequences that arrive later, which Section~\ref{sec:bellman}'s Bellman equation supplies and Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s Q-learning example puts to work. Keep the single state but shrink the gap between arms relative to the noise, and even this simplest problem becomes hard: Section~\ref{sec:rl-kelly-worked} returns to a bandit whose best and second-best arms differ by so little against so much noise that $200{,}000$ rounds still leave the agent on the wrong one.

\subsection{Value Functions and the Bellman Equation}
\label{sec:bellman}

Suppose two agents follow the same policy but start from different states, one with a small, comfortable inventory and one deep in an overextended position: the policy alone cannot say how much trouble each is in, only what each plans to do next. Two functions answer this by summarizing how good a state, or state-action pair, is under a policy $\pi$: the \textbf{state-value function}\index{value function} $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s]$, the expected return starting from state $s$ and following $\pi$ thereafter, and the \textbf{action-value function} $Q^\pi(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a]$, the expected return from taking action $a$ in state $s$ and following $\pi$ afterward.

Because the return decomposes into an immediate reward plus a discounted continuation, both functions satisfy a recursive relationship, the \textbf{Bellman equation}\index{Bellman equation}, named for Richard Bellman's original work on dynamic programming:
\begin{equation}
V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V^\pi(s')\Big].
\label{eq:rl-bellman}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{Bellman optimality equation} specializes this\index{Bellman equation} to the best possible policy, replacing the expectation over actions with a maximization:
\begin{equation}
Q^*(s,a) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\Big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s',a')\Big].
\label{eq:rl-bellman-optimality}
\end{equation}
A reader who worked through Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s two-step binomial tree (Section~\chnum{valuation}.4.2) has already solved a Bellman optimality equation by hand\index{binomial option pricing model!early exercise} under another name. At each node, backward induction compares the value of exercising immediately with the discounted expected value of continuing, and takes the larger: the $\max_{a'}$ above, applied to the actions ``exercise'' and ``hold.'' Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} makes the correspondence exact by reformulating that tree as a Markov decision process and re-deriving the same option value. Dynamic programming methods, value iteration and policy iteration, solve the Bellman equation directly whenever $P$ and $R$ are fully known, as Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s tree does. Reinforcement learning's model-free methods, developed next, estimate $Q^*$ from sampled transitions and rewards alone, which makes them applicable where, as in most real trading and portfolio problems, no one can specify $P$ and $R$ in closed form.

\subsection{A Taxonomy of Reinforcement Learning Methods}
\label{sec:rl-taxonomy}\index{reinforcement learning!taxonomy}

Two further distinctions organize the many reinforcement learning algorithms in use, and fixing them now makes it easier to place each method this chapter introduces. Figure~\ref{fig:rl_method_map} previews where each fits; $\pi_\theta$ there denotes a policy with tunable parameters $\theta$, the form Section~\ref{sec:policy-gradient} develops.

\textbf{Model-based methods} have access to\index{model-based versus model-free}, or explicitly learn, the transition function $P$ and reward function $R$, and use them to plan. Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s binomial tree is model-based: $q$, $u$, $d$, and every terminal payoff are known in advance, so backward induction computes the exact optimal value with no simulated experience. \textbf{Model-free methods} never estimate $P$ or $R$; they learn a value function or a policy from sampled transitions and rewards, treating the environment as a black box that can be queried (by simulating an episode) but not inspected. Every method in the rest of this chapter, tabular Q-learning, deep Q-networks, and the policy-gradient family, is model-free, which is why each remains usable in the settings Section~\ref{sec:rl-bridge} described, where no closed-form $P$ and $R$ exist for a real limit order book or a real client's multi-period utility.

A third, hybrid category, sometimes called \textbf{learned-model-based methods}, trains an approximate model of $P$ and $R$ from data (rather than assuming it, as the tree does) and plans against it, gaining some of model-based planning's sample efficiency without requiring $P$ and $R$ in closed form. This approach underlies some of reinforcement learning's most prominent successes outside finance, including game-playing systems that combine a learned model with tree search, but it introduces the sim-to-real gap Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} discusses, since a plan is only as good as the learned model behind it.

\textbf{Off-policy methods} learn about a policy different from the one actually generating their training data. Q-learning's 
update rule is a clear example: its bootstrapped target uses $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$, the value of the best available action, 
regardless of which action the 
$\varepsilon$-greedy behavior policy, one that takes a random action with probability $\varepsilon$ and the currently 
best-valued action otherwise, actually selected next. This lets a single stream of exploratory, partly random experience be 
reused to learn about the greedy-optimal policy while the agent is still exploring, which is why 
Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s worked example converges to the correct answer despite spending a meaningful 
fraction of its $100{,}000$ episodes taking random actions. Off-policy methods have a clear data-efficiency 
advantage, since one batch of experience can be reused across many rounds of learning, which is why DQN's replay buffer 
(Section~\ref{sec:dqn}) works at all.

\textbf{On-policy methods} instead evaluate and improve the policy being followed. The classic on-policy analogue of Q-learning, SARSA\index{SARSA} (State-Action-Reward-State-Action), replaces $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$ with $Q(s',a')$ for the action $a'$ the policy selects next, so its learned values reflect the still-exploring policy rather than the greedy target. Policy-gradient methods (Section~\ref{sec:policy-gradient}) are typically on-policy too, since their gradient estimator is valid only for returns experienced under the current policy $\pi_\theta$. On-policy methods are generally simpler to reason about and more stable to train, since they never correct for a mismatch between the policy that generated the data and the one being evaluated.

\subsection{Tabular Q-Learning: Recovering Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s Early-Exercise Decision}
\label{sec:rl-qlearning}\index{Q-learning!tabular}\index{American option!Q-learning recovery}

This subsection works tabular Q-learning on a problem whose answer is known: Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s American put early-exercise decision, solved first by backward induction and then learned from simulated experience alone.

\subsubsection*{Setting Up the Markov Decision Process}

Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s American put example used a two-step binomial tree with $S_0 = K = \$50$, up and down factors $u=1.2$ and $d=0.9$, and a gross risk-free return $R=1.04$ per period,
giving a risk-neutral up-probability
$$q = \frac{R-d}{u-d} = \frac{0.14}{0.30} \approx 0.4667$$
 and a per-step discount factor $\gamma = 1/R \approx 0.9615$.

That tree has three decision points: today, at $S_0=\$50$; after one up move, at $S_u=\$60$; and after one down move, at $S_d=\$45$. At each of these
three states the holder chooses between two actions: \textbf{exercise}, receive the intrinsic value $\max(K-S,0)$ immediately and end the episode, or
\textbf{hold}, receive nothing now and let the tree evolve one more step.

The two terminal nodes reachable from the up state, $S_{uu}=\$72$ and $S_{ud}=\$54$, both pay $\max(50-S,0)=\$0$; of the two reachable
from the down state, $S_{ud}$ again pays \$0, while $S_{dd}=\$40.50$ pays $\max(50-40.50,0)=\$9.50$.

Together these define a Markov decision process with states $\mathcal{S}=\{S_0, S_u, S_d\}$ and actions $\mathcal{A}=\{\text{exercise}, \text{hold}\}$. Transition probabilities are $q$ and $1-q$, rewards come from the intrinsic-value and terminal-payoff structure above, and $\gamma=1/R$ discounts every step as Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s tree did.

Figure~\ref{fig:mdp_tree} redraws Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s tree in this chapter's language, labeling each decision node with its two available actions rather than with a single option value.

\begin{bookfigure}[htbp][The American put's early-exercise decision as a Markov decision process]{The early-exercise decision of Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s American put, redrawn as a three-state Markov decision process, with the optimal action at each node marked by an asterisk}{fig:mdp_tree}

    \end{tikzpicture}

Chapter~\chnum{valuation} solved this problem by backward induction\index{early exercise}, working from the known terminal payoffs back to today. Restated in this chapter's notation, that solution is
\begin{align*}
Q^*(S_u, \text{exercise}) &= \$0.00, & Q^*(S_u, \text{hold}) &= \gamma\big[q(0) + (1-q)(0)\big] = \$0.00,\\
Q^*(S_d, \text{exercise}) &= \$5.00, & Q^*(S_d, \text{hold}) &= \gamma\big[q(0) + (1-q)(9.50)\big] \approx \$4.8718,\\
Q^*(S_0, \text{exercise}) &= \$0.00, & Q^*(S_0, \text{hold}) &= \gamma\big[q\,V^*(S_u) + (1-q)\,V^*(S_d)\big] \approx \$2.5641,
\end{align*}
where $V^*(S_u)=\max(0.00,0.00)=\$0.00$ and $V^*(S_d)=\max(5.00,4.8718)=\$5.00$, since exercising is optimal at the down node. The optimal policy is therefore to hold at $S_0$ and $S_u$ and exercise at $S_d$, giving an American put value of $V^*(S_0) \approx \$2.5641$, the value Chapter~\chnum{valuation} reports.

\subsubsection*{The Q-Learning Update Rule}\index{Q-learning!update rule}

Backward induction requires knowing $q$, $\gamma$, and every terminal payoff in advance, the closed-form knowledge Section~\ref{sec:rl-bridge} argued is unavailable in most real trading and portfolio problems. \textbf{Q-learning} instead estimates $Q^*$ from simulated experience alone, never using $q$ in its update rule.

Starting from an arbitrary initial table $Q(s,a)$ (here, all zeros), the agent repeatedly simulates an episode: at each state it chooses an action using an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy\index{epsilon-greedy exploration} (with probability $\varepsilon$, act randomly to keep exploring; otherwise, act greedily with respect to the current $Q$-table), observes the resulting reward $r$ and next state $s'$, and updates
\begin{equation}
Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Big[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\Big],
\label{eq:rl-qlearning-update}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is a learning rate. This update rule\index{learning rate}, \textbf{temporal-difference learning}\index{temporal-difference learning}, nudges $Q(s,a)$ toward a bootstrapped target, the observed reward plus the discounted value of the best action available in the next state, without requiring a model of the transition probabilities.

The agent learns $q$'s effect on the optimal decision by observing many simulated up and down moves and seeing which actions paid off, learning a relationship from data as every other machine learning method in this book does.

\subsubsection*{The First Two Learning Steps}

Starting from a $Q$-table initialized entirely to zero, with a learning rate of $\alpha=0.1$, suppose the first simulated episode reaches $S_d$, chooses \textbf{hold} (all actions still look equally worthless, so the choice is effectively random), and the simulated down-move occurs, landing on the terminal payoff of \$9.50 at $S_{dd}$. Since this transition ends the episode, the bootstrapped target's continuation term is zero, and the update is
\begin{equation*}
Q(S_d,\text{hold}) \leftarrow 0 + 0.1\big[9.50 + \gamma(0) - 0\big] = 0.1(9.50) = \$0.9500,
\end{equation*}
the first nonzero entry the table ever acquires. Now suppose a later episode reaches $S_0$, chooses \textbf{hold}, and the simulated move is down, transitioning to $S_d$ with an immediate reward of \$0 (holding earns nothing until a terminal payoff is reached). The bootstrapped target now uses the current, already partially learned table, $\max_{a'}Q(S_d,a') = \max(0, 0.9500) = 0.9500$, giving
\begin{equation*}
Q(S_0,\text{hold}) \leftarrow 0 + 0.1\big[0 + \gamma(0.9500) - 0\big] \approx 0.1(0.9615)(0.9500) \approx \$0.0913.
\end{equation*}
Two updates in, the table already shows the shape of the true solution, $Q(S_d,\text{hold})$ positive and $Q(S_0,\text{hold})$ smaller but nonzero, though both values are still far from their closed-form targets of \$4.8718 and \$2.5641.

The $100{,}000$-episode simulation below repeats this mechanism: each terminal payoff nudges the value of the state that led to it, and that nudge propagates one more step backward the next time an earlier state is visited, diffusing the terminal reward's information back to $S_0$ without ever consulting $q$.

The companion notebook simulates this process for $100{,}000$ episodes, using a learning rate that decays with each state-action pair's visit count and an exploration rate $\varepsilon$ that decays from $0.30$ to $0.02$ over training, standard choices that ensure enough early exploration to discover the exercise decision at $S_d$ while converging to a stable, greedy policy. Table~\ref{tab:rl_qlearning} compares the learned $Q$-table to the closed-form values derived above.

\subsubsection*{Comparing the Learned and Closed-Form Solutions}\index{Q-learning!early exercise}

The learned policy matches the closed-form optimal policy exactly: hold at $S_0$ ($\$2.5328 > \$0.0000$), exercise at
$S_d$ ($\$5.0000 > \$4.9234$), and, at $S_u$, both actions are learned as worth $\$0.0000$, an exact tie that matches Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s own
observation that ``both exercise and continuation are worth \$0'' at that node, so which action the tie-break happens to favor is
economically inconsequential.

Every ``exercise'' value converges exactly, with no residual error, since exercising ends the episode
immediately and its reward is observed without any noise; every ``hold'' value converges to within roughly $1\%$ of the true value but
not exactly, since a ``hold'' estimate is a bootstrapped average built from a finite number of noisy sampled continuations rather than
an exact expectation computed from a known $q$.

This gap is not a bug to be engineered away; it is the fundamental price of model-free\index{model-based versus model-free}
learning, and the most important lesson of this example: when the transition probabilities and payoffs are
known in closed form, as they are in Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s tree, backward induction is strictly better, exact, instantaneous,
and needs no simulated data, and reinforcement learning should not be used. Reinforcement learning earns its keep only
in the more realistic setting, developed throughout the rest of this chapter, where no such closed form exists and simulated or
historical experience is the only source of information available.

\subsection{From Tables to Function Approximation: Deep Q-Networks}\index{deep Q-network (DQN)}
\label{sec:dqn}

Tabular Q-learning stores one number for every state-action pair, which works only because Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s example has three states and two actions. A realistic trading state (inventory, elapsed time, recent order-flow imbalance from Chapter~\chnum{hft}, and a handful of price-based features) takes values in a continuous or very large discrete space, so an explicit table can be neither stored nor visited often enough to fill in\index{function approximation}.

The standard fix, introduced by \textcite{mnih2015human} and known as the \textbf{Deep Q-Network} (DQN), replaces the table with a neural network $Q(s,a;\theta)$, the feedforward architecture Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s Section~\chnum{ml}.6.4 introduced, trained to approximate the same Bellman-optimal $Q^*$ function. The network takes a state (or a fixed-size feature vector describing one) as input and outputs one value per possible action; training minimizes the squared temporal-difference error,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\big(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a';\theta^-) - Q(s,a;\theta)\big)^2\Big],
\label{eq:rl-dqn-loss}
\end{equation}
using the gradient-descent machinery Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s Section~\chnum{ml}.3.3 developed for supervised learning. Here $\theta^-$ is a periodically updated copy of the network's own parameters (a ``target network'')\index{deep Q-network (DQN)!target network} that keeps the bootstrapped target from chasing a constantly moving estimate. It is one of two stabilization tricks that made deep Q-learning reliable enough to train at all; the other is storing past transitions in a large ``replay buffer''\index{deep Q-network (DQN)!experience replay} and resampling them at random rather than training only on the most recent experience.

The update rule is otherwise identical to tabular Q-learning: the network generalizes across similar states instead of memorizing each one, so the agent can act sensibly in a state it has never visited, provided it resembles ones it has seen, the same generalization argument that justifies every other neural network in this book.

Deep Q-networks are also much more finicky to train than tabular Q-learning, which matters given this book's repeated warnings (Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s badly scaled optimizer example among them) that a plausible-looking numerical method can fail to converge without any error message.

A DQN's training loss can fall steadily while the learned policy remains poor, since the loss measures how well the network fits its own, still-inaccurate bootstrapped targets, not how good the policy is. The only reliable check is to evaluate the policy's performance directly: the discipline Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s example applied by comparing learned $Q$-values with an independently known closed-form answer, and that Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} generalizes into an evaluation methodology for problems where no such answer exists.

\subsection{Policy Gradient Methods and Continuous Actions}\index{policy gradient}
\label{sec:policy-gradient}

Q-learning and its deep variant learn a value function and derive a policy from it by acting greedily. This works when the action set is small and discrete (exercise or hold, buy or sell) but breaks down when the natural action is continuous, such as how many shares to trade this period or what target weight to give an asset, since $\max_{a'} Q(s',a')$ has no simple closed form once $a'$ ranges over a continuum.

\textbf{Policy gradient} methods sidestep this\index{policy gradient!continuous actions} by parameterizing the policy directly, $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$, typically as a neural network outputting the parameters of a continuous distribution (a mean and standard deviation, for instance), and adjusting $\theta$ to increase the expected return, using the policy gradient theorem,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \, G_t\Big],
\label{eq:rl-policy-gradient}
\end{equation}
which says, informally, that actions followed by a higher-than-expected return should be made more likely, and actions followed by a lower-than-expected return should be made less likely, with the size of the nudge proportional to how surprising the return was.

\textbf{Actor-critic}\index{actor-critic} methods reduce the noise in this estimate by learning a value function (the ``critic'') alongside the policy (the ``actor'') and using it to judge whether a given return was better or worse than expected from that state, rather than judging it against the raw, highly variable return alone.

\textbf{Proximal Policy Optimization}\index{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)} (PPO), introduced by \textcite{schulman2017proximal}, is the most widely used modern policy-gradient algorithm. It constrains each policy update to stay close to the previous policy, preventing the large, destabilizing updates that plagued earlier policy-gradient methods, and its combination of reasonable sample efficiency and training stability has made it the default starting point for continuous-action problems across robotics, game-playing, and, increasingly, the trading and portfolio applications developed next.

PPO is also the reinforcement-learning step inside the large language models of Chapter~\chnum{nlp}. In reinforcement learning from human feedback\index{reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)}, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is the language model itself, an action is the response it generates to a prompt, and the reward is the score a learned reward model assigns to that response, less a penalty for drifting too far from the model's starting point; \textcite{ouyang2022training} optimized this objective with PPO. The penalty plays the same role as PPO's own constraint on each update: both keep a policy from exploiting the quirks of an imperfect reward, the reward-hacking risk Section~\ref{sec:rl-challenges} raises for trading agents.

\subsection{Learning from Expert Demonstrations: Imitation and Inverse Reinforcement Learning}\index{imitation learning}
\label{sec:imitation}

Every method so far learns by trial and error: an agent that starts knowing nothing must discover which actions are good through the $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration of Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} or its neural-network analogues.

When a record of a skilled decision-maker's past choices exists, an experienced trader's execution decisions, say, there is a faster route: \textbf{behavioral cloning}\index{imitation learning!behavioral cloning}\index{behavioral cloning} treats the expert's historical state-action pairs as an ordinary supervised training set and fits a policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ to predict the expert's action from the state with Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s classification machinery. It sidesteps the exploration problem by never requiring the agent to try an action the expert did not.

Behavioral cloning has a well-known weakness: it is only as good as the expert it imitates, and it can fail badly in states the expert rarely visited. A classifier trained on the expert's typical behavior has no guidance for recovering once the agent's own small errors push it into unfamiliar states, and the errors compound over a long trajectory in a way a single supervised prediction's never do.

\textbf{Inverse reinforcement learning}\index{inverse reinforcement learning} takes a different route: rather than imitating the expert's actions, it infers the reward function the expert appears to optimize, then solves the resulting Markov decision process with this chapter's methods. The resulting policy can, in principle, generalize to states the expert never visited. The cost is a harder, less well-posed estimation problem, since many reward functions can rationalize the same observed behavior.

Both techniques bear on the applications that follow. Section~\ref{sec:loxm}'s LOXM, reported to be trained on ``a large history of past trades together with simulated trading scenarios,'' plausibly combines demonstration-based learning, bootstrapping an initial policy from historical human and algorithmic execution decisions, with further trial-and-error refinement in simulation.

This hybrid is common because it attacks reinforcement learning's sample inefficiency: starting from an expert-informed policy leaves the trial-and-error stage a far smaller gap to close, which justifies the added complexity in a high-stakes application.

\section{Financial Applications}

This part points the machinery at execution, allocation, and hedging, recovering earlier chapters' answers where they exist.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Optimal Execution and Market Making}
\label{sec:rl-execution}\index{optimal execution!reinforcement learning}

Optimal execution was among the earliest documented applications of reinforcement learning in finance. \textcite{nevmyvaka2006reinforcement} applied tabular Q-learning, structurally like Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s example but with a far larger state space built from order-book features, to historical limit order book data. The learned execution policy reduced trading costs relative to the TWAP and VWAP benchmarks\index{TWAP (time-weighted average price)}\index{execution algorithms} of Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Section~\chnum{algotrading}.3.4, without being given a closed-form market-impact model to optimize against.

The state in a modern version of this problem typically includes remaining inventory, time remaining in the execution window, the current bid-ask spread, and recent order-flow imbalance; the action is how many shares to submit, and at what limit price, this period; and the reward is the negative implementation shortfall\index{implementation shortfall}\index{reward design} (Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Section~\chnum{algotrading}.6.4 transaction-cost-analysis measure) realized on that period's fill.

Unlike Almgren-Chriss, which commits in advance to a schedule regardless of how the market behaves, a learned execution policy can condition its trade size on the order book it observes, trading faster when liquidity is unusually deep and slowing down when the book thins, an adaptive behavior no closed-form schedule replicates.

\weheading{\subsection}{rl-almgren-chriss}{Q-Learning Recovers the Almgren-Chriss Optimal Trajectory}\index{Almgren-Chriss model!Q-learning recovery}\index{Q-learning!optimal execution}
\label{sec:rl-execution-worked}

Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} verified Q-learning against a known answer on a three-state problem. The same check is worth doing on a problem closer to trading practice: Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Almgren-Chriss worked example, the $250$-share order split across $N=5$ intervals with impact coefficient $\eta=0.01$, per-interval price volatility $\sigma=\$0.02$ per share, and risk aversion $\phi=0.5$, whose closed-form optimal trajectory left unexecuted shares $(250,\ 194.2,\ 142.4,\ 93.4,\ 46.2,\ 0)$ at a total risk-adjusted cost of $139.4$.

Reformulated as a Markov decision process, the state at interval $j$ is the inventory $x_{j-1}$ still unexecuted, discretized to the nearest $5$ shares (a grid of $51$ possible inventory levels), and the action is how many of those shares, again in multiples of $5$, to sell this interval.

The reward for selling $a_j$ shares and leaving $x_j=x_{j-1}-a_j$ unexecuted is the negative of Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s per-interval cost,
\begin{equation*}
r_j = -\left[\eta\,\frac{a_j^2}{\tau} + \phi\sigma^2\tau\, x_j^2\right],
\end{equation*}
with $\tau=1$ matching Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s normalization, and the last interval ($j=5$) forces full liquidation, $x_5=0$, as the closed-form problem requires. Unlike Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s tree, this environment is \textbf{deterministic}: an action leaves no uncertainty about the resulting state or reward, since Almgren-Chriss prices timing risk analytically through the variance term $\phi\sigma^2\tau x_j^2$ rather than by exposing the agent to a randomly sampled price path.

Reinforcement learning is still the right tool here, for a different reason: not to cope with stochastic transitions, but to search roughly $5{,}300$ discretized state-action possibilities, far too many to enumerate by hand, without being given the closed-form $\sinh(\cdot)$ trajectory Chapter~\chnum{algotrading} derived analytically.

Exact backward-induction dynamic programming over the same grid\index{Almgren-Chriss model!optimal trajectory}, computable directly since only $51$ inventory levels and $4$ real decision points are involved, gives an optimal discretized trajectory of $(250,\ 195,\ 145,\ 95,\ 45,\ 0)$ shares at a total cost of $139.52$, close to but not identical to the continuous closed-form values, as expected from rounding a continuous optimum onto a $5$-share grid.

Tabular Q-learning, trained model-free for $600{,}000$ episodes with an $\varepsilon$-greedy behavior policy decaying from $\varepsilon=0.5$ to $0.05$ and a visit-count-decayed learning rate, \textbf{converges to this same trajectory and this same cost}, $139.52$, not merely close to it.

This differs from Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s example, where the learned ``hold'' values settled within roughly one percent of their closed-form targets: because this environment is deterministic, every reward Q-learning observes is noise-free, so once a state-action pair has been explored enough to identify its best continuation, the learned value converges to it exactly.

Figure~\ref{fig:dp_backward_induction} shows the backward-induction computation. Dynamic programming does not work through the five intervals in the order a trader experiences them; it starts from the one stage with no real decision left, the forced liquidation at $x_4$, and solves backward, using each solved stage's value function to solve the one before it, until $V_1(x_0)$ gives the answer for the full problem.

Only after the backward pass is complete can the optimal trajectory be traced forward, choosing at each state the action that attains its computed value: the bottom row of the figure.

\subsubsection*{Extending the Framework to Market Making}\index{market making!reinforcement learning for}

Market making fits the same framework. Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Avellaneda-Stoikov model derives\index{Avellaneda-Stoikov model} a closed-form optimal quote skew from an assumed order-arrival intensity function and a known risk-aversion parameter.

A reinforcement-learning market maker instead learns a quoting policy from simulated or historical order flow. The state again includes inventory, time-to-close, and order-book depth; the action is the bid and ask quotes (or, in a simpler discrete version, a few candidate skews); and the reward is realized spread capture net of inventory risk and adverse selection\index{adverse selection!in market making}\index{inventory risk}, the concern Chapter~\chnum{hft}'s Glosten-Milgrom\index{Glosten-Milgrom model} model formalizes. The appeal matches the execution case: a learned policy can adapt to order-arrival patterns that deviate from Avellaneda-Stoikov's assumed functional form, at the cost of needing much representative training data and giving up the closed-form model's transparency about why a quote was chosen, a trade-off the chapter's closing sections return to.

\subsubsection*{Designing States, Actions, and Rewards in Practice}\index{reward design}

Neither problem above specifies its Markov decision process uniquely, and the design choices matter enormously to whether the agent learns anything useful. A state that omits recent order-flow imbalance discards the signal Chapter~\chnum{hft}'s order-flow-imbalance regression showed carries information about near-term prices, leaving the agent weaker than a well-informed human trader or a simple rule that conditions on that signal. A state with too many redundant or noisy features slows learning, forcing the agent to discover which features matter: the feature-engineering problem of Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s Section~\chnum{ml}.5.2, made harder because the agent must learn it from its own trial and error rather than from a labeled training set.

Reward design carries the same tension in sharper form, because, as Section~\ref{sec:rl-challenges} shows, an imperfect reward produces not a slightly worse agent but one that is often confidently wrong about what it is optimizing. A common compromise is to shape the reward with a densely observed intermediate signal, a small penalty proportional to inventory held at each step, say, alongside the sparse terminal profit and loss. This speeds learning, since the agent gets feedback every step rather than only at the end of a long execution episode. But if the shaping term is not scaled carefully against the true objective, the agent can over-optimize the proxy, holding needlessly little inventory, at the expense of the goal it was meant to support. There is no universal recipe; the balance is validated as any other model choice in this book is, by comparing the policy's out-of-sample performance with the closed-form or rule-based benchmark it should improve on, as Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} recommends.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Portfolio Management}
\label{sec:rl-portfolio}\index{reinforcement learning!in portfolio management}

Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s mean-variance framework solves a single-period allocation problem: given today's estimated expected returns and covariance matrix, find the weights that optimize a risk-return trade-off today. Section~\chnum{portfolio}.4.1 acknowledges that real portfolios are rebalanced repeatedly and that transaction costs make each rebalancing depend on the current position, but stops at flagging this rather than solving the multi-period problem.

Framed as a Markov decision process, the state is the portfolio's current holdings together with estimated risk-factor exposures (Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s Section~\chnum{portfolio}.3.4 multi-factor model is a natural source of these features); the action is a new target weight vector, or equivalently the trades needed to reach it. The reward combines the period's realized return with an explicit penalty for transaction costs\index{transaction costs!portfolio rebalancing} and, if desired, for realized volatility or drawdown, letting the agent learn a rebalancing policy that trades off today's allocation quality against the cost of getting there, rather than treating each rebalancing as a fresh, myopic optimization as Chapter~\chnum{portfolio} does.

This framing is more general than Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s static optimization, and the generality is its cost. A reinforcement-learning portfolio agent needs either a large amount of representative historical data or a realistic market simulator to train in\index{market simulator}, and a poorly specified reward (one that ignores drawdown, or rewards raw return without risk adjustment) will produce a policy that optimizes the wrong objective with no warning, a version of the ``reward hacking'' problem Section~\ref{sec:rl-challenges} discusses.

In practice, most institutional applications of reinforcement learning to portfolio management remain closer to research than production: they test whether an adaptive rebalancing policy can beat Chapter~\chnum{portfolio}'s static mean-variance benchmark net of realistic transaction costs, rather than replacing it. Reinforcement learning has so far shown clearer, more reproducible gains in the execution and market-making applications of Section~\ref{sec:rl-execution}, where the reward (realized trading cost) is comparatively unambiguous, than in full portfolio management, where the right reward is an open question tied to a client's often unstated risk preferences.

Multi-period portfolio choice has a classical closed-form precedent that clarifies what a learned policy would need to rediscover. Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s Section~\chnum{intro}.5.2 introduced the \textbf{Kelly criterion}\index{Kelly criterion} for a single repeated bet; maximizing the expected \emph{logarithm} of long-run wealth rather than expected wealth extends to repeated rebalancing under a power-utility objective\index{expected utility!log utility}, giving an allocation that avoids both the ruin risk of over-betting and the excessive caution of under-betting. This is a sequential decision problem with a closed-form answer under simplifying assumptions (constant, known return and variance), the kind of further step Section~\ref{sec:rl-bridge} noted Chapter~\chnum{portfolio} could have taken. It also supplies a sanity check: on a simplified simulated market where the Kelly fraction is known exactly, a correctly implemented reinforcement-learning portfolio agent should converge toward it, a check with the same purpose as Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s comparison against Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s closed-form option value, before the agent is trusted on a real market with no closed form to check against.

\weheading{\subsection}{rl-kelly-bandit}{Why Model-Free Search Struggles on the Kelly Bet}\index{Kelly criterion!bandit formulation}\index{multi-armed bandit!Kelly bet}
\label{sec:rl-kelly-worked}

Running that sanity check, rather than asserting it would work, is instructive because it does not go as cleanly as Sections~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} and~\ref{sec:rl-execution-worked}. Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s repeated bet, an even-money wager ($b=1$) won with probability $p=0.6$, has closed-form optimal fraction $f^*=p-q/b=0.20$ and long-run growth rate $g(f^*)\approx0.0201$. As a Markov decision process this is a single, repeated state (the optimal fraction does not depend on current wealth, so wealth need not be tracked), with action $f$ on a grid of $5\%$ increments, $f\in\{0.00,0.05,\ldots,0.95\}$, and an immediate, noisy reward $r=\ln(1+f)$ with probability $p$ or $r=\ln(1-f)$ with probability $q$, drawn independently every period, with no next state and so no bootstrapping. It is a multi-armed bandit, structurally identical to Section~\ref{sec:rl-bandit-worked}'s venue-routing problem with twenty arms in place of three, but with no hope of an easy answer, for a reason worth stating before the numbers.

The difficulty is the ratio of the reward's noise to what there is to learn: the gap between the optimal action's growth rate and its nearest neighbors on the grid, $g(0.20)-g(0.15)\approx0.0013$ and $g(0.20)-g(0.25)\approx0.0013$, is tiny, while a single bet's outcome swings between $\ln(1.20)\approx0.182$ and $\ln(0.80)\approx-0.223$, a standard deviation roughly $150$ times larger than the gap Q-learning must resolve.

Trained for $200{,}000$ simulated bets with the same $\varepsilon$-greedy, visit-count-decayed setup used throughout this chapter, tabular Q-learning settles on $f=0.15$, not $f^*=0.20$, leaving $g(0.20)-g(0.15)\approx0.0013$ of achievable growth rate on the table, about $6.4\%$ of the optimal rate: a real, if modest, miss, unlike the exact or near-exact recovery of Sections~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} and~\ref{sec:rl-execution-worked}. Figure~\ref{fig:kelly_growth_curve} shows why: the growth-rate curve is nearly flat across the $0.10$-to-$0.30$ range, so the true optimum and Q-learning's answer sit almost on top of each other.

Rather than averaging opaque rewards over a large discretized action grid, it exploits the problem's compact underlying model: a single unknown parameter, the win probability $p$, plugged into Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s closed form.

Counting wins and losses across the same $200{,}000$ simulated bets gives an observed win frequency $\hat p=0.6003$, and substituting into $\hat f^*=\hat p-(1-\hat p)/b$ gives $\hat f^*\approx0.2006$, within $0.0006$ of the true optimum, an order of magnitude closer than Q-learning's answer from the same data. This is Section~\ref{sec:rl-taxonomy}'s model-based-versus-model-free distinction made numerical, and it sharpens Section~\ref{sec:rl-bridge}'s argument: reinforcement learning is the right tool once no compact model is available, but when one is, as here, where a single Bernoulli parameter stands for the whole problem, exploiting that structure can be far more sample-efficient than a black-box search over a discretized action grid.

Not every problem that can be written as a Markov decision process is best solved as one; recognizing when a problem's structure should be modeled directly rather than learned model-free is part of the practitioner's job, not something reinforcement learning relieves them of.

\subsection{Deep Hedging: Learning to Hedge Under Frictions}\index{deep hedging}
\label{sec:deep-hedging}

Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s replication argument prices an option by continuously holding $\Delta$ shares of the underlying, and Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s delta-normal example measures an option book's risk through the same delta. Both assume something no trading desk enjoys\index{delta hedging!limits of}: that the hedge can be rebalanced continuously and for free. A real desk pays a bid-ask spread and fees on every rebalancing trade, which creates a dilemma the frictionless theory defines away. Rebalance after every tick and transaction costs devour the option premium; rebalance rarely and the position runs unhedged in between, exposed to the gap risk Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk} quantified. Deciding \emph{when} and \emph{by how much} to adjust the hedge as the underlying moves and expiry approaches is a sequential decision problem under uncertainty, this chapter's subject.

Cast in Section~\ref{sec:rl-framework}'s terms, the state is what the hedger observes, time to maturity $\tau$, the underlying price $S_t$, and the position $h_t$ currently held; the action is the new position $h_{t+1}$ to hold until the next decision point; and the objective is defined on the terminal outcome: let $W_T$ be the hedger's final wealth after the option is settled and the hedge unwound, premium, trading gains, and all transaction costs included. \textbf{Deep hedging}\index{deep hedging} chooses the policy to minimize a convex risk measure of the shortfall,
\begin{equation}
\min_{\pi}\; \rho\!\left(-W_T^{\pi}\right), \qquad \text{for example } \rho = \mathrm{ES}_{95\%},
\label{eq:rl-deep-hedging}
\end{equation}
where the risk measure is the Expected Shortfall Chapter\index{Expected Shortfall (ES)!ES-optimal hedging}~\chnum{marketrisk} introduced, now serving not to measure a fixed position's risk but as the training objective a policy is optimized against.

A small worked example makes the trade-off concrete with numbers this book has already computed. Take Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s market maker, short the one-year \$50-strike call priced in Chapter~\chnum{valuation} ($S_0=K=\$50$, $r=4\%$, $\sigma=30\%$, $C_0\approx\$6.88$, $\Delta_0=0.6115$), and have them hedge it over the option's life on $10{,}000$ simulated price paths (geometric Brownian motion with real-world drift $\mu=8\%$, 252 daily steps, seed 7 in the companion notebook), paying a proportional transaction cost of 50 basis points\index{transaction costs!hedging under} of traded value on every trade, the friction an illiquid underlying imposes. The policy class is a simple one-parameter family: rebalance to the current Black-Scholes-Merton delta only when the held position has drifted more than a \emph{band} $b$ away from it, so $b=0$ is the textbook daily delta hedge\index{deep hedging!rebalancing band}\index{delta hedging!transaction costs} and larger $b$ trades less often. The first trade is hand-checkable: the hedger buys $\Delta_0=0.6115$ shares at \$50, paying $0.005 \times 0.6115 \times \$50 \approx \$0.15$ in costs. Table~\ref{tab:deep_hedging} reports, across the $10{,}000$ paths, the mean terminal profit and loss, its standard deviation, the mean total transaction cost, and the 95\% Expected Shortfall of the terminal loss.

The table repays a careful read. Leaving the position unhedged is catastrophic: a \$12.54 standard deviation and a \$39.30 Expected Shortfall against a \$6.88 premium collected. The textbook daily delta hedge cuts that risk twenty-fold, at a price the frictionless theory never mentions: \$1.56 of expected transaction costs, nearly a quarter of the premium. The band policy at $b=0.06$, selected by searching the grid for the lowest $\mathrm{ES}_{95\%}$, cuts mean costs by 40\% \emph{and} carries less tail risk than hedging every day, because each skipped rebalance saves a cost that would otherwise be paid with certainty, while the extra hedging error it accepts is small and diversifies across the path. Push the band too wide and unhedged risk floods back in, as the $b=0.20$ row shows. The friction level, not taste, dictates the answer: rerun with a 5-basis-point cost and the $\mathrm{ES}$-optimal band shrinks to $0.01$, essentially the textbook hedge, so Black-Scholes-Merton delta hedging is not wrong but is the frictionless limit of a more general policy\index{delta hedging!versus deep hedging} that the data-driven search recovers.

One feature of this simulation needs a warning before the method goes further. Its paths grow at the real-world drift $\mu=8\%$, not at the risk-free rate, because a hedger who wants to know how much it could lose needs the distribution of outcomes it will face: risk is measured under the \textbf{real-world measure}\index{real-world measure}, often written $P$. Prices are not. Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s Black-Scholes-Merton value is an expectation under the risk-neutral measure\index{risk-neutral pricing} $Q$, in which the underlying drifts at $r$, and the two answers differ by far more than rounding. Discounting the call's expected payoff along the simulator's own drift gives $e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{P}[(S_T-K)^+] = \$8.18$, against the $\$6.88$ arbitrage-free price, an overstatement of $\$1.30$, or $18.9\%$, because the faster-growing stock finishes in the money more often. An agent rewarded with discounted payoffs on $P$-paths, and then asked what the option is worth, will learn that wrong number, and nothing in its training signal will tell it so. Reinforcement learning used for pricing must therefore either simulate under $Q$, calibrated to traded prices, or, as deep hedging does, price through the hedge itself: \textcite{buehler2019deep} define the price as the cash amount that makes the hedger indifferent, under its risk measure, between writing the option and not, an indifference price that coincides with the risk-neutral price only in the frictionless, complete-market limit. Under frictions the gap between them is real, and it is the price of risk that cannot be hedged away.

Deep hedging proper, introduced by \textcite{buehler2019deep}\index{deep hedging!Buehler et al.}, replaces the one-parameter band with a neural network policy $\pi_\theta(\tau, S_t, h_t)$ trained by stochastic gradient descent over simulated paths to minimize the same kind of convex risk measure, the step from a hand-designed policy family to a learned one that Section~\ref{sec:dqn} made for value functions. The gain is generality, not magic. The network is never shown a delta, a gamma, or any other Greek, yet in the frictionless limit it rediscovers the Black-Scholes-Merton hedge, and away from that limit it can absorb what no closed-form hedge handles gracefully: several hedging instruments at once, transaction costs of realistic shape, discrete trading times, position limits, and payoffs with no replicating formula. Reports of production use at major derivatives desks followed within a few years of the paper, making hedging, alongside Section~\ref{sec:loxm}'s optimal execution, one of the two applications in this chapter where learned policies have reached practice. The caveats developed earlier bind here with full force: every path the policy learns from comes from a simulator, so the learned hedge is only as good as the simulator's realism (Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation}'s sim-to-real gap), and a neural hedging policy inherits the governance and explainability burden Chapter~\chnum{xai} attaches to any model whose decisions cannot be read off a formula.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Dynamics and the Limits of a Single-Agent Framework}\index{multi-agent reinforcement learning}
\label{sec:rl-multiagent}

Every Markov decision process in Section~\ref{sec:rl-framework} assumes a single agent facing a fixed, if stochastic, environment, an assumption real markets violate: the environment a trading agent faces is largely made up of other agents, some human and increasingly many algorithmic, each adapting to the same price and order-flow signals. Chapter~\chnum{hft}'s hot-potato effect during the 2010 Flash Crash\index{Flash Crash of 2010} illustrates the dynamic: individually reasonable inventory-management rules, followed by many participants at once, produced a collectively destabilizing outcome that no single rule, viewed on its own, would have predicted. An agent trained against a static historical simulator implicitly assumes other participants' behavior stays fixed, an assumption that weakens as the agent's own trading grows, since a strategy profitable at small scale can behave very differently once it moves the order flow it was trained to react to.

\textbf{Multi-agent reinforcement learning}\index{multi-agent reinforcement learning} extends the framework to several learning agents adapting to one another, and is an active research area for the market-making and execution problems of Section~\ref{sec:rl-execution}, since real limit order books are populated by competing, adapting participants. As of this writing, multi-agent methods are much harder to train reliably than single-agent ones: the convergence guarantees of tabular Q-learning in Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s simple setting generally fail when the environment changes because other agents are learning too. Most production trading systems, including Section~\ref{sec:loxm}'s LOXM, are best understood as single-agent policies trained against a historical or simulated environment, with the gap between that assumption and the real market showing up as the sim-to-real and non-stationarity concerns of Section~\ref{sec:rl-challenges}.

A more tractable middle ground treats other participants' aggregate behavior as part of the state. A single-agent method from Sections~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning} through~\ref{sec:policy-gradient} can then respond to features such as recent order-flow imbalance or realized volatility that summarize what others are collectively doing, without modeling each of them. Most of this chapter's real applications, execution and market making among them, rely on this compromise.

\section{From Simulation to Deployment}

A policy trained in simulation has proved nothing yet, and this part covers what would.

\vignetteheading{\subsection}{loxm}{JPMorgan's LOXM}\index{LOXM}
\label{sec:loxm}

In 2017, JPMorgan disclosed LOXM\index{JPMorgan Chase!LOXM}, an equity trade execution system trained using reinforcement learning on a large history of past trades together with simulated trading scenarios, initially deployed on the bank's European cash equities desk.

Structurally, LOXM addresses the problem of Section~\ref{sec:rl-execution}. At each point during an order's execution it decides how much of the remaining order to place, at what price, and how long to wait before reassessing, balancing the risk of trading too aggressively (unnecessary market impact) against trading too passively (missing the current opportunity and executing later at a worse price). That is the impact-versus-timing-risk trade-off Almgren-Chriss solves in closed form in Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}, learned here from data rather than derived from an assumed cost function. The system was reportedly trained on millions of historical and simulated trading scenarios so that it could adapt to prevailing market conditions rather than follow one fixed schedule, the advantage Section~\ref{sec:rl-execution} attributes to a learned execution policy.

LOXM illustrates both sides of this chapter's central tension in one deployment. It shows that reinforcement learning for execution is more than an academic exercise: a major bank judged a learned policy reliable enough for live client order flow, the practical validation closed-form models have had for decades and any machine-learning alternative must earn. But a deep reinforcement-learning execution policy is far harder to explain to a regulator or internal model validator than an Almgren-Chriss schedule, whose logic reduces to a handful of interpretable parameters, a governance burden that Section~\ref{sec:rl-challenges} and Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s explainability toolkit address and that any institution deploying such a system must take on explicitly.

LOXM was an early example, not an isolated one. Since 2017, industry reporting has described similar reinforcement-learning execution research and deployments at several other major banks and trading firms, consistent with the chapter's claim that optimal execution, alongside Section~\ref{sec:deep-hedging}'s hedging application, has the clearest record of reaching production, because its reward, realized execution cost against a well-defined benchmark, is comparatively unambiguous to specify, the property Section~\ref{sec:rl-portfolio} found still missing from full portfolio management.

\subsection{Simulation, Backtesting, and Off-Policy Evaluation}\index{off-policy evaluation}
\label{sec:rl-evaluation}

Every agent in this chapter learns by trial and error, which raises an operational question none of this book's supervised models faced: where does the trial and error happen? Training on live markets is rarely acceptable, since a partially trained policy will take some bad actions while exploring, and a bad trading action costs real money in a way a still-training classifier's bad prediction does not. The standard alternative is to train in a \textbf{simulator}\index{market simulator}, a model of the market built from historical data that can be reset and replayed without financial consequence; Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s backtesting framework\index{backtesting!reinforcement learning policies} (Section~\chnum{algotrading}.6) is a close cousin. It shares that framework's core limitation: a simulator built from history reflects how the market behaved, including in response to other participants' historical order flow, not how it would respond to a new agent's own actions at scale. This \textbf{sim-to-real gap}\index{sim-to-real gap} is at least as serious for a reinforcement-learning trading agent as backtest overfitting is for the supervised models Chapter~\chnum{algotrading} warned about.

A subtler problem is \textbf{off-policy evaluation}: given historical data generated by one policy (a human trader's decisions, say, or an older execution algorithm), how well would a newly trained policy have performed, without running it? Historical data reflects only the actions the deployed policy took, not the outcomes of actions it did not take: the missing-counterfactual problem Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s algorithmic-recourse discussion raised for credit decisions, now in a sequential setting where one wrong counterfactual estimate can compound over many steps. Techniques such as importance sampling reweight historical outcomes to estimate what a new policy would have achieved, but their reliability degrades quickly as the new policy diverges from the one that generated the data. A novel learned policy is therefore rolled out cautiously: first in simulation, then on a small fraction of live order flow, with continuous monitoring for the performance drift that Chapter~\chnum{fraud}'s precision-drift and Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s population-stability-index discussions developed, and never at full scale on the strength of a backtest or an off-policy estimate alone.

This staged approach, simulator, then a small live allocation, then a growing one, is sometimes called \textbf{curriculum deployment}\index{model deployment}. It mirrors the graduated rollout that Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s three-lines-of-defense model and Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s staged-deployment recommendation argue for any high-stakes model, reinforcement-learning policy or static classifier. What is new is that the policy may keep updating during the rollout, a live, still-learning system rather than a frozen model. Monitoring must therefore detect not only a fixed policy drifting from its backtested expectation but an adapting policy drifting toward behavior nobody has validated, a strictly harder problem than those Chapters~\chnum{fraud} and~\chnum{xai} posed.

\subsection{Evaluating Trained Policies: Metrics and Variance Across Training Runs}
\label{sec:rl-eval-metrics}\index{performance metrics}

Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Section~\chnum{algotrading}.6.1 developed a standard toolkit, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, hit rate, for evaluating a strategy's backtested performance, and every metric applies unchanged to a reinforcement-learning policy. What reinforcement learning adds is a source of variability a closed-form model like Almgren-Chriss lacks. Every method in this chapter is trained by a stochastic process (random initialization, $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration, and, for the neural-network methods of Sections~\ref{sec:dqn} and~\ref{sec:policy-gradient}, stochastic gradient descent), so two training runs of the same algorithm on the same data, differing only in their random seed, can converge to meaningfully different policies with meaningfully different realized performance.

Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s worked example shows this in miniature. It was trained with a fixed seed (seed $=7$) so that its reported numbers, \$2.5328 and \$4.9234 among them, would be reproducible for a reader rerunning the companion notebook. A different seed would give slightly different learned values, still close to the closed-form $\$2.5641$ and $\$4.8718$ but not identical to those in Table~\ref{tab:rl_qlearning}. In a three-state problem with a known answer this is a curiosity; in a large trading application with no closed form it is a serious concern, since a single run's backtested performance could be a favorable draw rather than a property of the algorithm the institution intends to rely on.

The standard remedy is to train several independent runs from different seeds\index{reproducibility!random seeds} and report performance as a distribution rather than a single number. The practice, standard in machine learning, has not been needed elsewhere in this book, whose supervised and closed-form models are deterministic given their data. It extends the caution of Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s confidence intervals and Chapter~\chnum{timeseries}'s out-of-sample RMSE comparisons to the uncertainty introduced by the training process itself.

\subsection{Practical Considerations: What Deployment Actually Requires}
\label{sec:rl-practical}\index{reinforcement learning!deployment}

The methods above can leave the impression that adopting reinforcement learning is mainly an algorithmic choice, DQN or PPO, Q-learning or actor-critic, when in practice the harder and more expensive decisions are organizational and infrastructural.

\textbf{A trustworthy simulator is usually the binding constraint, not the algorithm.}\index{market simulator} Every method here needs a large volume of training experience, and Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} explained why it rarely comes from live markets. Building a simulator accurate enough to train a useful policy, with realistic order-book dynamics, price impact, and plausible reactions from other participants, is a substantial engineering project, often larger than the learning algorithm itself, and few institutions can justify it unless the decision is important and recurring enough. That is why optimal execution at a large bank, a high-volume, high-value recurring decision, adopted early, while more bespoke, lower-frequency problems largely have not.

\textbf{The required skills must complement, not replace, those of this book's earlier chapters.} A team building a reinforcement-learning trading system needs the market-microstructure fluency of Chapters~\chnum{algotrading} and~\chnum{hft}, the statistical and machine-learning foundations of Chapter~\chnum{ml}, and, on top of both, expertise in the distinct engineering discipline of reinforcement learning: stable training, careful hyperparameter selection, and the evaluation methodology of Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation}. That combination is still rare, and most institutions build it gradually. This is one more reason closed-form models like Almgren-Chriss and Avellaneda-Stoikov remain the right default for most applications: they need the microstructure expertise this book develops, but not the specialized reinforcement-learning engineering on top of it.

\textbf{The relevant cost-benefit comparison is against the closed-form alternative, not against doing nothing.} A closed-form model, once specified, costs almost nothing to run and is immediately auditable by the model-risk-management process of Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}; a reinforcement-learning policy costs far more to build, validate, and monitor, and the extra cost is justified only when the closed-form model's simplifying assumptions demonstrably cost real money, through the market-impact and reward-specification gaps Section~\ref{sec:rl-bridge} identified. Consistent with this book's compute-first approach, build and validate the closed-form baseline first, know what it costs in realized performance, and only then invest in a learned alternative, with a pre-specified bar for how much improvement would justify the added complexity and governance burden.

\textbf{Compute cost is a recurring line item, not a one-time expense.} Training a deep Q-network or a PPO policy over the hundreds of thousands or millions of simulated episodes a realistic problem requires consumes far more computing resources than fitting any supervised model elsewhere in this book, and the cost recurs every time the policy is retrained, on a schedule to keep pace with the non-stationarity Section~\ref{sec:rl-challenges} describes or in response to detected drift in live performance. This retraining cost, together with the monitoring Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} requires, belongs in the same total-cost-of-ownership comparison as the simulator investment above.

\section{Challenges in Reinforcement Learning for Finance}
\label{sec:rl-challenges}

Reinforcement learning inherits every model-risk concern raised elsewhere in this book and adds several that are specific to its sequential, trial-and-error structure.

\textbf{Reward specification is the entire problem in disguise.} An agent optimizes the reward it is given, not the objective its designer had in mind, and the gap between them is usually invisible until the agent finds and exploits it, a failure mode called \textbf{reward hacking}\index{reward function}. An execution agent rewarded only for beating a benchmark price, with no penalty for the market impact it leaves for the next order, can learn to trade in ways that look good on that metric while making the market worse for the desk's future orders; a portfolio agent rewarded for raw return with no risk adjustment can learn to take tail risk a real client would never accept. Specifying a reward that captures an institution's objective, including the parts that are hard to quantify, is consistently harder than designing the learning algorithm.

\textbf{Non-stationarity compounds ordinary concept drift.}\index{concept drift} Chapters~\chnum{fraud} and~\chnum{xai} warned that a supervised model's accuracy can degrade as the world changes. A reinforcement-learning trading agent faces the same risk with a twist: other market participants adapt to \emph{its} behavior, particularly once a learned strategy is large enough to influence prices. The environment then changes partly \emph{because of} the agent's own past actions, an adversarial dynamic no supervised classifier in this book has had to face.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning is far more sample-hungry than the supervised methods elsewhere in this book.} Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s worked example needed $100{,}000$ simulated episodes to estimate six numbers to within roughly one percent, with only three states and two actions. Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s supervised classifiers reach useful accuracy from training sets orders of magnitude smaller relative to their problem's apparent complexity, because a supervised label gives direct feedback on every example, while a reinforcement-learning reward, especially a delayed terminal one, says little per episode about which of many earlier actions deserves credit, the credit-assignment difficulty discussed below. This inefficiency is why Section~\ref{sec:rl-practical}'s simulator investment is not optional infrastructure but the prerequisite for any of this chapter's methods to work outside small, hand-verifiable settings.

\textbf{Exploration is expensive and risky in a way it is not in a game.}\index{exploration-exploitation trade-off} The $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration of Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s worked example costs nothing beyond simulated time; on a live trading desk the same exploration means deliberately taking actions believed to be suboptimal, in real markets, with real capital, to learn whether they are better than believed. This risk profile differs fundamentally from a research agent exploring a video game, and it is the main reason simulation-based and off-policy training (Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation}) are close to mandatory for any serious financial application.

\textbf{Explainability and governance do not disappear because a policy performs well.}\index{explainability} A deep reinforcement-learning policy inherits the opacity concerns Chapter~\chnum{xai} raised for deep neural networks, compounded because its behavior is defined over a trajectory of decisions rather than a single prediction, so a SHAP-style, single-decision explanation is only a partial account. A model validator or regulator evaluating a system like Section~\ref{sec:loxm}'s LOXM needs an account of why the policy behaves as it does across a representative range of market conditions, not just a performance summary: the model-risk-management discipline of Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}, extended to explainability in Chapter~\chnum{xai}, applied to a policy that acts rather than predicts.

\textbf{Credit assignment gets harder the longer the horizon.} A supervised classifier gets immediate feedback on every prediction; Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s confusion matrix scores a decision the moment it is made. A reinforcement-learning agent often learns whether an early action was good only many steps later, when the reward arrives: the \textbf{credit assignment problem}\index{credit assignment problem}. Section~\ref{sec:rl-qlearning}'s worked example largely sidesteps it, since its longest episode is two decisions deep, but over a trading day's execution decisions or a multi-quarter rebalancing episode it can be hard to tell which earlier action caused a later gain or loss, which slows learning and demands far more data than a supervised problem of similar apparent complexity.

\textbf{Simulators are themselves models, with their own model risk.}\index{model risk} The sim-to-real gap of Section~\ref{sec:rl-evaluation} is more than a data-availability inconvenience. A simulator built from historical data encodes its own assumptions about prices and order flow, and an agent trained only against a flawed simulator learns a policy optimal for that simulator's biases rather than the real market, the same point as Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s warning that a risk model is only as trustworthy as its assumptions. Validating the simulator against out-of-sample market behavior, not just the agent trained inside it, is part of the same model-risk discipline.

\textbf{The regulatory and legal framework for autonomous trading policies is still catching up.} Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}'s model-risk guidance and Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s explainability requirements were written largely for static, single-prediction models. A policy that adapts over time, possibly learning after deployment, raises questions those frameworks do not yet answer: how often must a live, adapting policy be re-approved, and what counts as an adequate explanation of a policy rather than a prediction? Existing supervisory guidance addresses neither with the specificity it applies to a fixed logistic regression or gradient-boosted tree, so institutions deploying reinforcement learning in a regulated trading or advisory context should expect to extend, and in places invent, their own governance answers.

\notocsection{Key Takeaways}

Reinforcement learning formalizes sequential decision-making as a Markov decision process, with states, actions, rewards, and a discount factor, and its objective, maximizing expected discounted return, generalizes the one-shot optimizations this book has relied on since Chapter~\chnum{valuation}.

The Bellman equation expresses a state's or action's value as immediate reward plus discounted continuation value, and Chapter~\chnum{valuation}'s binomial-tree backward induction already solves a Bellman optimality equation by hand. The chapter's central worked example recovered that tree's known value, \$2.5641, with model-free tabular Q-learning; the learned and closed-form solutions agree to within roughly one percent after 100,000 simulated episodes.

Deep Q-networks and policy-gradient methods, including actor-critic algorithms and PPO, extend the value-based and policy-based logic to large or continuous state and action spaces where a table is impossible to maintain.

Optimal execution and market making, Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s closed-form models, become reinforcement-learning problems once market impact and order arrival are too complex for a clean parametric form, a generalization validated in research \parencite{nevmyvaka2006reinforcement} and in industry practice (JPMorgan's LOXM). Portfolio management admits the same framing but remains harder, chiefly because its reward is harder to specify than an execution cost.

All these applications inherit reinforcement learning's distinctive risks: reward misspecification, non-stationarity driven by other agents' adaptation, the cost and danger of live exploration, severe sample inefficiency relative to this book's supervised methods, and an explainability burden that compounds Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s concerns. All argue for simulation-based training, staged deployment, and continuous monitoring rather than treating a trained policy as a finished product.

Markets are not single-agent environments: other participants adapt to a learned policy as it adapts to them, which is why most production systems remain single-agent approximations of a multi-agent problem. The practical bottleneck to adopting these methods is usually a trustworthy simulator and the engineering skill to train against it reliably, not the choice of algorithm.
