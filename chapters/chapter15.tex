\chapterkey{fraud}
\chapter{Fraud Detection, Compliance, and Financial Crime}

Chapter~\chnum{intro} previewed this chapter with a single number: a fraud detection model that correctly flags 95\% of actual fraud and mistakenly flags only 3\% of legitimate transactions still produces a flagged transaction that is fraudulent only about 24\% of the time, because fraud is rare enough that even a small false-positive rate generates many more false alarms than true detections\index{base-rate fallacy}. That number is the central operating constraint of every fraud detection\index{fraud detection} and anti-money-laundering (AML) system\index{anti-money laundering (AML)}, and this chapter is built around managing it. Chapter~\chnum{ml} developed classification models and the confusion matrix\index{confusion matrix} using loan default as the running example; Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk} applied logistic regression\index{logistic regression}, scoring, and rating models to credit risk. This chapter applies the same machinery to fraud, laundered money, and other financial crime. There the base rate is typically far lower than a loan portfolio's default rate, and the consequences of a false positive or a missed detection differ.

Four worked examples run through the chapter. Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s Bayes' theorem\index{Bayes' theorem} calculation is scaled up to a day's transactions, turning a posterior probability into an alert queue a compliance team must work through. A small fraud-scoring logistic regression shows how a few transaction features combine into a fraud probability, and where such a model still misses a case. A Kaplan-Meier\index{Kaplan-Meier estimator} survival analysis, new to this book, estimates how long flagged accounts stay active before fraud is confirmed, introducing censored observations. Finally, a structuring example shows how AML rules are built around a legal reporting threshold, and how criminals evade it.

\section{Detecting Fraud}

Fraud is a rare-event classification problem, and this part builds a detector while confronting how rare the positives are.

\subsection{The Landscape of Financial Crime and Where AI Fits}
\label{sec:crime-taxonomy}\index{financial crime!landscape of}

Suppose a bank builds a fraud-scoring model that catches nearly every stolen-card transaction and considers its detection obligations satisfied, only to face a regulatory fine for missing a money-laundering scheme the model was never designed to catch. ``Fraud detection'' understates the breadth of what financial institutions are legally required to monitor for.

Table~\ref{tab:crime_taxonomy} organizes the main categories of financial crime this book touches on, the detection approach each typically relies on, and where it is developed.

\begin{booktable}[htbp]{A taxonomy of financial crime and detection approaches}{tab:crime_taxonomy}

\toprule
Crime type & Typical detection approach & Developed in \\
\midrule
Card and payment fraud & Real-time transaction scoring, anomaly detection & Sections~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}, \ref{sec:fraud-scoring} \\

Every row in Table~\ref{tab:crime_taxonomy} shares the same statistical structure: a rare, adversarial event must be distinguished from an overwhelming volume of legitimate activity, and the adversary adapts once a detection method becomes known\index{adversarial adaptation}. A credit-scoring model's borrowers, Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s subject, are not trying to defeat the model; a money launderer designing a transaction pattern to stay under a reporting threshold is. Financial crime detection therefore combines the statistical tools of Chapters~\chnum{ml} and~\chnum{creditrisk} with rules, network analysis, and a regulatory reporting apparatus with no counterpart in credit scoring.

A second difference between the rows decides more about which methods are available than the crime type does: what \emph{labels} each problem produces as a by-product of its operation. Card fraud sits at one extreme.\index{card fraud} A cardholder disputes a charge within days, the dispute is ground truth rather than an opinion, and millions arrive automatically, so a supervised model can be retrained continuously against real outcomes. Money laundering sits at the other. The only label a bank ordinarily obtains is that one of its own analysts filed a Suspicious Activity Report, which records a suspicion rather than a confirmed crime, and law enforcement almost never reports back what came of it. A model fitted to those filings learns its institution's alerting behavior rather than criminality, and the true prevalence of laundering, the base rate every calculation in the next section depends on, is unmeasurable.

This asymmetry is why the methods in this chapter are distributed as they are. Supervised learning dominates where outcomes are observed; unsupervised detection, rules, and network structure carry the weight where they are not; and machine learning enters anti-money laundering largely through alert triage, ranking work the rules have already produced, because analyst dispositions are the one label that problem generates in quantity. Ask of each method below what it is trained on, and whether that is an outcome or an opinion.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection in Transactions}\index{anomaly detection!transactions}
\label{sec:anomaly-bayes}

Suppose a bank wants to stop a stolen credit card from being drained before the cardholder notices it is missing, deciding in real time whether each new charge looks like the cardholder or a thief. The simplest systems built for this decision are rule-based\index{transaction monitoring!rules-based}: a transaction is flagged if it exceeds a fixed dollar threshold, occurs in a country the cardholder has never transacted in, or arrives within seconds of another transaction from the same account in a distant location (a velocity check).

Rule-based systems are transparent and easy to audit, which matters for the regulatory reasons discussed later, but they are brittle: a threshold set to catch fraud will also catch a legitimate customer's unusual but innocent purchase. A publicly known threshold (such as the \$10,000 reporting trigger of Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring}) can be engineered around.

Statistical anomaly detection asks instead whether a transaction is unusual relative to the customer's own history, computing something like a $z$-score for amount, location, or timing against that customer's rolling mean and standard deviation, or using Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s unsupervised clustering to flag transactions that resemble none of the customer's usual behavioral clusters. Both approaches produce the same output as Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s classifiers, a binary flag or a risk score thresholded into one, and so face the same trade-off Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s Bayes' theorem example quantified.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{fraud-bayes-volume}{The Bayes' Theorem Result at Full Transaction Volume}\index{Bayes' theorem!at full transaction volume}

Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s example assumed a bank where 1\% of transactions are fraudulent, a model correctly flags 95\% of actual fraud, and incorrectly flags 3\% of legitimate transactions, and found that a flagged transaction is fraudulent only about 24.2\% of the time. That posterior's practical significance is easy to underestimate until it is scaled up to the volume a real institution processes.

Suppose the bank handles $500{,}000$ transactions in a day. At a 1\% fraud rate, this implies $5{,}000$ fraudulent and $495{,}000$ legitimate transactions, and applying the model's 95\% true-positive rate and 3\% false-positive rate gives
\begin{equation*}
TP = 5{,}000 \times 0.95 = 4{,}750, \qquad FN = 5{,}000 \times 0.05 = 250,
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
FP = 495{,}000 \times 0.03 = 14{,}850, \qquad TN = 495{,}000 \times 0.97 = 480{,}150.
\end{equation*}
The model flags $4{,}750 + 14{,}850 = 19{,}600$ transactions a day, of which only $4{,}750$ are fraud, giving
\begin{equation*}
\text{Precision} = \frac{4{,}750}{19{,}600} \approx 0.2423, \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{4{,}750}{5{,}000} = 0.95,
\end{equation*}
matching, up to rounding, Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s $24.2\%$ posterior probability, since precision and $\Pr(\text{Fraud}\mid\text{Flag})$ are the same
quantity computed two ways.\index{precision and recall} The $F_1$ score, $2\times0.2423\times0.95/(0.2423+0.95)\approx0.386$, is a useful single-number summary for\index{F1 score}
comparing models, but it is the raw count, $19{,}600$ flagged transactions in one day, that a compliance team must staff and review;
Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics} returns to this cost.

This is why fraud detection systems almost never rely on a single model or threshold. A bank that lowered its false-positive rate to improve precision would, by the same arithmetic, also lower recall and let more
fraud through, unless it also improved the model's discriminative power. That is the motivation for combining
distinct signals (transaction-level scoring, behavioral risk tiering, and survival modeling) rather
than relying on any one alone.

\subsubsection*{A Second Application: Insider Trading Surveillance}\index{insider trading surveillance}

The same Bayesian structure applies beyond transaction fraud. Market surveillance teams monitor for insider trading (trading ahead of material
non-public information) largely by flagging abnormal trading volume or price movement in the days before a public announcement
(an earnings release, a merger, a regulatory decision). This is the anomaly-detection logic of Chapter~\chnum{hft}'s order-flow-imbalance model, applied to a much lower-frequency, event-driven setting.

Suppose that, across all announcements a surveillance team reviews
in a year, insider trading precedes about $2\%$ of them, a volume-anomaly detector flags $75\%$ of these cases, and
it also flags $8\%$ of announcements with no insider trading, a higher false-positive rate than Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}'s
transaction-fraud example because pre-announcement volume is noisier than a single card transaction. Applying Bayes'
theorem as in Chapter~\chnum{intro},
\begin{equation*}
\Pr(\text{Flag}) = 0.75(0.02) + 0.08(0.98) = 0.0934, \qquad \Pr(\text{Insider}\mid\text{Flag}) = \frac{0.75(0.02)}{0.0934} \approx 0.1606,
\end{equation*}
so a flagged announcement reflects insider trading only about $16.1\%$ of the time, even lower than Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}'s $24.2\%$. The interesting question is \emph{why}: this example's true-positive rate ($75\%$) is worse than the
transaction-fraud example's ($95\%$), which alone would push precision down only modestly, but its false-positive rate ($8\%$) is more
than double the other's ($3\%$), and that difference dominates.

At a low base rate, precision is far
more sensitive to the false-positive rate than to the true-positive rate, since the false-positive rate is multiplied by the (much larger) population
of non-events;\index{false positive!false positive rate} design effort is therefore almost always better spent tightening false-positive rates than chasing marginal
gains in true-positive rates, a lesson that applies equally to the transaction-fraud and AML alert systems in this
chapter.

\weheading{\subsection}{fraud-logistic-scoring}{Fraud Scoring with Logistic Regression}\index{fraud detection!logistic scoring}
\label{sec:fraud-scoring}

Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}'s example treated the detection model as a black box with a fixed true- and false-positive rate; this section
builds one explicitly, using the logistic regression Chapter~\chnum{ml} introduced\index{logistic regression!fraud scoring} and Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk} applied to credit scoring.
Table~\ref{tab:fraud_scoring_data} records ten transactions, each with three features: the transaction amount expressed as a $z$-score
relative to the cardholder's typical spending, the distance from the cardholder's home address in hundreds of kilometers, and a late-night
indicator (1 if the transaction occurred between midnight and 5 a.m. local time), together with whether the transaction was fraudulent.

Fitting a logistic regression
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{Fraud}) = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta_1 z + \beta_2 d + \beta_3 n)
\label{eq:fraud-logistic}
\end{equation}
using the sigmoid function introduced in Chapter~\chnum{ml}\index{sigmoid function}, to these ten observations gives coefficients $\hat\beta_0 \approx \vChFifteenFitIntercept$, $\hat\beta_1 \approx \vChFifteenFitCoefAmount$ (amount $z$-score), $\hat\beta_2 \approx \vChFifteenFitCoefDistance$ (distance), and $\hat\beta_3 \approx \vChFifteenFitCoefNight$ (late night), all with the intuitive sign: an unusually large purchase, far from home, late at night, each raises the predicted fraud probability.

These coefficients carry one caveat. The ten transactions are linearly separable (a linear support vector machine classifies all ten correctly), and on separable data plain maximum likelihood has no finite solution, because scaling up any separating coefficient vector always raises the likelihood. The fit above therefore uses scikit-learn's default L2 (ridge) penalty\index{regularization!ridge} with $C=1$, which shrinks the coefficients toward zero as Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s ridge regression shrinks its slopes, acting like a prior that keeps a ten-point model from claiming certainty it has not earned. Weakening the penalty shows what it is holding back: at $C=10^6$ the coefficients run to $(\vChFifteenSepCoefAmount, \vChFifteenSepCoefDistance, \vChFifteenSepCoefNight)$ with an intercept of $\vChFifteenSepIntercept$, every transaction is classified correctly, and the distance coefficient even turns negative. The penalized fit is the more sensible model, but transaction 9's miss below is partly a product of the penalty, not of the data alone.

At a 0.5 threshold, the model flags transactions 1, 3, and 6 (true positives) and clears 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, and 10 (true negatives), but assigns transaction 9 a predicted probability of only about $33\%$, missing a fraud case (a false negative) despite the late-night flag, because its amount and distance were unremarkable. There are no false positives in this small sample, giving
\begin{equation*}
\text{Precision} = \frac{3}{3+0} = 1.00, \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{3}{3+1} = 0.75,
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
 F_1 = \frac{2(1.00)(0.75)}{1.00+0.75} \approx 0.857,
\end{equation*}
a recall figure that, by design, mirrors Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s loan-default confusion matrix: three true positives and one false negative out of four positive cases.

Transaction 9 illustrates a structural limitation: a fraud that looks unremarkable in isolation will not be caught by a model that looks at one transaction at a time, however well it is fit. That is why Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering} layers an account-level behavioral risk score on top of transaction scoring, and why Section~\ref{sec:survival-fraud} tracks how account risk accumulates over time.

\subsection{The Threshold Choice: A Precision-Recall Trade-off}
\label{sec:threshold-choice}\index{precision and recall!precision-recall trade-off}\index{fraud detection!thresholds}

The 0.5 threshold is a modeling choice, not a mathematical requirement, and moving it trades precision against recall as Chapter~\chnum{ml} described. Table~\ref{tab:threshold_tradeoff} recomputes the same ten-transaction confusion matrix at two alternative thresholds.

\begin{booktable}[htbp]{Precision-recall trade-off at three classification thresholds}{tab:threshold_tradeoff}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Threshold & Precision & Recall & $F_1$ \\
\midrule
0.30 & 0.800 & 1.000 & 0.889 \\
0.50 & 1.000 & 0.750 & 0.857 \\
0.85 & 1.000 & 0.500 & 0.667 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{booktable}

Lowering the threshold to $0.30$ also flags transaction 4 (predicted probability $\vChFifteenProbTxFour$, legitimate), a false positive, but catches transaction 9 as well, raising recall to $100\%$ while precision falls from $1.00$ to $0.80$. Raising the threshold to $0.85$ does the opposite: transaction 3 (predicted probability $\vChFifteenProbTxThree$) falls below the bar and becomes a second false negative, so recall drops to $50\%$ while precision stays perfect. Neither threshold is objectively correct: a bank sets its threshold by weighing the cost of a missed fraud against the cost of another false-positive investigation, not by any property of the model.

The choice becomes determinate once a cost is attached to each kind of error. Suppose reviewing a flagged transaction costs \$50 of investigator time and a missed fraud costs \$200 on average, so that operating at threshold $\tau$ costs
\begin{equation}
C(\tau) = \$50 \times \text{(transactions flagged)} + \$200 \times \text{(frauds missed)}.
\label{eq:fraud-threshold-cost}
\end{equation}
On the ten fitted probabilities this gives \$350 at the default $\tau = 0.5$ (three reviews and one missed fraud) and \$500 at $\tau = 0.85$, against a minimum of \$250 at $\tau = 0.25$, where five transactions are flagged and none is missed. The default threshold is $29\%$ more expensive than the best, and the optimum is no accident of this sample: flagging pays whenever the expected loss avoided exceeds the review cost, $p \times \$200 > \$50$, so the cost-minimizing threshold is the ratio\index{decision threshold!cost-sensitive choice} $\$50/\$200 = 0.25$. A threshold of $0.5$ is correct only when a missed fraud costs exactly twice a review. Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics} runs the calculation at the scale of a real alert queue.

\subsection{Class Imbalance: Reweighting and Resampling}
\label{sec:advanced-imbalance}\index{fraud detection!class imbalance}

The examples so far used ordinary logistic regression to keep the statistical issue, extreme class imbalance\index{class imbalance}, transparent. Production systems add more expressive models and dedicated imbalance techniques, which solve different parts of the problem and are often combined.

\subsubsection*{Cost-Sensitive Learning: Class Weighting}\index{cost-sensitive learning}\index{class imbalance!class weighting}

The most direct fix reweights the training loss so that a missed fraud case (a false negative) counts for more than a false positive. Refitting Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}'s logistic regression on the ten transactions of Table~\ref{tab:fraud_scoring_data}, with each class weighted inversely to its frequency,
\begin{align*}
\text{Weight (legitimate)} &= 10/(2\times6) \approx 0.83, & \text{Weight (fraud)} &= 10/(2\times4) = 1.25,
\end{align*}
shifts the fitted coefficients to
\begin{align*}
\hat\beta_0 &\approx -2.25, & \hat\beta_1 &\approx 0.86, & \hat\beta_2 &\approx 0.13, & \hat\beta_3 &\approx 0.85,
\end{align*}
and raises transaction 9's predicted probability from $33\%$ to about $41.7\%$, still short of $0.5$. At the unchanged $0.5$ threshold, class weighting alone makes this small sample's confusion matrix worse: transaction 4 crosses into a false positive, giving precision $0.75$, recall unchanged at $0.75$, and $F_1\approx0.75$, below the original model's $0.857$. Class weighting reshapes the decision boundary, but it does not guarantee that a missed case crosses the existing threshold, and on a sample this small its effect is noisy.

Lowering the threshold to $0.40$ alongside the reweighted coefficients (compare Table~\ref{tab:threshold_tradeoff}) does catch transaction 9 (now at $41.7\%$) while still flagging transaction 4, giving precision $0.80$, recall $1.00$, and $F_1\approx0.889$, the same operating point the unweighted model reached at a threshold of $0.30$. For a linear model, class weighting and threshold selection move the decision boundary in closely related ways, so a team tuning one should revisit the other.

\subsubsection*{Resampling: Undersampling and SMOTE}\index{SMOTE}

A second family of techniques changes the training data rather than the loss function. Random undersampling discards\index{class imbalance!undersampling} a random subset of majority-class (legitimate) observations until the training set is more balanced: cheap, but wasteful, since it throws away information about legitimate behavior that helps the model avoid false positives. The Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE)\index{class imbalance!SMOTE} instead generates synthetic fraud examples: for each real fraud case, it finds its nearest neighbors among other fraud cases (Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s $k$-nearest-neighbors distance calculation, used here to construct training points rather than to classify) and creates synthetic points along the line segments connecting them. This adds minority-class examples without discarding legitimate data, but a synthetic point interpolated between two fraud cases is not a real transaction. If the two cases do not represent a coherent fraud pattern, SMOTE can teach a model a feature combination that corresponds to no real fraud behavior, a subtler version of the overfitting\index{overfitting} risk Chapter~\chnum{ml} introduced.

Applying SMOTE to Table~\ref{tab:fraud_scoring_data}'s four fraud cases (transactions 1, 3, 6, and 9), asking for two synthetic points with $k=3$ neighbors, produces:
\begin{align*}
\text{Synthetic point 1:} &\quad (\vChFifteenSmoteAx,\ \vChFifteenSmoteAy,\ 1), \ \ \vChFifteenSmoteALamPct\% \text{ from transaction 9's } (1.2, 0.3, 1) \\
&\quad \text{toward transaction 3's } (2.8, 6.5, 1), \\
\text{Synthetic point 2:} &\quad (\vChFifteenSmoteBx,\ \vChFifteenSmoteBy,\ 1), \ \ \vChFifteenSmoteBLamPct\% \text{ from transaction 1's } (3.1, 8.0, 1) \\
&\quad \text{toward transaction 6's } (3.4, 9.2, 1),
\end{align*}
verified by solving for each point's interpolation fraction $\lambda$ along the amount-$z$-score axis and confirming it reproduces the distance coordinate exactly. Neither point is an observed transaction: both fall between similar fraud cases here, but SMOTE cannot know whether that holds for every pair it selects on a larger, messier dataset.

\subsection{More Expressive Models: Ensembles and Unsupervised Anomaly Detection}
\label{sec:expressive-models}\index{fraud detection!ensembles}\index{ensemble methods}

Logistic regression's score is additive in its features, which is part of why Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}'s coefficients are easy to interpret, but real fraud often depends on \emph{combinations} of features that an additive model can only approximate. Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s tree-based ensembles, Random Forest\index{random forest} and gradient boosting\index{gradient boosting}, split the feature space recursively and can represent a rule like ``flag this transaction only if the amount is unusual \emph{and} it occurs at an unusual location \emph{and} at an unusual time, but not if only one of these is true,'' a nonlinear interaction a single logistic coefficient vector cannot express. This flexibility does not solve class imbalance (a gradient-boosted model trained on the same skewed data shows the same base-rate problem unless combined with class weighting or resampling), and it costs interpretability, which Section~\ref{sec:governance-3lod} returns to as a regulatory constraint.

Since ten transactions are too few to show an ensemble advantage, the companion notebook simulates 5,000 transactions with a three-way interaction built into the fraud-generating process: amount, distance, and lateness each contribute modestly on their own, but fraud probability jumps only when all three are unusual at once. It then evaluates a logistic regression and a gradient-boosted ensemble, with matched class weighting and no information about the interaction structure, on a held-out test set using precision-recall AUC, the metric argued for below.

Logistic regression, unable to represent the three-way interaction, reaches a PR-AUC of\index{area under the curve (AUC)!PR-AUC} $\vChFifteenPRAUCLogit$; gradient boosting, which can split on combinations of features, reaches $\vChFifteenPRAUCGbm$, a $\vChFifteenPRAUCGainPct\%$ relative improvement, with feature importances of $\vChFifteenGbmImpAmount$ (amount), $\vChFifteenGbmImpDistance$ (distance), and $\vChFifteenGbmImpNight$ (lateness) confirming it uses all three features jointly. This is a real improvement, not the in-sample perfection Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s Gini/AUC validation example warned against, because it is measured on data the ensemble never saw.

A fundamentally different approach abandons supervised labels. Isolation Forest\index{isolation forest} and autoencoder\index{autoencoder}-based anomaly detection learn what \emph{normal} behavior looks like from the (overwhelmingly legitimate) data itself\index{unsupervised learning}, without being told which cases were confirmed fraud, and flag observations that are isolated or poorly reconstructed relative to that norm. This matters because confirmed-fraud labels can take weeks to arrive (Section~\ref{sec:survival-fraud}), and adversarial adaptation means a labeled training set always describes yesterday's fraud. An unsupervised detector needs no label to flag a new pattern, but its flag only says ``this looks unusual,'' not ``this is fraud,'' so it typically has even lower precision than the supervised models and is best used as one more signal alongside them, not a replacement.

Fitting an Isolation Forest to the same simulated training transactions\index{anomaly detection!isolation forest}, without the fraud labels, and scoring the held-out transactions confirms this works in practice: the 34 held-out fraud cases receive a mean anomaly score of $\vChFifteenIsoScoreFraud$ versus $\vChFifteenIsoScoreLegit$ for legitimate transactions, and the unsupervised score alone achieves a ROC-AUC of $\vChFifteenIsoAUC$ against labels it never saw, modest but real discriminatory power obtained without a single confirmed fraud label.

\subsubsection*{Evaluating Models Under Imbalance: Precision-Recall AUC, Not ROC-AUC}\index{receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve}

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate across every threshold, and its area (ROC-AUC)\index{area under the curve (AUC)!ROC-AUC} is a common summary of a classifier's ranking ability. Under severe class imbalance, ROC-AUC can look deceptively good: because the false-positive rate is a share of an enormous legitimate population, even a model that ranks fraud only modestly well can show a high ROC-AUC, the base-rate distortion of Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}. The precision-recall (PR) curve\index{precision and recall!PR-AUC}\index{class imbalance!evaluation under} and its area (PR-AUC) avoid this blind spot, since precision is computed against the \emph{predicted} positives rather than the huge negative population, which makes PR-AUC the more informative summary for rare events.

Figure~\ref{fig:roc_vs_pr} draws both curves for the two simulated models on the same 1,000 held-out transactions, 34 of them fraudulent, and the two summaries disagree. ROC-AUC prefers logistic regression, $\vChFifteenROCAUCLogit$ against $\vChFifteenROCAUCGbm$ for gradient boosting; PR-AUC reverses the order, $\vChFifteenPRAUCGbm$ against $\vChFifteenPRAUCLogit$. The metrics look in different places. Among the 20 transactions each model ranks highest, gradient boosting finds \vChFifteenTopTwentyGbm{} frauds and logistic regression \vChFifteenTopTwentyLogit, a precision of $\vChFifteenTopTwentyPrecGbm$ against $\vChFifteenTopTwentyPrecLogit$; that is the top of an alert queue, where a bank operates, and it is where the precision-recall curve puts its weight. Logistic regression earns its ROC advantage far down the ranking: at a $40\%$ false-positive rate it finds \vChFifteenFraudsAtFortyLogit{} of the 34 frauds against gradient boosting's \vChFifteenFraudsAtFortyGbm, but only by flagging 386 legitimate transactions, a precision near $6\%$ that no fraud team would accept. ROC-AUC weights every false-positive rate equally, so it rewards the ordering of transactions no one will review, while the operating region, the first percent or two of false positives, is a thin sliver at the plot's left edge. The right-hand panel also shows the baseline the ROC plot hides: a no-skill classifier sits on the diagonal in ROC space, a respectable-looking half of the area, but at a precision of $0.034$, the base rate, in precision-recall space.

\section{Two Fraud Types That Defeat Transaction-Level Scoring}
\label{sec:new-fraud-types}

The preceding part assumed a fraudulent transaction is, in principle, distinguishable from a legitimate one given enough features and a good enough model. Two of the fastest-growing categories of fraud violate that assumption, each in a different way, and both show where the machinery developed above stops being sufficient.

\subsection{Synthetic Identity Fraud: The Case With No Labels}\index{synthetic identity fraud}
\label{sec:synthetic-identity}

A synthetic identity is not a stolen one. The fraudster assembles a person who does not exist, typically pairing a real but unused identity number (often a child's, a recent immigrant's, or a deceased person's) with a fabricated name, date of birth, and address. Because no real person owns the composite, no one ever notices it, disputes a charge, or files a report.

That fact changes the detection problem. The account is then \emph{cultivated}: it takes a small credit line, pays every bill on time, and is rewarded with progressively higher limits, as a creditworthy new customer would be. Suppose four tradelines are grown from a \$500 to a \$10,000 limit over eighteen months, with about \$120 a month paid across all of them,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Paid in} = 18 \times \$120 = \$2{,}160, \qquad \text{Exposure at bust-out} = 4 \times \$10{,}000 = \$40{,}000,
\end{equation*}
so the ``bust-out,'' drawing every line to its limit and disappearing, nets roughly \$37,840 on an outlay of \$2,160, a return of about eighteen times. The behavioral risk score of Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering} would have said throughout: velocity normal, geography stable, activity consistent with the profile declared at onboarding. Every input this chapter has built points the wrong way, because during cultivation the account is a model customer. The score spikes only at the bust-out, when the money is gone.

The deeper problem is the labels. A bust-out is usually written off as credit loss, since there is no victim and no obvious moment of theft. If $5\%$ of a lender's $100{,}000$ annual charge-offs are synthetic identities, then $5{,}000$ fraud cases a year sit in the training data labeled ``default,'' and a supervised fraud model never learns them. This is the structural blindness of Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s reject inference\index{reject inference}, in a more damaging form: the measured fraud base rate is understated, so every precision figure computed against those labels is optimistic by an unknown margin.

It is also the strongest argument in this chapter for the unsupervised methods of Section~\ref{sec:expressive-models}. An Isolation Forest or autoencoder needs no confirmed fraud label, and the cultivation pattern (several thin-file accounts sharing an address, device fingerprint, or phone number, all maturing on similar trajectories) is the kind of structure an anomaly detector or the network analysis of Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring} can surface without one.

\subsection{Authorized Push Payment Fraud: The Case Where Every Feature Is Legitimate}\index{authorized push payment (APP) fraud}
\label{sec:app-fraud}

In an authorized push payment scam the customer is deceived, by a fake invoice, an impersonated bank official, or a romance or investment pretext, into sending the money themselves. The credentials are correct, the device is the customer's usual one, the authentication succeeds, and the instruction is genuine. This is transaction 9 of Table~\ref{tab:fraud_scoring_data} carried to its limit: a payment indistinguishable from a legitimate one on every feature a transaction-level model observes, because it \emph{is} a legitimate instruction from the account holder.

This was long treated as a customer-education problem, since a bank that correctly executed an authorized instruction had done nothing wrong. Mandatory reimbursement regimes\index{authorized push payment (APP) fraud}, most prominently the United Kingdom's, which since 7 October 2024 has required the sending and receiving firms to split reimbursement of qualifying scam losses equally, moved the loss onto the bank's books and made it an expected-cost calculation.

The economics then become favorable quickly. Suppose a bank processes $500{,}000$ faster payments a month, $0.02\%$ of which are scams, at an average loss of \$4,200, and that it bears half of the reimbursement,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Scams} = 0.0002 \times 500{,}000 = 100, \qquad \text{Bank's exposure} = 100 \times \$4{,}200 \times 0.5 = \$210{,}000 \text{ per month}.
\end{equation*}
A model operating at $60\%$ recall and $5\%$ precision, poor by this chapter's earlier standards, catches 60 scams while raising $60/0.05 = 1{,}200$ alerts. At 15 minutes per intervention (a call to the customer before the payment is released) and a fully loaded analyst cost of \$45 an hour (the rule of thumb Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics} also uses),
\begin{equation*}
\text{Cost} = \frac{1{,}200 \times 15}{60} \times \$45 = \$13{,}500, \qquad \text{Loss prevented} = 60 \times \$4{,}200 \times 0.5 = \$126{,}000,
\end{equation*}
a net benefit of \$112,500 a month\index{alert economics!dollar cost}, or \$1.35 million a year. The break-even recall is $6.43\%$: the model pays for itself once it stops six or seven of the hundred monthly scams, a far more forgiving operating point than anything in Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics}, because a missed case now costs the bank \$2,100 rather than nothing.

Because the instruction itself carries no signal, detection has to look elsewhere: at deviation from the customer's own payment history (a first-time payee, an unusual amount, an account that has never sent a faster payment), at context such as a payment initiated during or right after a long inbound call, and above all at the \emph{receiving} side, where mule accounts show the short-dwell\index{money mule}, high-pass-through pattern of Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring}'s layering example. The victim's transaction looks normal; the beneficiary's does not.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{fraud-app-realtime}{Scoring a Real-Time Payment Under a Latency Budget}\index{authorized push payment (APP) fraud}\index{instant payments}

The calculation above treated the model as a recall and a precision. On an instant-payment rail it must return a decision while the customer waits, and the institutions around it have changed quickly. In the first eighteen months of the United Kingdom's regime, from 7 October 2024 to 31 March 2026, the Payment Systems Regulator reports that 88\% of the money lost to APP scams, \pounds316 million, was returned to victims; reimbursement is capped at \pounds85,000 per claim, and the sending firm may charge an excess of up to \pounds100 \parencite{psr2026dashboard}. Firms received a matching tool: since 30 October 2024 a UK payment firm may delay an outbound payment by up to four business days when it has reasonable grounds to suspect APP fraud \parencite{fca2024fg246}, which makes a \emph{hold} (a payment paused while the customer is called) a lawful alternative to releasing or refusing it. The problem is spreading with the rails. The Federal Reserve's FedNow service went live in July 2023 and raised its per-payment limit to \$10 million in November 2025 \parencite{frb2025fednow}, and the European Union's Instant Payments Regulation requires euro-area payment firms to check, from October 2025, that the payee's name matches the account before a transfer is sent \parencite{eu2024ipr}. In the United States, by contrast, Regulation E's consumer protections are built around \emph{unauthorized} transfers, so a payment a deceived customer authorized generally falls outside them, and who bears the loss is set by network rules and each bank's policy, not a federal reimbursement mandate.

Consider one payment, scored at the moment the customer presses ``send.'' Because the evidence arrives as separate signals, the natural first model is the Naive Bayes form of Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s sequential Bayesian updating\index{Naive Bayes classifier}: if the signals are independent given the payment's true class, the posterior log-odds is the prior log-odds plus one log-likelihood ratio per signal. Suppose that a new payee appears in $85\%$ of scams but $10\%$ of legitimate payments, a phone call in progress during the session in $60\%$ against $3\%$, a payee account opened within the last 30 days in $70\%$ against $2\%$, and a payee account that forwards more than $90\%$ of what it receives within 24 hours\index{money mule} in $75\%$ against $3\%$, and that the amount's $z$-score against the customer's history is distributed $N(1.5,1)$ for scams and $N(0,1)$ for legitimate payments, so that its likelihood ratio is $e^{1.5z-1.125}$. The payment goes to a new payee, its amount is ordinary ($z=0.5$), and no call is in progress. From the sender's side alone, starting from the $0.02\%$ scam rate,
\begin{align*}
\text{log-odds} &= \ln\tfrac{0.0002}{0.9998} + \ln\tfrac{0.85}{0.10} + (0.75 - 1.125) + \ln\tfrac{0.40}{0.97} \\
&= -8.517 + 2.140 - 0.375 - 0.886 = -7.638, \qquad \Pr(\text{scam}) = 0.048\%,
\end{align*}
about 2.4 times the prior, but far too low to act on. The payee's account, however, was opened nine days ago and forwards nearly everything it receives, adding $\ln 35 = 3.555$ and $\ln 25 = 3.219$:
\begin{equation*}
\text{log-odds} = -7.638 + 3.555 + 3.219 = -0.864, \qquad \Pr(\text{scam}) = 29.66\%.
\end{equation*}
The two halves of the evidence disagree by a factor of more than 600: everything suspicious about this payment is on the receiving side.

Whether to hold follows from the same cost comparison as Equation~\eqref{eq:fraud-threshold-cost}. A hold costs a 15-minute call at \$45 an hour plus an assumed \$5 of customer friction, \$16.25 in all, and the call breaks the scam in an assumed $70\%$ of cases, so with the bank bearing half of a \$4,200 loss a hold pays when $p \times 0.70 \times \$2{,}100 > \$16.25$, that is, above
\begin{equation*}
p^{*} = \frac{\$16.25}{\$1{,}470} = 0.01105.
\end{equation*}
The sender-side score implies an expected saving of $0.00048 \times \$1{,}470 = \$0.71$, so the payment is released; the full score implies \$436.00, so it is held. The threshold of about $1.1\%$ is the reason a model with $5\%$ precision paid for itself above: under mandatory reimbursement a bank should rationally hold payments it believes are almost certainly genuine.

The payee-side signals are also the slow ones. They live at the receiving firm or a shared intelligence service, and fetching them costs a network round trip inside the decision budget. Suppose 220 milliseconds of the budget remain once the sender-side model has run, and the lookup's latency is lognormal with a median of 80 ms and a log standard deviation of $0.6$. The lookup then misses the budget with probability
\begin{equation*}
\Pr\left(Z > \frac{\ln(220/80)}{0.6} = 1.686\right) = 0.0459,
\end{equation*}
which rises to $0.1240$ with 160 ms left and $0.2496$ with 120 ms, and falls to $0.0138$ with 300 ms. Table~\ref{tab:app_policies} shows what the system should do when the answer is late. The companion notebook simulates $5{,}000{,}000$ payments containing $1{,}000$ scams, scores each with the model above, and rescales to the earlier example's $500{,}000$ payments and 100 scams a month, counting \$16.25 per hold, \$2,100 per scam released, and $30\%$ of \$2,100 for each scam held but not stopped.

Three lessons follow. First, the receiving-side signals improve recall and precision at once, from $51.9\%$ to $85.6\%$ and from $4.8\%$ to $13.0\%$, while \emph{reducing} holds from about 1,090 to 660 a month, and they cut the monthly cost by \$56,523; on the sender side alone the scam and the genuine payment look alike. Second, when the lookup is late the right fallback is the next-best score, which costs only \$3,784 a month against a system that is never late. Third, the instinct to fail safe by holding whatever cannot be scored in time is ruinous: $4.59\%$ of $500{,}000$ payments is $22{,}950$ extra holds, and the policy costs more than twice as much as releasing everything. The latency budget is therefore a modeling parameter, not an engineering detail: it decides how often the model's best evidence is available. Two cautions apply to the numbers. The likelihood ratios are assumptions, and a young account and a high pass-through rate are both symptoms of a mule, so treating them as independent overstates the evidence; the $29.66\%$ is too high, and a fitted logistic regression, which learns that correlation, would temper it. And the $70\%$ stop rate is the least certain parameter, since a customer coached by a scammer will often insist that the payment is genuine.

\subsection{Generative AI as an Accelerant: Deepfakes and Forged Identities}\index{deepfake fraud}
\label{sec:genai-fraud}

Generative AI has handed both preceding fraud types a cheap accelerant\index{generative AI!as a fraud accelerant}. Image generators can produce a convincing face for a nonexistent person, or alter a real identity document, and pair it with stolen or fabricated data: the synthetic identity of Section~\ref{sec:synthetic-identity}, now with a photograph and a document that pass the remote identity verification know-your-customer (KYC) rules require\index{know your customer (KYC)} at account opening. Voice cloning and real-time video impersonation give an authorized push payment scam what it used to lack, a familiar face and voice. In the best-documented case, reported by Hong Kong police in February 2024, a finance employee in the Hong Kong office of a multinational, later confirmed as the British engineering firm Arup, joined a video conference in which the chief financial officer and several colleagues were all deepfakes, and made 15 transfers totaling HK\$200 million, about US\$25 million, to accounts the fraudsters controlled \parencite{chen2024deepfake,magramo2024arup}. Every payment was authorized by a real employee on the firm's own systems: Section~\ref{sec:app-fraud}'s pattern at corporate scale.

Regulators have taken note. On 13 November 2024 the U.S. Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN)\index{Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN)} issued alert FIN-2024-Alert004, reporting a rise since 2023 in suspicious activity reports describing suspected deepfake media, above all falsified identity documents used to circumvent verification, and asking institutions to tag such reports with the key term ``FIN-2024-DEEPFAKEFRAUD'' \parencite{fincen2024deepfake}. Its red flags read like a feature list for a detection model: a photo inconsistent with the customer's other identifying information, identity documents inconsistent with each other, a third-party webcam plugin or a sudden request to change communication method during a live verification check, a refusal to use multi-factor authentication, and device or location data that do not match the documents.

The industry response has three layers, and only the first inspects the media itself. \emph{Liveness detection}\index{deepfake fraud!liveness detection}\index{liveness detection} prompts the applicant to respond in a live audio or video session and looks for signs of synthesis or injected video; it is an arms race, and FinCEN's alert notes that criminals may answer live prompts with tools that generate synthetic audio and video, although the responses may still reveal inconsistencies. \emph{Out-of-band verification}, confirming an instruction through an independently known channel (a call back to a number already on file, not one supplied in the request), defeats impersonation however good the fake; it would have stopped the Hong Kong transfers. \emph{Behavioral and device signals}, the account-level features of Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering} and the receiving-side mule detection above, observe what the fraudster does rather than looks like, and so are the layer least eroded by better generators. The lesson generalizes: once the artifact a control inspects can be forged at near-zero cost, detection has to move to signals that remain expensive to fake.

\section{Risk Tiering and Survival Analysis}

Beyond a single score, this part ranks customers by behavior and models time-to-detection.

\subsection{Behavioral Risk Frameworks and Customer Risk Tiering}
\label{sec:risk-tiering}\index{customer risk tiering}

Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s credit-scoring model mapped a predicted default probability into letter-grade ratings; in fraud and AML, institutions apply an analogous mapping, customer risk tiering, built from behavioral features accumulated over the whole account relationship rather than a single transaction.

Typical inputs include the account's transaction velocity (how this month's frequency and size of transactions compare to its own trailing average, an account-wide analogue of Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}'s amount $z$-score), geographic diversity of counterparties, newly added payees, and, for anti-money laundering (Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring}), whether the occupation and expected volume the customer declared at onboarding under Know-Your-Customer (KYC)\index{know your customer (KYC)} requirements are consistent with observed activity.

Table~\ref{tab:risk_tiers} shows an illustrative scheme built from a composite behavioral score on a 0-100 scale, combining such features with weights calibrated from the institution's own fraud and AML case data, like Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s rating buckets.

A concrete composite score makes Table~\ref{tab:risk_tiers} usable\index{customer risk tiering!behavioral risk score}. Suppose an institution combines three behavioral sub-scores, each normalized to a 0-100 scale: a transaction-velocity sub-score (how far this month's activity deviates from the account's trailing average), a geographic-diversity sub-score (how unusual the mix of counterparty locations is), and a KYC-consistency sub-score (how far observed activity deviates from what the customer declared at onboarding), weighted $40\%$, $35\%$, and $25\%$ based on the institution's backtested case outcomes.

For an account with a velocity sub-score of $70$ (volume well above its recent average), a geographic-diversity sub-score of $50$ (a moderate increase in counterparty locations), and a KYC-consistency sub-score of $90$ (activity far exceeding what was declared), the composite score is
\begin{equation*}
0.40(70) + 0.35(50) + 0.25(90) = 28.0 + 17.5 + 22.5 = 68.0,
\end{equation*}
placing the account in the High tier of Table~\ref{tab:risk_tiers}, triggering manual review and tighter velocity limits, even though no single sub-score (velocity at $70$, geographic diversity at $50$) would necessarily have triggered that response alone.

This is the point of a composite score: the KYC-consistency mismatch, the largest contributor at $22.5$ of the $68.0$ total, combines with two moderate signals to cross a review threshold that no single signal reached, the accumulating, multi-signal evidence of Chapter~\chnum{intro}'s sequential Bayesian updating example.

The weights are written here as though a committee chose them, and in many institutions one did. But calibrating them from historical case data is supervised learning: given a labeled history of which accounts eventually warranted a report, the weights are fitted coefficients from the logistic regression of Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}, and the tiers discretize its fitted score, as Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s letter grades discretize a predicted default probability.

Tiering changes what the system optimizes for: rather than asking whether \emph{this} transaction looks fraudulent, it asks whether \emph{this account} has become risky enough to warrant tighter monitoring of everything it does. That is the right framework for a case like transaction 9 of Table~\ref{tab:fraud_scoring_data}, unremarkable alone but possibly the first sign of a compromised account whose score has begun drifting upward.

Regulators (Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring}) generally require such ongoing, risk-based due diligence rather than a one-time onboarding check, because a customer's risk, like credit risk in Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s rating-transition framework, evolves.

\subsection{Survival Models: Time to Detection and Account Risk Over Time}
\label{sec:survival-fraud}\index{survival analysis!time to detection}

Every classification model in this book, including Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}'s logistic regression, answers a yes-or-no question at a single point in time. A new question, and one institutions care about operationally, is how long a flagged account survives before fraud is confirmed and the account is closed. The answer helps a compliance team prioritize alerts and a fraud team estimate how much exposure a compromised account accumulates before detection.

Survival analysis, developed in biostatistics\index{survival analysis} to study time until death or disease recurrence, answers this kind of question for data in which the outcome, here fraud confirmation, has not yet happened for every observation. The key complication, which ordinary regression cannot handle, is censoring:\index{censoring}\index{survival analysis!censoring} some accounts are closed for unrelated reasons (voluntary closure, a non-fraud risk exit) or are still open when the data are pulled, so their time to fraud confirmation is known only to be \emph{at least} the observed time.

Dropping censored observations, or treating them as if fraud were confirmed at the moment of censoring, both bias the estimate; the Kaplan-Meier estimator instead uses every observation\index{Kaplan-Meier estimator} up until the point it is censored. The estimator computes the survival function, the probability an account remains unconfirmed (open, or closed for reasons unrelated to fraud) beyond time $t$, as a product of conditional survival probabilities at each observed event time,
\begin{equation}
\hat S(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right),
\label{eq:fraud-kaplan-meier}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the number of accounts still under observation (not yet closed or censored) just before time $t_i$, and $d_i$ is the number of confirmed-fraud closures at time $t_i$. This book's companion technical note on the Kaplan-Meier estimator shows that this product-limit formula is not an ad hoc device but the exact nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimator of the survival function once right censoring is written into the likelihood, which is also why the curve steps down only at event times, never at censoring times.

\subsection{How Precise Is $\hat S(t)$? Greenwood's Formula}\index{Greenwood's formula}
\label{sec:greenwood}

A survival estimate like $\hat S(35)\approx0.2025$ is half the story without a sense of how precisely 12 accounts pin it down.

Greenwood's formula gives the standard error of the Kaplan-Meier estimator\index{Kaplan-Meier estimator!Greenwood's formula}\index{standard error} at any time $t$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat S(t)] = \hat S(t)^2 \sum_{t_i \le t} \frac{d_i}{n_i(n_i-d_i)},
\label{eq:fraud-greenwood}
\end{equation}
built from the at-risk counts $n_i$ and event counts $d_i$ already in Table~\ref{tab:km_table}. Applying this at $t=20$, where $\hat S(20)\approx0.5401$, the summed term $\sum d_i/(n_i(n_i-d_i))$ across the five event times up to and including day 20 is approximately $0.08175$, giving $\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat S(20)] \approx 0.5401^2(0.08175) \approx 0.02385$ and a standard error of $\text{SE}\approx0.154$, so a normal-approximation 95\% confidence interval\index{confidence interval} is $0.540\pm1.96(0.154)\approx(0.237,\,0.843)$, wide enough to span nearly the entire plausible range.

At $t=35$, the final event time, $\hat S(35)\approx0.2025$ with $\text{SE}\approx0.165$, giving a 95\% interval of approximately $(0.000,\,0.526)$ once the lower bound is truncated at zero (a known limitation of the normal approximation applied to a probability): the point estimate of roughly $20\%$ is consistent with a 35-day survival probability anywhere from essentially zero to over $50\%$.

The dashed band of Figure~\ref{fig:km_curve} applies the same calculation at every event day, widening as the risk set shrinks. This is the small-sample caution of Chapter~\chnum{timeseries}'s AR(1) confidence interval and Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s collateral-coverage regression: with 12 accounts and 7 confirmed closures, the curve's later points carry large uncertainty however precise their four decimal places look.

The $80\%$-confirmed-within-35-days figure is a planning number from a small pilot sample, not a population parameter, and its interval will narrow as outcomes accumulate.

This is where the survival curve earns its prioritization role, since what orders an alert queue is a \emph{conditional} probability read off $\hat S(t)$. A freshly flagged account is confirmed within its first fifteen days with probability $1 - \hat S(15) = 0.352$, whereas an account still unresolved at day 20 is confirmed by day 35 with probability $1 - \hat S(35)/\hat S(20) = 1 - 0.2025/0.5401 = 0.625$, nearly twice as likely to convert over the coming fortnight as the fresh flag is over its first. An investigator should therefore open the aging case before the newest one, the opposite of the instinct to work a queue newest-first, and this follows from Table~\ref{tab:km_table} without any additional data.

Kaplan-Meier is nonparametric and uses no covariates, so it can say \emph{when} flagged accounts are confirmed but not \emph{which} ones. The learning extensions supply the missing half. Cox proportional hazards multiplies a baseline hazard by a function of an account's features, and survival forests and gradient-boosted survival models drop the proportionality assumption, so the same censored data yield a per-account predicted time to confirmation, which is what an alert-ranking system consumes. Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}'s discrete-time hazard formulation\index{discrete-time hazard model} is the credit-risk counterpart of the same idea.

\section{Money Laundering and Sanctions}

This part turns to laundering typologies, sanctions screening, and detection shared across institutions.

\subsection{Anti-Money Laundering: Structuring, Monitoring, and Regulation}
\label{sec:aml-structuring}\index{anti-money laundering (AML)}

Money laundering, disguising the origin of criminal funds\index{money laundering} so that they appear to come from a legitimate source, differs from transaction fraud: the funds are often not stolen from the institution, and the concealed crime frequently occurred entirely outside the financial system (drug trafficking, corruption, sanctions evasion) before the money reached a bank.

In the United States, the Bank Secrecy Act\index{Bank Secrecy Act (BSA)} requires banks to file a Currency Transaction Report (CTR)\index{Currency Transaction Report (CTR)} for any cash transaction exceeding \$10,000, and a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)\index{Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)} for any transaction pattern, regardless of size, that appears designed to evade reporting requirements or otherwise suggests criminal activity; equivalent frameworks exist in most jurisdictions under the international standards\index{anti-money laundering (AML)!regulation} set by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF)\index{Financial Action Task Force (FATF)}. The European Union is replacing its patchwork of nationally transposed directives with a 2024 legislative package, published in the Official Journal on 19 June 2024: a directly applicable AML Regulation that applies from 10 July 2027, a sixth AML Directive, and a new EU Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA)\index{Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA)} seated in Frankfurt, which began operating in July 2025 and, as of October 2026, is expected to select around 40 high-risk obliged entities during 2027 for direct supervision from 2028 \parencite{eu2024amlr,eu2024amla,amla2025about}.

\weheading{\subsubsection*}{fraud-structuring}{Detecting Structuring}\index{structuring!detection}

Structuring\index{structuring} (sometimes called ``smurfing'') is the practice of breaking a large cash transaction into several smaller ones, each below the CTR reporting threshold, to avoid triggering a report. Suppose a customer makes five cash deposits of \$9,500 each over three days, each individually \$500 below the \$10,000 CTR threshold,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Total deposited} = 5 \times \$9{,}500 = \$47{,}500,
\end{equation*}
an amount that would trigger a CTR as a single deposit but, spread across five deposits each under the threshold, triggers none. This is why AML monitoring cannot rely on Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}'s single-transaction rules: ``flag any transaction over \$10,000'' is by construction blind to a pattern engineered to stay under it.

Structuring detection instead applies a rule across a rolling window\index{anti-money laundering (AML)!structuring}, for example flagging any customer with three or more cash deposits within seven days each within \$1,000 of the threshold, which the five \$9,500 deposits would trigger even though no single deposit would. This is the account-level monitoring philosophy of Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering}: the signal is a pattern across transactions and time, not any single transaction.

\subsection{Placement, Layering, and Integration: A Network Example}
\label{sec:aml-network}\index{anti-money laundering (AML)!placement, layering, integration}

Structuring is the classic technique for the first of the three stages of laundering: \emph{placement}, introducing cash\index{money laundering!placement} into the financial system, the deposit pattern above. \emph{Layering} follows\index{money laundering!layering}: moving the placed funds through a chain of transactions and accounts, often across institutions and jurisdictions, to obscure their criminal origin.

\emph{Integration} is the final stage\index{money laundering!integration}, reintroducing the layered funds into the legitimate economy, for example through a real estate purchase or an investment in a business, after which they are practically indistinguishable from legitimately earned money.

Layering is where the network-based detection of Table~\ref{tab:crime_taxonomy} becomes necessary, because no single account's activity, viewed in isolation, looks suspicious. Suppose \$500,000 in placed funds moves through four shell entities\index{shell company!layering network}\index{transaction network}, Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta, each receiving the funds and forwarding $98\%$ of what it received onward within 24 hours (retaining $2\%$, nominally a service or consulting fee), a repeating pattern that Table~\ref{tab:layering_network} traces hop by hop.

After four hops, $\$461{,}184.08$, over $92\%$ of the original placed amount, reaches its destination, with only $\$38{,}815.92$ lost to retained ``fees,'' having passed through four legally separate entities in under a week. Figure~\ref{fig:layering_network} draws this chain as the network it is, rather than as four individually unremarkable rows in a table.

No individual hop in Table~\ref{tab:layering_network} looks wrong; each is a plausible business payment of a plausible size. What is anomalous is the pattern viewed as a network, and each of its three features is a number rather than an impression: a pass-through ratio of exactly $0.98$ at every hop, where a real operating business retains and redeploys most of what it receives; a dwell time under 24 hours, against the weeks a real payables cycle takes; and, at every interior node, exactly one party paying in and one party paid out, an in-degree and out-degree of one in the vocabulary of graphs\index{transaction network!in-degree and out-degree}\index{in-degree and out-degree}, where a real supplier has many customers and many suppliers. Each has innocent explanations on its own. All three at once, repeated four hops in a row, do not.

That conjunction is a reason to look, not a conclusion. A factoring company, an escrow agent, or a freight forwarder shows high pass-through and short dwell as ordinary business, and a sole-source subcontractor does have one customer and one supplier; the network's shape alone cannot supply a reason to believe the money is criminal. Three further tests do that work. The first is \emph{commercial substance}\index{commercial substance}: a real intermediary has employees, premises, and counterparties outside this chain, whereas Alpha has a bank account, an incorporation date shortly before the flows began, and a registered address shared with dozens of unrelated entities. The second is \emph{common control}: shared directors or beneficial owners, a shared address, telephone number, or device, or four accounts opened at one branch within days of each other, which turns four legally separate companies into one hand moving its own money and has no innocent reading. The third is the fee. Two percent of principal, identical at every hop, is a cut rather than a price, because a real service is charged for work performed and does not scale with the sum passing through. Only with such corroboration does an alert amount to the suspicion of criminal origin a Suspicious Activity Report requires; the graph by itself reports, as Section~\ref{sec:expressive-models} said of any unsupervised flag, that something is unusual, not that it is a crime.

This is why anti-money-laundering systems increasingly supplement Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering}'s per-account scoring with graph-based analysis of the relationships between accounts, since a layering scheme is constructed so that every individual node looks unremarkable.

As a learning problem, this is node and subgraph classification on the transaction graph. Community detection isolates tightly connected clusters that ordinary commerce does not produce, and graph neural networks, which Chapter~\chnum{altdata} develops\index{graph neural network (GNN)} on this layering network, learn each account's representation from its neighbors' features as well as its own, scoring an account on the company it keeps. The supervision is weak, since confirmed laundering is rare and arrives late, so in practice these models rank leads for investigators rather than deciding anything.

\subsection{Ongoing Due Diligence and Adverse-Media Screening}
\label{sec:aml-due-diligence}\index{customer due diligence (CDD)!ongoing}

AML regulation also imposes an ongoing due-diligence obligation that goes beyond fraud detection's largely reactive posture. Customer Due Diligence (CDD)\index{customer due diligence (CDD)} at onboarding establishes a customer's expected activity profile (Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering}'s KYC inputs); Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD)\index{enhanced due diligence (EDD)} applies heightened scrutiny to higher-risk customers such as politically exposed persons\index{politically exposed person (PEP)} or those in high-risk jurisdictions; and ongoing monitoring continuously compares behavior against that profile, the time-varying risk view Section~\ref{sec:survival-fraud}'s survival framework formalizes.

Two of these obligations are increasingly discharged with the language models of Chapter~\chnum{nlp}. Adverse-media screening, checking whether a customer appears in news reporting\index{adverse-media screening}\index{customer due diligence (CDD)!adverse-media screening} tied to fraud, corruption, or sanctions evasion, is a document-classification problem\index{document classification} of the kind that chapter's Naive Bayes and embedding methods address, and it inherits this chapter's precision problem in acute form, since most name matches in news text are the wrong person. Drafting the suspicious-activity report itself, a structured account of what was observed and why it appears suspicious, is now commonly assisted by a large language model working from the alert's records. Both uses are governed rather than autonomous: a filed SAR is a legal statement by the institution, so the model proposes, an investigator signs, and the model is subject to the validation regime of Section~\ref{sec:governance-3lod}.

\subsection{Sanctions Screening: A Matching Problem, Not a Lookup}\index{sanctions screening}
\label{sec:sanctions-screening}

A bank must not move money for a party on a government sanctions list, and unlike almost every other obligation in this chapter, this one admits no risk appetite: in the United States liability under the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)\index{Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)} regime is strict, so a bank that processes a prohibited payment has violated the law whether or not it knew, intended, or profited.

It is tempting to conclude that sanctions screening is a database lookup rather than a modeling problem. The legal \emph{consequence} of a match is binary, but the \emph{match itself} is not, and conflating the two is the most common way to misunderstand where the difficulty lies. A watchlist records a name in one romanization; a payment message carries whatever spelling, word order, punctuation, and truncation the originating bank's system produced. Nothing guarantees the two strings agree even when they name the same person, and nothing prevents two different people from having names that differ by a single letter.

The standard tool for comparing two strings that should match but do not\index{fuzzy matching}\index{sanctions screening!fuzzy matching} is the edit (Levenshtein) distance\index{Levenshtein distance} $d(a,b)$, the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, or substitutions turning one string into the other, normalized into a similarity score in $[0,1]$,
\begin{equation}
\text{sim}(a,b) = 1 - \frac{d(a,b)}{\max(|a|,|b|)}.
\label{eq:fraud-name-similarity}
\end{equation}
Two short surnames show the mechanics. Turning \texttt{SMITH} into \texttt{SMYTH} requires one substitution, so $d=1$ and $\text{sim} = 1 - 1/5 = 0.800$; turning \texttt{SMITH} into \texttt{SCHMIDT} requires four edits against a longer target, giving $\text{sim} = 1 - 4/7 \approx 0.429$. A screening engine flags any pair scoring above some threshold and sends it to an analyst.

Table~\ref{tab:sanctions_matching} applies this to a watchlist entry, \texttt{Mohammed Al-Rashid}, against seven incoming counterparty names, four of which are the same individual and three of which are not. Scores are computed twice: after simple normalization (uppercase, punctuation stripped), and after also sorting the name's tokens alphabetically, a standard step to defeat surname-first ordering.

Token sorting earns its place: \texttt{Al-Rashid, Mohammed} is the same name written surname-first, and plain edit distance scores it $0.2941$, below every non-match in the table, because reversing two words costs almost as many edits as rewriting the string. Sorting the tokens raises it to $1.0000$. The same step pushes \texttt{Ahmed Al-Rashid}, a different person, down from $0.7647$ to $0.2941$, improving separation in both directions.

The result that matters is the one no preprocessing fixes. \texttt{Mohammed Al-Rashad}, a different individual whose family name differs from the target's by a single vowel, scores $0.9412$, tying \emph{exactly} with two of the four true matches, while the weakest true match, \texttt{Muhammad Al-Rasheed}, scores only $0.7778$. The highest-scoring non-match outranks a true match, and no threshold separates the two classes: set the bar at $0.90$ and the engine clears a sanctioned party while flagging an innocent one; set it at $0.75$ and it catches everything by flagging almost everyone.

This is not a defect a better string distance would repair. A name alone does not identify a person, and two of these strings are too close for any function of the characters to tell apart. Production systems therefore treat the string score as a first-stage filter and resolve the remainder with secondary identifiers (date of birth, nationality, passport or national identity number, address), which is why the Know-Your-Customer records collected at onboarding (Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering}) double as screening inputs.

Here strict liability bites. Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}'s threshold choice was an economic trade-off; here one missed true match is a legal violation however favorable the arithmetic, so a bank cannot buy precision with a little less recall. It sets the threshold low enough to make a miss nearly impossible and absorbs the resulting false positives, which is what makes screening expensive.

Suppose a mid-sized bank screens $1{,}200{,}000$ payment messages a month against consolidated watchlists, that $15$ of them involve a sanctioned party, and that a threshold set for near-certain recall produces a $0.5\%$ false-positive rate against the remaining traffic. Then
\begin{equation*}
\text{Alerts} = 0.99(15) + 0.005(1{,}199{,}985) \approx 6{,}015, \qquad \text{Precision} = \frac{14.85}{6{,}015} \approx 0.00247,
\end{equation*}
so roughly $0.25\%$ of screening alerts are true matches, against the $3.0\%$ alert-to-SAR conversion Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics} computes for transaction monitoring: a precision twelve times worse. At $10$ minutes per screening alert (half an AML alert's time, since most are cleared on sight) and the same \$45 hourly cost used above,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Analyst hours} = \frac{6{,}015 \times 10}{60} \approx 1{,}002, \qquad \text{Monthly cost} \approx 1{,}002 \times \$45 \approx \$45{,}100,
\end{equation*}
or roughly \$541,000 a year. This is a second alert queue, three-quarters the size of the $8{,}000$-alert transaction-monitoring queue analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics}, running at one-twelfth its precision, at every institution that moves money across borders. Supervised learning enters here because labels, scarce almost everywhere else in this chapter, are abundant in screening: every alert is adjudicated by an analyst, so a bank accumulates hundreds of thousands of match and no-match decisions a year. A classifier trained on those dispositions scores a candidate pair on far more than string distance: how common the name is in the relevant population, whether date of birth, nationality, and document number agree, whether the two spellings belong to the same transliteration family\index{sanctions screening!transliteration}, and which kind of list entry was matched. Because it \emph{ranks} rather than thresholds, it can hold recall fixed while pushing most false positives below the line at which the queue is worked.

If a reader takes one operational fact from this chapter, it should be this: in most banks, name screening, not transaction monitoring, is the largest single source of compliance alerts\index{alert economics}, and reducing its false-positive rate without touching recall is among the highest-value applications of machine learning in financial crime compliance.

\subsection{Privacy-Preserving Collaboration: Federated Learning Across Institutions}
\label{sec:federated-aml}

The layering networks above expose a weakness of single-institution detection: a laundering scheme spreads its transactions across many banks so that no single bank sees enough of the pattern. The obvious remedy, pooling transaction data, is largely prohibited: bank-secrecy and data-protection law restricts sharing customer data\index{data privacy}, and no bank wants its records in a competitor's hands. In federated learning\index{federated learning}, institutions jointly train one shared model \emph{without any of them transmitting its underlying data}. Each bank fits the current shared model to its own transactions and sends back only the parameter estimates, and a coordinator combines them, most simply by federated averaging\index{federated learning!federated averaging}\index{federated averaging} (a sample-size-weighted mean), into the next version of the model.

A hand-checkable round shows the mechanics.\index{federated averaging} Suppose three banks jointly train the fraud-scoring logistic regression of Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring} on a shared structuring-intensity feature, holding $2{,}000$, $5{,}000$, and $3{,}000$ labeled transactions respectively. Their local coefficient estimates come back as $0.90$, $1.30$, and $1.10$, the smallest bank's being the noisiest. Federated averaging weights each estimate by its sample share,
\begin{equation*}
\hat\beta_{\text{fed}} \;=\; \frac{2{,}000 \times 0.90 + 5{,}000 \times 1.30 + 3{,}000 \times 1.10}{10{,}000} \;=\; \frac{11{,}600}{10{,}000} \;=\; 1.16,
\end{equation*}
against an unweighted mean of $1.10$; the weighting lets the estimate lean toward the banks with the most evidence. The payoff is clearest from the smallest bank's seat: scoring a transaction with structuring intensity $x=2$ (with intercept $-3$), its own local model flags it with probability $\sigma(-3 + 0.90 \times 2) \approx 0.2315$, while the federated model assigns $\sigma(-3 + 1.16 \times 2) \approx 0.3363$, a sharper detector obtained without a single customer record leaving any bank.

Model updates are not automatically harmless: an estimate computed from a small dataset can leak information about the records behind it, so production systems add differential privacy\index{differential privacy}, calibrated random noise added to each update before transmission so that no observer can reliably infer whether any single transaction was in the data. Aggregation makes this affordable: perturbing the three local estimates by illustrative noise draws of $+0.04$, $-0.02$, and $+0.01$ changes the federated coefficient only from $1.16$ to $1.161$, because independent noise largely cancels in a weighted average while masking each contribution. The privacy-utility trade-off does bind (more noise, more privacy, a worse model), and federated systems raise new operational questions (a malicious participant could poison the shared model through its updates, the adversarial dynamic of Chapter~\chnum{xai}'s robustness discussion moved into the training loop). But the core bargain, most of the pooled-data detection gain with none of the pooled-data legal exposure, is why central banks have begun testing it. The BIS Innovation Hub's Project Aurora\index{Project Aurora}, concluded in May 2023, combined privacy-enhancing technologies, machine learning, and network analysis on a synthetic dataset of domestic and cross-border payments with embedded laundering patterns, and found this collaborative analysis more effective than today's siloed, rules-based monitoring: it detected up to three times more laundering in complex schemes and cut false positives by up to 80\% \parencite{bis2023aurora}. Those are proof-of-concept results on simulated data, not production evidence; as of October 2026 a second phase is still under way.

\section{Economics and Governance}

A detection model's value is decided by the cost of its false positives and by who is accountable for it. This part is deliberately the shortest in the chapter: detection methods earn their length because they can be taught with worked examples, whereas the failures that produce enforcement actions are organizational, and are better shown once, by the case vignette that follows.

\subsection{Alert Economics and the Cost of Low Precision}
\label{sec:alert-economics}\index{alert economics!cost of low precision}

Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes} showed that one bank's fraud model can generate $19{,}600$ flagged transactions in a day; AML monitoring systems, which layer structuring rules, behavioral risk scores, and sanctions screening on top of transaction scoring, typically generate a comparably large alert volume relative to the number that warrant a filed report.

Suppose a bank's combined monitoring system generates $8{,}000$ alerts in a typical month, of which $240$ are ultimately investigated and filed as SARs,\index{Suspicious Activity Report (SAR)}
\begin{equation*}
\text{Alert-to-SAR conversion rate} = \frac{240}{8{,}000} = 3.0\%,
\end{equation*}
well below Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes}'s $24.2\%$ fraud-flag precision, since each additional rule or model adds its own false-positive rate. If each alert takes an average of 20 minutes to review, including the $97\%$ ultimately cleared as false positives,
\begin{equation*}
\text{Analyst hours per month} = \frac{8{,}000 \times 20 \text{ minutes}}{60} \approx 2{,}667 \text{ hours},
\end{equation*}
roughly 16 to 17 full-time analysts working a standard month just to clear this one system's queue. At a fully loaded analyst cost of \$45 per hour (a common rule of thumb including salary, benefits, training, and overhead), this is a monthly labor cost of
\begin{equation*}
2{,}666.67 \times \$45 \approx \$120{,}000,
\end{equation*}
or roughly \$1.44 million annually to staff alert review for one system at one institution, before the cost of data infrastructure, the models, or the SAR-filing process. This dollar figure makes precision a direct cost-reduction lever\index{alert economics!dollar cost} rather than a statistical nicety.

If a model improvement doubled precision from $3.0\%$ to $6.0\%$ while holding recall (and therefore the $240$ SARs filed) fixed, the same $240$ cases would require only $240/0.06 = 4{,}000$ alerts, half of the original $8{,}000$, cutting analyst hours to $4{,}000 \times 20/60 \approx 1{,}333$ and monthly labor cost to $1{,}333 \times \$45 \approx \$60{,}000$, half of the original \$120,000, since cost scales linearly with alert count once recall is held constant.

This is why a bank sets its compliance technology budget and its model-development priorities jointly: a data-science team's precision improvement translates dollar for dollar into the compliance department's staffing budget, as few other model metrics do. It is also why the layered approach of this chapter, transaction scoring (Section~\ref{sec:fraud-scoring}), behavioral risk tiering (Section~\ref{sec:risk-tiering}), and survival-informed prioritization (Section~\ref{sec:survival-fraud}), matters as much for cost as for detection accuracy. A system that better ranks alerts by true risk, even at unchanged recall, lets a fixed staff spend its hours on the alerts most likely to be real: Chapter~\chnum{ml}'s precision-recall trade-off expressed in analyst-hours and dollars.

\subsection{Governance: Model Risk Management and the Three Lines of Defense}
\label{sec:governance-3lod}

Chapters~\chnum{marketrisk} and~\chnum{creditrisk} introduced model risk management\index{model risk management} (independent validation, ongoing performance monitoring\index{model validation!independent}, and clear accountability for a model's use) for market and credit risk models; Chapter~\chnum{hft} revisited it for algorithmic trading, building on Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}'s Knight Capital\index{Knight Capital trading glitch} vignette. Fraud and AML models carry an additional obligation: a poorly governed AML program exposes the institution to direct regulatory and criminal liability, not merely financial loss, since failing to detect and report money laundering is in most jurisdictions a legal violation whether or not the institution profited.

Accountability follows the three lines of defense\index{three lines of defense} framework of Chapter~\chnum{marketrisk}, unchanged: business units own the first line, an independent compliance and risk function the second, internal audit the third. What differs here is what each line is accountable \emph{for}. The second line builds and validates this chapter's scoring and monitoring models rather than VaR models, and the third line's testing is itself subject to regulatory examination, so a weak audit function is not merely an internal control gap but a finding a supervisor can act on.

This structure answers the failure mode of the case vignette below: a monitoring system can exist on paper, and even generate large alert volumes, while failing in practice if the first line lacks the incentive or staff to act, the second line's challenge is weak, or the third line's audits are not independent of the business they review.

\subsubsection*{Model Monitoring: Precision Drift and Adversarial Adaptation}\index{model monitoring!drift}\index{adversarial adaptation!precision drift}

Chapter~\chnum{hft} introduced concept drift, a model's\index{concept drift} fitted relationship growing stale as conditions change, as a reason high-frequency trading models need frequent retraining. Fraud and AML models face a distinct version: because financial crime is adversarial (Section~\ref{sec:crime-taxonomy}), fraudsters who learn, directly or by trial and error, which patterns a bank's model flags will shift away from them, degrading the model's precision even if the population of legitimate customers has not changed. \vignetteheading{\section}{danske}{The Danske Bank Estonia Case}\index{Danske Bank Estonia scandal}\index{Danske Bank Estonia scandal}

Between roughly 2007 and 2015, the Estonian branch of Danske Bank, Denmark's largest bank, processed an estimated 200 billion euros in payments for non-resident customers, many connected to Russia and other former Soviet states, including large volumes of transactions with no plausible legitimate economic purpose. The branch had inherited this portfolio through an earlier acquisition and, rather than winding it down as a high-risk legacy business, kept serving and even expanding it, drawn by the fee income of a small branch's outsized transaction volume. A whistleblower first raised concerns in 2013; an external investigation the bank later commissioned found extensive, longstanding failures in customer due diligence, ongoing monitoring, and internal escalation of red flags, the first- and second-line failures the three-lines-of-defense framework of Section~\ref{sec:governance-3lod} is designed to prevent. Estonian authorities ultimately required the branch's closure in 2019, and in 2022 Danske Bank pleaded guilty in the United States to fraud charges connected to the case, agreeing to forfeit and pay roughly \$2 billion.

The case is instructive because it was not primarily a failure of statistical modeling. Rule-based transaction monitoring of the kind Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring} describes was reportedly in place. But it generated so many alerts relative to the staff available to review them (the problem of Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics}) that a large fraction went uninvestigated or were cleared without adequate scrutiny. It is also a case study in correspondent banking risk: much of the flagged activity moved through other banks acting as intermediaries for often hard-to-identify beneficial owners, which makes a single bank's customer due diligence, however well executed, an incomplete safeguard. The case's lasting legacy has been closer supervisory focus, across the European Union and beyond, on the governance question of Section~\ref{sec:governance-3lod}: not whether a monitoring system technically exists, but whether an institution can demonstrate, to an independent third line and ultimately a regulator, that alerts are investigated and reported at a standard consistent with the risk its customer base presents.

\subsection*{A Current Parallel: TD Bank (2024)}\index{TD Bank AML case}

The same failure recurred, at larger scale, at TD Bank, the U.S. arm of Canada's Toronto-Dominion Bank. On 10 October 2024 TD Bank, N.A. became the largest bank in U.S. history to plead guilty to Bank Secrecy Act program failures\index{Bank Secrecy Act (BSA)}, and the first to plead guilty to conspiracy to commit money laundering. The Justice Department resolution carried about \$1.89 billion in criminal fines and forfeiture; coordinated actions by FinCEN (a record \$1.3 billion civil penalty), the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, and the Federal Reserve brought the total to roughly \$3 billion, with independent monitors and an OCC cap holding the bank's total U.S. assets at their 30 September 2024 level \parencite{doj2024tdbank,fincen2024tdbank,occ2024tdbank}. The failures were transaction-monitoring failures in the literal sense\index{transaction monitoring}: from January 2018 to April 2024 the bank left 92\% of its transaction volume, about \$18.3 trillion, unmonitored; its monitoring scenarios went essentially unchanged from 2014 through late 2022; and three laundering networks moved more than \$670 million through its accounts. Prosecutors traced this to a ``flat cost paradigm'' that held compliance spending flat while the bank's profits and risk grew. That is Section~\ref{sec:alert-economics}'s alert economics resolved in the worst way: rather than buying precision to make the queue affordable, the bank kept it affordable by not generating alerts, and the second and third lines of Section~\ref{sec:governance-3lod} did not stop it.

\section{Challenges in Fraud Detection, Compliance, and Financial Crime}

Several challenges recur across nearly every application in this chapter.

\textbf{Extreme class imbalance makes accuracy a misleading metric.}\index{class imbalance} As Section~\ref{sec:anomaly-bayes} showed, even an accurate-sounding model produces many false positives when the event is rare, which is why this chapter, like Chapter~\chnum{ml}, insists on precision, recall, and alert economics rather than raw accuracy.

\textbf{The adversary adapts, which most of this book's other models do not have to contend with.} Borrowers (Chapter~\chnum{creditrisk}) are not trying to defeat a credit-scoring model; a launderer engaged in structuring (Section~\ref{sec:aml-structuring}) is. A fraud or AML model can therefore decay not just through the concept drift of Chapter~\chnum{hft} but through targeted evasion once a threshold becomes known or guessable.

\textbf{Data across institutions and jurisdictions is fragmented.} As the case vignette's correspondent-banking structure shows, any single institution observes only its own slice of a transaction chain that may span several banks and countries. That is why cross-institutional information sharing, within the limits of privacy and banking secrecy law, materially improves detection, and why the federated learning of Section~\ref{sec:federated-aml} is worth its complexity.

\textbf{False positives carry a real customer and reputational cost, not just an operational one.}\index{false positive!cost of} Every wrongly frozen account or declined transaction is a legitimate customer temporarily denied their own funds, a cost absent from any precision or recall statistic but material to customer relationships and, in aggregate, to regulators judging whether a program's false-positive rate is proportionate.

\textbf{Explainability requirements constrain model choice.} As Chapter~\chnum{xai} develops, a suspicious activity report or account closure frequently must be explained to a regulator or, in some jurisdictions, the customer, which limits how much a program can rely on the least interpretable models of Chapter~\chnum{ml}, despite their potential accuracy advantage.

\textbf{Governance failures, not modeling failures, are the more common root cause of large-scale enforcement actions.} The Danske Bank Estonia case, like the Knight Capital vignette in Chapter~\chnum{algotrading}, shows that the binding constraint is frequently not the detection model's sophistication but whether the organization around it (staffing, escalation, independent challenge, and audit) functions as the three-lines-of-defense framework of Section~\ref{sec:governance-3lod} assumes on paper.

\notocsection{Key Takeaways}

Fraud detection and anti-money-laundering compliance apply the classification tools of Chapters~\chnum{ml} and~\chnum{creditrisk} to a domain defined by extreme class imbalance and an adversary that adapts to known detection rules. Beyond a single logistic regression, production systems draw on the toolkit of Sections~\ref{sec:advanced-imbalance} and~\ref{sec:expressive-models}: class weighting and resampling such as SMOTE address the imbalance, tree-based ensembles capture nonlinear interactions an additive model misses, unsupervised anomaly detection catches patterns no confirmed label yet describes, and precision-recall AUC, not ROC-AUC, is the metric suited to a rare-event problem.

At a low base rate, even an apparently accurate model generates a large false-positive queue, and precision is far more sensitive to the false-positive rate than to the true-positive rate, so alert economics, not just accuracy, must guide how a program is designed and staffed.

A single-transaction model cannot catch a fraud that looks unremarkable in isolation, which motivates account-level risk tiering and survival analysis (the Kaplan-Meier estimator), which uses both confirmed events and censored observations to describe how account risk resolves over time.

Two growing fraud types defeat transaction-level scoring outright. A synthetic identity has no victim, so its losses are booked as credit loss and never become fraud labels, the strongest case for unsupervised detection and cross-account linkage. An authorized push payment scam is authorized by the customer, so detection must move to the customer's own baseline and the beneficiary account; mandatory reimbursement made it an expected-cost calculation, and on an instant-payment rail, when receiving-side evidence arrives late, falling back to the sender-side score is cheap while holding by default is ruinous.

Sanctions screening is a matching problem, not a lookup: a non-match can tie exactly with true matches on name similarity, so secondary identifiers must finish the job, and because liability is strict, recall cannot be traded for precision. Name screening is therefore typically a bank's largest and least precise alert queue.

Anti-money-laundering regulation adds a reporting and due-diligence apparatus (CTRs, SARs, KYC, sanctions screening) built around legal thresholds that launderers evade through placement (structuring), layering (shell-entity chains), and integration. The Danske Bank Estonia case shows that the binding constraint on a compliance program is at least as often organizational as statistical: governance discipline separates a monitoring system that works on paper from one that works in practice.
